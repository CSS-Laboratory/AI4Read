# Political Functions of the Public Sphere

## L0 — One line
The bourgeois public sphere took on political functions first in Britain, then on the Continent, and it was credible as the organizing principle of the constitutional state only while liberal-capitalist conditions made "property owner" and "human being" seem the same, so its idea of dissolving domination into reason was both ideology and more than ideology.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Habermas asks how a debating public of private people became a political organ, and what social conditions made this possible. Britain is the model case: press, parties and Parliament changed together over a century or more. France and Germany took different routes. He then ties the public sphere to a liberalized market and private law. The constitutional state wrote publicity into law, but its idea of law as reason rather than domination was contradictory. The model was credible only while education and property seemed open to everyone. Habermas calls the result ideology and also something more than ideology. When the preconditions changed, the contradiction came to the fore.

**Prerequisites:** the literary public sphere and the bourgeois family (earlier chapters); basic early-modern European history (absolutism, mercantilism, Parliament); the idea of private versus public law.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Britain is the "model case". Its public sphere moved gradually from press criticism to Parliament becoming "the very organ" of public opinion, so it suits the study of how a public takes over political control (pp. 57, 62).
- A split inside capital (restrictive versus expansive interests) made it an obvious step for the weaker party to appeal to a public already engaged in rational-critical debate (pp. 57–58).
- Public opinion in the strict sense differs from the older "sense of the people". It is formed in argument over substantive issues, after the public has been informed, and it needs issues more than prominent persons (pp. 64, 66–67).
- France also had a critical public but, before the Revolution, could not institutionalize it; the Revolution created the institutions overnight (if less stably) and anchored them in constitutional law. In Germany debate took place predominantly in private bourgeois gatherings (pp. 67–73).
- The political public sphere presupposes a liberalized market and private law, which gave commodity owners private autonomy. That phase was brief and tied to Britain's position (pp. 73–79).
- The constitutional state rests on a contradiction: law as will (domination) versus law as reason (public opinion). Public opinion aimed to dissolve domination, not to hold power (pp. 81–83).
- Universal access defines a public sphere, yet access was limited by education and property. The restriction was credible only while the conditions of classical economics seemed to give everyone an equal chance (pp. 85–87).
- The bourgeois public sphere was ideology and more than ideology; once its preconditions changed, its contradiction came to the fore (p. 88).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 8 The Model Case of British Development (pp. 57–67)

**Why Britain first (pp. 57–58).**
- A politically functioning public sphere arose first in Britain around 1700. Forces trying to influence state decisions appealed to the critical public, and the assembly of estates became a modern parliament over the whole century (p. 57).
- Habermas says why Britain was so early is still an unresolved problem. A literary public existed on the Continent too. There it became politically virulent only when capitalism, under mercantilism, reached the stage Britain had reached after the Glorious Revolution (p. 57).
- From the late seventeenth century, new textile, metal and paper enterprises appeared. The old landed versus moneyed opposition was not a sharp class conflict in Britain. It was now overlaid by a conflict between the restrictive interests of commercial and finance capital and the expansive interests of manufacturing and industrial capital (pp. 57–58).
- This repeated an older antagonism between established and younger market players. Confined to merchant-princes, as under the Tudors, it would hardly have led both sides to appeal to the public (p. 58).
- After the revolution the conflict drew in broader strata, in step with capitalism's advance. These strata were already debating rationally and critically, so it was "an obvious step" for the weaker party to carry the conflict into the public sphere. Around 1700 party conflict reached even the disenfranchised (p. 58).

**Three events of 1694–95 (pp. 58–59).**
- The Bank of England marked a new stage of capitalism: it promised to consolidate a system until then held together by commerce (p. 58).
- The end of censorship opened the press to rational-critical argument, and the press could become an instrument for bringing political decisions before the public (p. 58).
- The first cabinet government was a first step in the parliamentarization of state authority. That path led to the point where the political public became "an organ of the state" (pp. 58–59).

**Press and parties (pp. 59–61).**
- In the 1670s the government already issued proclamations against coffee-house talk, treated as a seedbed of unrest (p. 59). The Licensing Act of 1695 ended censorship. The press remained under the Law of Libel and parliamentary and Crown privileges, and the stamp tax of 1712 was a setback. Even so, compared with the press elsewhere in Europe, Britain's press had unique liberties (p. 59).
- Harley was first to exploit the new situation. He engaged writers such as Defoe, who defended the Whigs in journals as well as pamphlets and turned "party spirit" into "public spirit". The journals were discussed in clubs, coffee houses, homes and streets, and Walpole and Bolingbroke addressed the public (p. 59).
- At first the leading press was not in opposition hands (p. 59). Later the Whigs dominated, and they bought the *London Journal* in 1722. Grand-style political journalism was nonetheless created by the Tories under Bolingbroke, who formed an opposition (p. 60).
- A cited scholar (block quotation, note 9; the name is not visible on these pages) says the novelty was an independent journalism that asserted itself against the government and made critical commentary and public opposition a normal part of state affairs. Demagogy, mob scenes and sloganeering were not new (p. 60).
- Bolingbroke's *Craftsman* (from November 1726 until his emigration in 1735) and then the *Gentleman's Magazine* made the press for the first time a genuinely critical organ of a debating public, "the fourth estate" (p. 60).
- Continuing criticism of Crown and Parliament made a public authority answer before the forum of the public. The authority became "public" in a double sense. The development of the public sphere was now measured by the state of the confrontation between government and press (p. 60). The *Letters of Junius* (1768–72) are a highly visible marker; they accused king, ministers and others of machinations and uncovered secret connections (pp. 60–61).

**Parliamentary secrecy (pp. 61–62).**
- Parliament kept a privilege of secrecy. The "votes" were authorized in 1681, but wider publication stayed forbidden. Government-leaning journals reported cautiously, and the opposition got only occasional reports (p. 61).
- Debate reports appeared from the early 1730s. In 1738 Parliament tightened the ban, so that even publication between sessions counted as a breach of privilege (p. 61).
- In 1771 Wilkes nullified the privilege in fact, not in law. The exclusion of the public could no longer be kept up, as shown by Woodfall's verbatim reports from memory (pp. 61–62).
- Journalists were officially given a gallery place only in 1803. Reporters' stands were installed after the 1834 fire, two years after the first Reform Bill had made Parliament "the very organ" of public opinion (p. 62).

> **Intuition note.** The British case is a slow chain of small steps: an end to censorship (though libel law and privileges remained), then secret debates leaking out, then open debates, then reform of Parliament. At each step the press and the public gained a firmer place as critics of Parliament and Crown.

**Why the case is exemplary (pp. 62–64).**
- The transformation lasted nearly 150 years. In Britain alone, at the end of the seventeenth century, a constitution was instituted together with the end of the religious civil war. Habermas says this did not entirely anticipate the continental bourgeois revolutions but made them superfluous at home (p. 62).
- The leading moneyed interests came from a conservative high bourgeoisie closely tied to the nobility, so Parliament had an aristocratic social homogeneity (p. 62).
- The Commons lost its estate character. Protestant middle-class business strata, not represented in Parliament, formed an expanding "pre-parliamentary forum" that followed its debates. Some had the vote and some did not (pp. 62–63).
- Parliament's change was more than the sovereign being bound to the Bill of Rights. The new relation of Parliament to the public sphere, ending in full publicity of debates, brought a "qualitative difference" (p. 63).
- The King had to secure a following in Parliament. Habermas says the Whig–Tory origins may be structurally related to social interests, but the development of factions can be understood only through dynamics internal to Parliament. These were caught between public argument and the King's corrupting influence (p. 63).
- The defeated minority could appeal to the public. The majority, held together by bribery, felt bound to justify its authority by reason (p. 63). From 1727, under the *Craftsman*, a systematic opposition (for a time with a shadow cabinet) informed the public until 1742 (p. 63).
- National opposition had earlier been possible only through violence (Fronde, Civil War). Now it became a permanent controversy between government and opposition. It extended to "topics of government" such as the separation of powers, corruption and the legality of opposition (p. 64). Bolingbroke's theory of opposition arose from this journal debate. He cast the opposition as the party of the "country" and the government as the corrupted "court" (p. 64).

**Public opinion and its limits (pp. 64–67).**
- From early in the century, "the sense of the people" was distinguished from official election results. It was an entity to which the opposition could appeal, and it forced concessions from Walpole more than once (p. 64).
- Habermas warns against reading this as rule by public opinion. The ineffectiveness of mass petitions since 1680 is a more reliable gauge. Dissolutions in 1701 and 1710 followed petitions but were basically mere acclamations of which the King made use. The petitions of 1768–71 did not bring dissolution, and the 1784 dissolution was not chiefly due to public pressure (pp. 64–65).
- Yet new institutions arose: the *Times* (1785), larger public meetings in Wilkes's days, and political associations. Twenty-six county associations were founded in 1779. Parties got a solid organizational form outside Parliament only toward the century's end, with local committees (p. 65).
- In 1792 a speech by Fox in the Commons introduced public opinion in the strict sense into Parliament for the first time. Fox said he ought to give the public the means of forming an opinion. He spoke against Pitt, who in 1791 had dropped war preparations against Russia under pressure of public opinion (pp. 65–66).
- By 1800 the public was a permanent critical commentator and the officially designated discussion partner of the delegate. The term "public opinion" replaced "the sense of the people". It was formed in discussion after the public had been put in a position, through education and information, to reach a considered opinion (p. 66).
- The Reform Bill (two years after the July Revolution) gave almost one million of about 24 million residents the vote. It went to the upper middle class, from which most of the debating public was recruited (p. 66). Peel's Tamworth Manifesto of 1834 was the first published election platform and completed the conditions for a "temporary era" of government by public opinion (p. 66).
- Habermas says public opinion arises from conflict of arguments over a substantive issue, not from uncritical assent to persons. It needs a defined issue more than prominent persons. The Whigs' election proclamation urged voters to remember they were fighting for things, not for men (pp. 66–67).

> **Intuition note.** For Habermas "public opinion" means more than a count of views. It is a result of informed argument about issues. Habermas dates the "temporary era" of government by public opinion to the 1830s. Earlier, the ineffective mass petitions and the King's calculations show how limited the public's real power still was.

### 9 The Continental Variants (pp. 67–73)

**France (pp. 67–71).**
- A politically debating public appeared about the mid-eighteenth century. It could not institutionalize its critique. Censorship allowed no political journalism, and the periodical press was scanty. The *Mercure de France* had only 1600 subscribers in 1763. Illegal Dutch journals were read in secret (p. 67).
- There was no estates assembly that could have become a people's representation. The Estates General had not met since 1614. The parliaments were high courts of "bourgeoisified intermediate powers" (p. 67).
- The social basis was also missing. A wealthy upper bourgeoisie existed under the Regency but could not affect national fate. It was not united with nobility and *noblesse de robe* into a homogeneous top stratum as in England (pp. 67–68).
- Class differences ran deep. Rich merchants bought noble titles and so left production and distribution. The nobility withdrew from trade and depended on the crown, paying for its political insignificance with tax privileges. The king largely monopolized public authority, and civic equality existed only in a negative form, since all but the king (and one official) were subjects. The nobility's salons were in many ways more receptive to enlightened thought than the bourgeoisie itself. Political, economic and in-between "society" sectors were clearly separable (p. 68).
- The philosophes' criticism concerned religion, literature and art in the first half of the century. The *Encyclopedia* gave it at least indirect political meaning (pp. 68–69). Later the philosophes turned into economists (physiocrats), and in 1774 Turgot and Malesherbes entered government as, so to speak, the first exponents of public opinion (p. 69).
- Necker opened a breach in absolutism by publishing the state budget. The King dismissed him three months later. Still, public debate had proved itself as a check on the government at the nerve center of bourgeois interests, the state debt. Habermas adds that from then on this public sphere could only be suppressed, not effectively put out of commission (p. 69). The *Cahiers de Doléances* led to convening the Estates General, which took up the estates tradition at a social level where it had to become a modern parliament (p. 69).
- The Revolution created in France overnight, if less stably, what took Britain over a century: club-based parties, a political daily press and publicity of debates (pp. 69–70). The public sphere was also anchored in statutes. Habermas suggests this is why the continental bourgeoisie became so precisely aware of its political functions, and the German word *Öffentlichkeit* was formed after the French (p. 70).
- The constitution of 1791 (paragraph 11; the digit is garbled in the text layer) protected free communication of ideas, with responsibility for abuse. The 1793 constitution added freedom of assembly (pp. 70–71). Reality diverged: an August 1792 edict called opponents "poisoners of public opinion". In 1800 Napoleon abolished press freedom, leaving only thirteen named papers, and from 1811 only three besides the *Moniteur* (p. 71).
- The Bourbons promised press freedom in the 1814 Charte (art. 8), but the opposition could speak only cautiously. The July Revolution restored the latitude the revolutionary rights of man had promised (p. 71).

**Germany (pp. 71–73).**
- Parliamentary life began only after the July Revolution, briefly, in a few southern and southwestern territories. The representative bodies recommended in 1815 (Vienna Final Act) had been linked to territorial-estate traditions but were almost everywhere thwarted by the Karlsbad Resolutions (p. 71).
- Estate barriers, especially between nobility and bourgeoisie, lasted longer under continental absolutism. The bourgeois distanced themselves from "the people" (rural population, lower class, shopkeepers, artisans, workers). Their criterion was education (*Bildung*) (pp. 71–72).
- The German nobility depended completely on the courts. Unlike the French nobility it could not develop "society" into a culturally dominant, critically involved public (p. 72).
- Political debate happened mainly in private bourgeois gatherings. Journals were crystallization points of "social" life. Reading societies spread from the 1770s, with more than 270 by century's end. They read periodicals and also discussed them. They elected committees by bylaw, voted on members, excluded women and forbade gambling (pp. 72–73).
- Political journals had the most readers; Schlözer's *Staatsanzeigen* reached 4000 (p. 73). The princes' brutal treatment of Wekherlin and Schubart (one died in prison, the other was broken after ten years in a fortress) is called symptomatic of a certain critical strength of the public sphere (p. 73).

> **Intuition note.** The three countries suggest that a critical public alone was not enough. In Habermas's account, Britain had institutions (press, parliament) and a socially homogeneous upper stratum; France lacked institutions and such a stratum before the Revolution; in Germany the nobility's dependence on the courts kept "society" from becoming a culturally dominant critical public.

### 10 Civil Society as the Sphere of Private Autonomy (pp. 73–79)

- The institutional account stays "abstract". The kind of political function can be grasped only against the phase of civil society in which commodity exchange and social labor were largely freed from state directives (pp. 73–74). In this order the public sphere became the organizational principle of bourgeois constitutional states (Britain after 1832; with reservations, constitutional monarchies on the Belgian model of 1830) (p. 74).
- It had normative status as an organ for civil society's self-articulation. Its social precondition was a market tending to liberalization, which left social reproduction as far as possible to private people and completed the privatization of civil society (p. 74).
- Under absolutism "private" meant first only being stripped of quasi-public character, and the private domain was still under mercantilist regulation. Mercantilism nonetheless began a positive privatization: reproduction could follow market laws. With the market's expansion, commodity owners gained private autonomy (p. 74).

**Private law (pp. 75–79).**
- The contract based on free declaration of will was modelled on the exchange transaction of free commodity owners. Exchange relations were the model for all contract relations (p. 75).
- The statuses of *libertatis*, *civitatis* and *familiae* gave way to one *status naturalis* for all legal subjects. This corresponds to the parity of commodity owners in the market and of educated individuals in the public sphere (p. 75).
- The great civil codes secured private property and the freedoms of contract, trade and inheritance, at least in tendency free from estate and state (p. 75). Britain's Common Law developed these forms earlier. Dates: Prussia's *Landrecht* 1794, the Code Civil 1804, the Austrian code 1811 (p. 75).
- Many times the codes went through critical scrutiny by private people as a public, through prize competitions and questionnaires. The draft was submitted to the public in Paris in 1800, as in Berlin and Vienna (p. 76).
- Under absolutism, Roman Law was more a legal technique and tool of princes against estates. "Private law" stayed a creature of state authority. A cited passage (note 45; author not visible here) says the digests assumed contractual freedom, while local rules imposed price controls, restrictions and obligations on labor, residence and property (pp. 76–77).
- By the later eighteenth century modern private law had in principle removed these controls. Complete breakthrough of the move "from status to contract" took another hundred years (p. 77). Examples: Britain ended state wage regulation in 1757 (textiles), had free wage labor in all branches by 1813, and abolished the seven-year apprenticeship law in 1814, with a strict ban on unionization. France eliminated most regulations by 1791. Austria did so under Joseph II and Prussia under the Stein–Hardenberg Reforms (p. 77). Inheritance reform in Britain is dated 1843 in the text (p. 78).
- Industrial capital cleared domestic obstacles before foreign trade was freed (p. 78). British foreign trade could be liberalized with consistency only after the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846. The old conflict between defensive and expansive interests recurred "at a higher level". It led, over the longer run, to a turnover in market dominance. Britain, dominating sea and market, had everything to gain from *laisser faire* (p. 78).
- Habermas stresses that the liberal phase lasted only "one blissful moment" in capitalism's history and arose from a unique British constellation. Other countries did not fully apply *laisser faire* even at its height. Only then was civil society freed from public authority far enough for the political public sphere to develop fully (p. 79).

> **Intuition note.** The public sphere and the free market belong together historically. People who manage their own property as private persons are the ones who claim to argue about common rules. Habermas says the political public sphere reached full development only in this liberal phase (p. 79).

### 11 The Contradictory Institutionalization of the Public Sphere in the Bourgeois Constitutional State (pp. 79–88)

**Law and market (pp. 79–81).**
- Civil society saw itself as self-regulating and free from domination and coercion. Each owner's economic power was too small to affect prices. State intervention without legal basis was objectionable chiefly because it was unpredictable, and profit calculation needs calculable expectations (Weber's "guarantees of calculability") (pp. 79–80).
- State laws, like market laws, were to be general and objective, with no privileges. Market laws prevail because they are intrinsic, which led classical economics to give them the appearance of an *ordre naturel*; state laws must be explicitly enacted (p. 80).
- Being a constitutional state did not necessarily require a parliamentary form of government with a constitutionalized public sphere. The physiocrats' "legal despotism" imagined public opinion sovereign under an enlightened monarch (p. 80). But landed interests dominated Parliament until 1832 and delayed repeal of the Corn Laws, so the enlightened monarch "remained a pure fiction". Only through its own power to legislate could the public be sure of laws serving bourgeois commerce, so the state made the public sphere an organ of the state (pp. 80–81).

**The contradiction in the concept of law (pp. 81–83).**
- A cited passage (note 57; author not visible here) says participation of the people's representatives became the decisive mark of law. Then whatever arises with their participation counts as law (p. 81). On this side law is an expression of will and includes a claim to domination. On the other side law expresses reason and keeps traces of its origin in public opinion (p. 81).
- Schmitt put the second first: "Law is not the will of one or of many people, but something rational-universal; not voluntas, but ratio." (p. 81).
- The rule of law aimed to dissolve domination altogether and bind all state activity to norms legitimated by public opinion. Acts of sovereignty were suspect in themselves (pp. 81–82).
- Since public debate claimed to be a noncoercive inquiry into what is correct and right, legislation that drew on it could not explicitly be considered domination. Yet legislative authority was won in struggle, so it kept the character of "coercive power". Locke said "legislative power" and Montesquieu "pouvoir". The legislative–executive distinction was still modelled on norm versus action, reason versus will (p. 82).
- Rousseau's change from princely to popular sovereignty did not solve the dilemma. Public opinion could not strictly be called sovereign, since it is subject to the laws of a public of debating private persons. It wanted to be neither a check on power, nor power itself, nor the source of all powers. Domination itself was meant to change in its medium. The inversion of Hobbes is *veritas non auctoritas facit legem*, and it was lost when public opinion was conceived through sovereignty and the *pouvoirs* (p. 82).
- Public debate was to turn *voluntas* into *ratio*, a consensus on what is practically necessary in everyone's interest (p. 83).

> **Intuition note.** The state's laws came from political struggle (will), but their claim to legitimacy rested on reasoned public agreement. Habermas locates the tension here. The model aims at power dissolved into reason, but laws still have to be made and enforced.

**Basic rights and publicity (pp. 83–84).**
- In continental constitutions three sets of basic rights are named. The first protects the debating public and the political role of private people: opinion, press, assembly and association, petition, equal vote. The second protects the individual as free human being, grounded in the intimate sphere of the conjugal family. The third protects property owners' dealings in civil society, including equality before the law and protection of property (p. 83).
- Publicity became the organizing principle of state organs themselves: public parliamentary debates and public trials. Even the independent judiciary seemed secured only through a critical public (pp. 83–84).
- The bureaucracy resisted publicity most, as a means of princely power against bourgeois society. A Prussian royal order of 1804 nevertheless endorsed "decent publicity" against subordinate officials' negligence (p. 84).

**Model versus reality (pp. 84–85).**
- The article that all power comes from the people most clearly betrays the public sphere's character as an order of domination. The constitutional norms implied a model of civil society that by no means corresponded to its reality. The categories were historical and denoted tendencies only (p. 84).
- The autonomous "private people" were a small minority, even counting the petty bourgeoisie. The "common people" were far more numerous, and princes and landed nobility still ruled by precapitalist political laws (p. 84).
- Constitutions nevertheless addressed citizens and human beings as such, necessarily, as long as publicity was their organizing principle (p. 85).

**Universal access and its conditions (pp. 85–87).**
- Habermas says the public sphere of civil society depended on the principle of universal access. A sphere that excludes particular groups from the start was, for him, not a public sphere at all. The bourgeois public saw itself as anticipating that all human beings belong to it (p. 85).
- In practice, education and property were the two criteria for admission. They marked largely the same circle, since education followed social status, which was set mainly by property. The census could equal the tax list, and the French Revolution used it to separate full citizens from others (p. 85).
- The franchise restriction need not count as a restriction of the public sphere as long as it ratified a status won economically in the private sphere. Universal accessibility must be decided by the structure of civil society, not only by the constitution. The public sphere was safeguarded if economic and social conditions gave everyone an equal chance to meet the criteria (pp. 85–86).
- Classical political economy supplied three presuppositions (p. 86):
  1. *Economic:* free competition. Everyone calculates alone and without collusion. Production was subjectively anarchic yet objectively harmonious.
  2. *Sociological:* commodities exchange according to labor value. This condition holds only if each supplier produces his commodities himself and each worker owns his means of production, so the presupposition amounts to a society of petty commodity producers. The first presupposition (independent formation of prices) in turn implies relatively widely and evenly distributed ownership.
  3. *Theoretical* (the elder Mill, later Say's Law): under complete mobility of producers, products and capital, supply and demand would always be in equilibrium. No capacity or labor lies idle, and the system is in principle crisis-free.
- Habermas says that under these conditions, and only under them, would everyone have an equal chance, with ability and "luck", to become a property owner and so qualify for the public sphere (p. 87). They were not fulfilled even in the first half of the nineteenth century (p. 87).
- The liberal model still approximated reality closely enough for the bourgeois class interest to pass as the general interest. In that phase the public sphere had credibility: if everyone, as it might appear, had the chance to become a citizen, then only citizens (property owners) should enter it, without giving up publicity. Only property owners were thought to have private interests that converge automatically in the common interest of preserving a private-property society. For them there was no break between *homme* and *citoyen*, as long as the *homme* was also an owner of private property (p. 87).
- Class interest was the basis of public opinion, but in that phase it must also have been objectively congruent with the general interest, at least to the extent that the opinion counted as public and rational. Closing off as a ruling class would have turned critical debate into dogma and rational insight into authoritarian command (p. 87).

**Ideology and more than ideology (p. 88).**
- While these presuppositions could be assumed and publicity worked as a principle, what the public believed it was doing was ideology and also more than ideology. The dominant class built institutions that credibly embodied the idea of their own abolition: *veritas non auctoritas facit legem* (p. 88).
- If ideologies have a truth-claim insofar as they transcend the status quo in a utopian way, then ideology exists only from this period on. Its origin would be a set of identifications: property owner with human being as such, the political public sphere with the literary one, class interest with general interest, and domination with its dissolution into pure reason (p. 88).
- The preconditions soon changed deeply, and with this the contradiction came to the fore. Its principle, according to its own idea, opposed all domination, yet it founded a political order whose social basis did not make domination superfluous (p. 88).

> **Intuition note.** The bourgeois public sphere offered a promise (rule by argument, open to all) that its own social basis could not keep. Habermas keeps both sides. The promise misdescribes reality, yet it also carries a truth-claim because it points beyond the status quo.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates §11's point: restricting admission by property and education is "mere ratification" only if the conditions of an equal chance hold (pp. 85–87).

A village has 10 households. Assume education follows property (as the text says of that era, p. 85). Admission to the public sphere requires holdings of at least 8 units.
- **Case A (evenly spread means of production, the sociological presupposition, p. 86):** each household holds 10 units. Total = 10 × 10 = 100. All 10 households have ≥ 8, so 10/10 = 100% are admitted. The restriction ratifies a status everyone attained.
- **Case B (concentrated):** holdings are 40, 20, 12, 8, 6, 4, 3, 3, 2, 2. Check the total: 40 + 20 = 60; + 12 = 72; + 8 = 80; + 6 = 86; + 4 = 90; + 3 = 93; + 3 = 96; + 2 = 98; + 2 = 100. Households with ≥ 8: 40, 20, 12, 8, so 4/10 = 40% are admitted. The six others (6, 4, 3, 3, 2, 2) are shut out by the market distribution itself.

In B, the admitted four decide on the shared rules. Their "general interest" no longer clearly covers the 6 who are excluded. In A it is plausible that each private interest converges on the common one, which is the credibility Habermas describes (p. 87).

## Concepts
- [[public-sphere]] — a sphere of private people arguing about public affairs; here it acquires political functions and becomes an organ of the constitutional state (pp. 57, 74)
- [[bourgeois-public-opinion]] (new) — opinion formed in rational-critical debate on substantive issues after the public is informed; distinguished from "the sense of the people" and plebiscitary assent (pp. 64, 66–67)
- [[fourth-estate]] (new) — the press as an institutionalized critical organ of a debating public, first with the *Craftsman* (p. 60)
- [[publicity-principle]] (new) — publicity as organizing principle of state organs (open parliament, open trials), linking delegates and voters (p. 83)
- [[private-autonomy]] (new) — in the positive sense, commodity owners' free power of control over property in a liberalized market (p. 74)
- [[law-as-voluntas-and-ratio]] (new) — the constitutional state's contradictory concept of law as will (domination) and as reason (public opinion) (pp. 81–82)
- [[universal-access]] (new) — a public sphere that excludes groups from the outset is not a public sphere at all; in practice limited to the propertied and educated (p. 85)
- [[ideology-and-more-than-ideology]] (new) — the bourgeois public's self-understanding as false consciousness but also a utopian truth-claim that transcends the status quo (p. 88)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Public opinion formed through informed argument (Fox's maxim) ↔ knowledge gap hypothesis: both tie equal participation to unequal access to information and education.
- [structural-analogy] The public's "sense of the people" versus election results ↔ spiral of silence / quasi-statistical sense: both concern how opinion distribution is perceived and used outside voting.
- [metaphor] Law as *voluntas* versus *ratio* ↔ Habermas's later consensus theory of legitimacy (canon link; not stated in this unit): reason as the source of political authority.

## Questions this raises
- Why does Habermas say the political public sphere only "temporarily" governed through public opinion (1834)? What replaced these conditions (p. 88: "before long they all changed profoundly")?
- If "the public" excluded women and the poor in practice, how much is left of its claim to universal access? How does Habermas weigh ideology against the norm (pp. 73, 85–88)?
- Does the finding that mass petitions were ineffective (pp. 64–65) conflict with the claim that public opinion shaped Parliament? Which evidence counts as "influence"?
- Could a non-liberal capitalist order, such as one with strong state regulation, still support a public sphere (pp. 79, 87)?
