# The Bourgeois Public Sphere: Idea and Ideology, part 2 (Hegel, Marx, Mill, Tocqueville)

## L0 — One line
Habermas argues that Hegel and Marx unmasked the bourgeois public sphere as ideology, while Mill and Tocqueville, once the public widened, came to treat public opinion as a power to be limited, so neither the liberal nor the socialist model fitted what the public sphere became.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The unit asks what happened to the idea that a public of private people could turn domination into reason once its "natural" social basis was doubted. Habermas follows four thinkers. Hegel demotes public opinion. Marx calls it false consciousness, yet expects the public sphere, widened by non-owners, to dissolve political domination into administration. Mill and Tocqueville fear public opinion as a coercive majority and propose elite, representative publicity and intermediate powers. Tocqueville also fears the bureaucratic state. Both models still presuppose a "natural order" of society. Habermas ends by saying that, as capitalism became "organized," the public sphere grew wider but lost its political function of critical control.

**Prerequisites:** the eighteenth-century idea of public opinion as reasoned consensus (Kant, physiocrats); civil society (the private sphere of trade and labour) versus the state; basic Marxian terms (class, surplus value).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Kant's public agreement and Hegel's "public opinion" look alike, but Hegel's analysis of civil society undermines the liberal claim that opinion is plain reason (pp. 117–119).
- Hegel demotes public opinion to a "means of education" that integrates citizens into the state from above. The state's existence, not public debate, guarantees reason (pp. 120–122).
- Marx exposes three liberal identifications as fictions: property owners with human beings, the public with the nation, and public opinion with reason. The bourgeois constitutional state is therefore "mere ideology" (pp. 124–125).
- Marx also sees a dialectic. Non-owners entering press, parties and parliament turn the weapons of publicity against the bourgeoisie, which leads to a socialist counter-model (pp. 126–129).
- In the counter-model, private autonomy derives from the public sphere, not from property, so private persons are private persons of a public (pp. 128–129).
- Liberals (Mill, Tocqueville) react to the ideology critique (which Habermas calls obviously correct): they drop the philosophy of history, and treat public opinion as a coercive majority power. They want an elite, representative public (pp. 130–137).
- Tocqueville adds the danger of bureaucratic centralization. Marx later shares this worry, but socialism still assumes a "natural order" (pp. 138–140).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of §13 (p. 117)
- The self-understanding of bourgeois consciousness as "public opinion" rested on a series of fictions that reached into the Kantian system. From it one could derive the idea of the public sphere together with the presupposition of a natural basis of the juridical condition (p. 117).
- Once that connection became doubtful, the public sphere turned against the system's own foundations. Hegel doubted that civil society could work as a natural order, because its privatized sphere of commodity exchange and social labour threatened to break apart through its own conflicts (p. 117).
- Even the public sphere then no longer sufficed to link politics and morality. In Hegel's concept of opinion, the public of civil society was already denounced as ideology (p. 117).

### §14 On the Dialectic of the Public Sphere (Hegel and Marx)

**Hegel on public opinion (pp. 117–122)**
- Hegel's "public opinion" is "an empirical universal" of which the Many's opinions are particulars (p. 117). It manifests the formal subjective freedom to hold and express private judgments on affairs of state (p. 117).
- At first sight he repeats the eighteenth-century function: domination is subjected to reason. Authority comes from "insight and argument," and the modern principle requires that whatever one recognizes must show itself entitled to recognition (pp. 117–118). He also expects much from the great assembly compared with fireside "castle-building" (p. 118).
- **Hegel's qualification:** public opinion is formally universal, with its substance outside itself, so it is knowledge "merely as appearance." Science is therefore outside public opinion, since it needs unambiguous, determinate expression (p. 118).
- **Habermas:** this demotion followed necessarily from Hegel's concept of civil society (p. 118). Hegel praised the political economists' laws, but he saw the "system of needs" as anarchic and antagonistic. That destroyed the liberal claim that opinion is plain reason (p. 118).
- Hegel's argument: civil society raises natural inequality to inequality of skill, resources and attainment. Wealth accumulation and the subdivision of jobs produce dependence and distress, so that, despite an excess of wealth, civil society is not rich enough: its own resources cannot check excessive poverty and the creation of a "penurious rabble" (pp. 118–119).
- Habermas: the proletariat appears only negatively, as a category of poor relief. Still, the underconsumption theory (with anticipated imperialism, *Philosophy of Right* §246) diagnosed a conflict of interests. This exposed the supposedly universal interest of property-owning private people as particularist. Public opinion lost its basis of unity and truth and became a merely subjective opining of the many (p. 119).
- The state risks being "confused" with civil society. If private people as such take part in state affairs, the disorganization of civil society infiltrates the state, producing, in Hegel's words, an unorganized opinion and volition and a powerful bloc opposed to the organized state. Habermas: Hegel therefore wanted police and corporate controls (pp. 119–120). With a corporatively reintegrated society Hegel has definitely left liberalism behind, and so, Habermas adds, has his public sphere (p. 120).
- Public opinion has the form of common sense and carries prejudices, yet reflects genuine needs. It becomes self-aware in the assembly of estates. That publicity does not link parliament to a critical public. It integrates citizens into the state from above and serves as a "means of education" (p. 120).
- **Hegel:** public opinion deserves respect for its basis and contempt for its concrete expression. It has no criterion of discrimination, so independence from it is the first formal condition of achieving anything great or rational, in life or in science (p. 121).
- **Habermas:** reason realized in the existing state thus keeps the impenetrability of personal domination that Kant wanted dissolved by publicity (p. 121). Subjective freedom reaches its right in the monarch. The people is not deceived about its substantive basis but is self-deceived about how it knows it (p. 121).
- Domination is limited only by the nation's mind, one with the quasi-natural order of substantive morality. The realm of enlightenment, where the nation's mind knows itself as public opinion, had no power to obligate (pp. 121–122).
- Hegel rejects the congruence of politics and morality as a false question: the state's welfare has claims different from an individual's (p. 122).
- **Habermas's assessment:** Hegel took the teeth out of the public sphere idea. Civil society could not dispense with domination and needed political integration (p. 122). His estates-based state reacted to contradictions he had noticed in British and French constitutional states, but he did not accept those as advanced civil society (p. 122).

*Intuition note.* For Kant, open debate could turn power into reason. Hegel thinks market society is too conflict-ridden for that, so he lets the state, not the public, carry reason.

**Marx: public opinion as false consciousness (pp. 122–125)**
- The young Marx saw that the "political" estates had dissolved into merely "social" classes. Reviving an estates constitution was an attempt to turn the clock back (pp. 122–123). A "republic" had to appear wherever private spheres became independent (p. 123).
- Marx (quoted): the political revolution abolished the political character of civil society and dissolved it into individuals and their material and cultural elements. It set the political spirit free as a community sphere, "in principle independent" of those elements (p. 123).
- **Habermas:** Marx treats this independence ironically. Yet he takes the idea seriously as the self-image of Britain and France, using the constitutional state's own standard to unmask it. He holds the public sphere up as in a mirror to the social conditions of its "utterly unbourgeois realization" (pp. 123–124).
- Marx's critique of political economy (as reported by Habermas): left to itself, capitalism cannot reproduce itself as a "natural order" without crises. Valorization rests on appropriating surplus value from owners of only their labour power. Upward mobility shrinks, markets become oligopolies, and new power relations arise within contractual freedom (p. 124).
- Fictions demolished (p. 124–125):
  1. Equality of opportunity to become a property owner, and thereby a private person with access to the public sphere, is lacking. The public cannot claim to be the nation (p. 124).
  2. "Property owner = human being" fails. Owners' interest in keeping labour and exchange private is a particular interest that prevails by power over wage earners (pp. 124–125).
  3. The "rights of man" protect an abstract egoism. As bourgeois, the private person must give up his civil reality to act as citizen. Public opinion as agreement reached by discourse cannot be equated with what is right and just (p. 125).
- **Conclusion (Habermas, on Marx's view):** replacing feudal domination by a public of rational-critical debate perpetuated political domination "in different guise." The bourgeois constitutional state with its public sphere was "mere ideology" (p. 125).

*Intuition note.* Marx tests the public sphere by its own promises: open to all, owners = humans, debate = reason. On Marx's critique, each promise fails because power relations (above all between owners and wage earners) arise inside the private sphere itself.

**Marx's dialectic and the socialist counter-model (pp. 125–129)**
- Electoral reform expressed the struggle over the constitutional state (p. 125). Marx (1840s) read the push of civil society into the legislature as civil society seeking a political existence for itself (p. 126).
- After the June uprising Marx asked, rhetorically, how a parliamentary regime that lives by discussion could forbid discussion, and how the great majorities outside parliament could fail to want to decide too. When the fiddle is played at the top of the state, he added, those down below dance (p. 126).
- **Habermas:** as non-bourgeois strata enter press, parties and parliament, the bourgeoisie's weapons of publicity are turned against it. Marx's idea is that society itself takes on a political form. Electoral reform signals dissolution of the abstract political state and thus of civil society (pp. 126–127).
- **Habermas's summary:** the public sphere arose with a society separate from the state. It gained political function by making the sanctioning of society as private sphere a public topic (p. 127). Around mid-century one could foresee control passing to groups without property, who had no interest in keeping society private. Once the mass of non-owners debate the general rules of social transactions, the reproduction of social life itself becomes a universal concern. The "political society" puzzle was resolved in the phrase "socialization of the means of production" (p. 127).
- Under such conditions the public sphere was presumed able to realize in earnest what it had promised: subjecting political domination to reason (pp. 127–128). *Manifesto* (quoted): when class distinctions have disappeared and production is concentrated in an association of the whole nation, the public power loses its political character. Habermas: Engels read this as the administration of things replacing rule over men. Political authority would disappear and public functions would become administrative, but this was possible only for the "associated producers" rationally regulating their exchange with nature (p. 128).
- **Counter-model:** criticism and control by the public extend to the part of the private sphere that owners held through control of the means of production. Autonomy no longer rests on property but on the public sphere itself, so private autonomy is derived from it. Private persons become "the private persons of a public rather than a public of private persons." Bourgeois = homme is replaced by citoyen = homme (pp. 128–129).
- The autonomous public, through a state absorbed into society, secures a sphere of personal freedom, leisure and movement, in which informal interaction would for the first time be emancipated from the constraints of social labour (p. 129). Engels: abolishing private property removes the family's old basis, making relations between the sexes purely private, a matter concerning only the two persons involved. Marx: marriage, if not the basis of the family, would not be subject to law, just as friendship is not. Both treat a relation as truly "private" only when free of legal regulation (p. 129).

*Intuition note.* Liberal order: property makes you a private person, which earns you a place in the public. Marx's model reverses this: shared public control of production grants each person private freedom.

### §15 The Ambivalent View of the Public Sphere in the Theory of Liberalism (Mill and Tocqueville)

**The dialectic did not go as expected (pp. 129–131)**
- Equal political rights for all classes proved possible inside class society, and the widened public sphere did not in principle eliminate the basis on which the public of property-owning private people had at first tried to achieve something akin to a rule of public opinion (p. 130). Habermas: the ideology critique was so obviously correct that liberal social philosophers around mid-century were forced almost to deny the principle even as they celebrated it. This ambivalent conception did not admit to itself the structural conflict of its society (p. 130).
- Liberal apologetics beat the socialist critique in one respect. They questioned the presuppositions shared by the classic model and its counter-model (p. 130).
- The shared presupposition: a natural order guaranteeing harmonious social reproduction. Public opinion would then only need to establish the truth in principle, conflicts and bureaucratic decisions would stay minimal, and conflicts that could not be avoided would be subject to reliable criteria of public evaluation (pp. 130–131).
- Socialists demonstrated (Habermas's word) that the actual basis lacked these preconditions and had to be set up differently. Liberals doubted the presupposition and defended a relativized public sphere. They gave up the philosophy of history for "common sense meliorism" and became "realistic" (p. 131).

**The widened public (pp. 131–133)**
- Chartism and the February revolt changed the public's appearance. Before, the public could be seen as free individuals in "society," with rules of equality and frankness, resting on trust that, given shared class interest, friend-or-foe relations were impossible (p. 131).
- With press and propaganda the public expanded, lost social exclusiveness and its coherence from sociability and education. Private conflicts emerged in public, and group needs sought state regulation. Laws passed under pressure from the street could hardly be seen any longer as the reasonable consensus of debating private persons. They corresponded more or less overtly to compromises between competing private interests (pp. 131–132).
- Mill approved the movements against the aristocracy of money, gender and colour (p. 132). Tocqueville, days before February, urged extending the franchise (p. 132). He said public life appeared where it should not, and had stopped where the laws placed it, because rights were restricted to one class (p. 132).
- **Habermas:** electoral reform, not publicity as such, became the nineteenth-century topic. Public opinion lost its clear polemical goal and its unity, which a common foe no longer guaranteed. Liberals favoured the enlargement on principle but devalued its consequences. Unreconciled interests made the dominant opinion a coercive force (pp. 132–133).

**Mill and Tocqueville on the tyranny of opinion (pp. 133–136)**
- Mill deplored the "yoke of public opinion." His plea *On Liberty* was aimed at the power of the public. Mill wrote that public opinion now rules the world, and that the masses' thinking is done for them by men much like themselves in the newspapers (p. 133).
- Tocqueville: in democracies opinion presses on each mind, and the American majority supplies ready-made opinions. Against injustice there is no appeal, since legislature, executive, police and jury all express the majority (pp. 133–134).
- Hence free speech now protects nonconformists against the public, not the public's debate against the police. Mill: eccentricity is itself a service against this tyranny (p. 134).
- **Habermas on Mill:** Mill (quoted) held that, in the existing state of human intellect, only through diversity of opinion is there a chance of fair play to all sides of the truth. Habermas adds that the public was thereby prevented from attaining a rational opinion, and that Mill demanded tolerance, not criticism. This resignation was disguised as a perspectivist epistemology. Interests are no longer measured against a general interest, so opinions keep an irreducible kernel of faith (pp. 134–135).
- Bentham took the majority as criterion of the general interest. Mill (quoted) answers that the expanded public's majority is manual labourers in the same position. Identity of position brings identity of partialities, passions and prejudices (p. 135). Opinion becomes one power among others. Mill (quoted): once a power has been made the strongest, enough has been done for it; care is then needed to stop it from swallowing up all others (pp. 135–136).
- **Habermas:** the public sphere now serves the division of power, not its dissolution. Habermas calls the liberal reading of the constitutional state reactionary: it reacted to the power of the idea of a critically debating public's self-determination as soon as that public was subverted by the propertyless and uneducated masses. It was liberalism that first read the constitutional state as dualist (democratic plus liberal) (p. 136).

*Intuition note.* Once the majority is a class with a shared position, "the majority" no longer signals the general interest. Mill therefore treats opinion as a power to be balanced, not as a final judge.

**Elite publics and intermediate powers (pp. 136–139)**
- Mill rejects the multitude deciding political questions. He wants them decided by a small number of specially educated persons. Tocqueville agrees, wanting opinion purified by materially independent citizens, since the press does not suffice (p. 136).
- Tocqueville recalls the pouvoirs intermédiaires. Since a new aristocracy cannot be made overnight, he says associations of plain citizens can be powerful "aristocratic bodies." Habermas: such educated and powerful citizens were supposed to form an elite public whose critical debate determined public opinion (pp. 136–137).
- Habermas: liberalism, faithful to its own ratio, could only appeal to public opinion once again, now augmented by elements of representative publicity so that an esoteric public of representatives could emerge (p. 137). Mill: voters should judge persons' characters and talents, not the questions. **Habermas:** this slides into personalization. Mill "made his peace" with the mass public's social psychology and wanted a public structured in layers of representation (p. 137).
- Tocqueville, closer to the Fronde, wanted new intermediate powers to integrate opinion into the division of powers. Mill called him the "Montesquieu of our age." The bourgeoisie turned to pre-bourgeois safeguards (p. 138).
- Tocqueville went beyond Mill by pointing to the despotism of an increasingly bureaucratized state, which he called the centralization of government power. The modern Civil Service was created in Britain in this liberal century. His portrait is of a gentle, provident power that keeps citizens in "perpetual childhood" (p. 138).
- Socialism seemed to him an extension of this. In 1848 he opposed a constitutional right to work, because the state would become sole owner of everything: "that is communism" (p. 139).

**Marx's second thoughts (pp. 139–140)**
- The *Manifesto's* theory was gauged to the limited liberal state. In the 1852 essay on Napoleon III's coup, Marx had second thoughts and gave the phenomenon the same name Tocqueville had, "centralization of government power." In the Commune address he was so worried by the weight of state power that he considered socialism possible only if the working class did not simply lay hold of the ready-made state machinery. Instead the bureaucratic-military machinery had to be smashed (p. 139).
- Habermas notes that Lenin replaced the "smashed" Tsarist apparatus with the more powerful Central Committee, and that Marx summed up the socialist public sphere as the state "withering away" (p. 139).
- The liberal warning exposed a presupposition the socialists shared with the bourgeoisie, a "natural order" of social reproduction. Administration of things could be governed by the laws of political economy without extended controversy. Implicitly, Marx thus still viewed socialistically emancipated public opinion as the physiocrats had once viewed it: as insight into the ordre naturel (p. 140).

**Closing diagnosis (p. 140)**
- Over the hundred years after liberalism's heyday, capitalism became "organized," and the earlier relation of public and private spheres dissolved. Neither model fits a public sphere suspended between them (p. 140).
- Two related tendencies signal breakdown: it penetrates more spheres but loses its political function of subjecting what it publicizes to critical control (p. 140). Goldschmidt (quoted by Habermas) names them as too much publicity, which disregards the individual's privacy, and too little publicity, with more secrecy in formerly public areas (p. 140).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* A town of 100 adults debates one rule: "Leave the market unregulated."
- **Property-owners' franchise:** 20 voters, all owners with the same interest. Vote: 20 for, 0 against. Agreement is 20/20 = 100%. It looks like rational consensus (the eighteenth-century picture, pp. 131, 135).
- **After extension:** 80 non-owners join, giving 100 voters. Suppose 80 favour regulation and 20 owners oppose. Result: 80 against, 20 for. The majority share is 80/100 = 80%, the minority 20/100 = 20%.
- **Reading with the text:** the majority is a group of equal position, so for Mill it is just a power. Its decision cannot be assumed to be the general interest, and the 20% need protection of free expression and tolerance (pp. 134–135). For Marx, the non-owners' entry is also what could move the rules of production onto the public agenda (p. 127).

## Concepts
- [[public-sphere]] — a sphere of private people debating state and society. Here it is shown as ideology and then reshaped by a wider public (pp. 117, 125, 127).
- [[ideology-critique]] (new) — showing that a claim (public opinion = reason) hides particular class interests (pp. 117, 124–125).
- [[civil-society]] (new) — the private sphere of commodity exchange and social labour, in Hegel and Marx a source of antagonism rather than natural harmony (pp. 117–119, 124).
- [[public-opinion-hegel]] (new) — "empirical universal" of the Many's views, formally free but with no criterion of its own (pp. 117, 121).
- [[natural-order-presupposition]] (new) — the assumption (physiocrats, bourgeois, later socialists) that social reproduction is harmonious and law-governed (pp. 130–131, 140).
- [[tyranny-of-public-opinion]] (new) — Mill's and Tocqueville's view that majority opinion coerces and enforces conformity (pp. 133–134).
- [[representative-publicity]] (new) — liberal proposal of an elite public of representatives to guard publicity against the masses (p. 137).
- [[derivative-private-autonomy]] (new) — the counter-model in which private freedom derives from the citizens' public sphere (pp. 128–129).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Mill/Tocqueville on public opinion as coercive majority ↔ spiral of silence: the dominant opinion silences dissent — both link perceived majority pressure to conformity.
- [metaphor] Habermas's "two tendencies" (too much / too little publicity, p. 140) ↔ refeudalization: staged publicity by organized interests.
- [structural-analogy] Representative publicity (elite filter of opinion) ↔ two-step flow: opinion passes through a smaller circle before reaching the mass.
- [metaphor] Marx's "weapons of publicity pointed against the bourgeoisie" ↔ encoding/decoding: institutions turned to oppositional uses.

## Questions this raises
- If Hegel and Marx show that the public sphere hid class power, what normative standard survives to judge later "organized" publics?
- Does Mill's elite public escape the personalization he allowed, or does it reproduce the representative publicity of pre-bourgeois society?
- Can any socialist or liberal model avoid the "natural order" presupposition and still keep critical debate?
- What does "too much publicity" and "too little publicity" look like in an organized capitalism (the following chapters presumably take this up)?
