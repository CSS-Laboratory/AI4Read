# The Bourgeois Public Sphere: Idea and Ideology (part 1) — prehistory of "public opinion" and Kant's publicity

## L0 — One line
The idea of "public opinion" took shape when "opinion" (uncertain judgment, reputation) was merged with "critique," and Kant gave it its classic form as the principle of publicity linking politics to morality, while tying it to a property-owning public.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Habermas asks where the idea of "public opinion" came from and what it claimed. Section 12 follows the word from "opinion" (uncertain judgment, reputation) through Hobbes, Locke, Bayle, Bolingbroke, Burke, the physiocrats, Rousseau, Bentham and Guizot to Germany. Section 13 shows Kant giving the bourgeois public sphere its fully developed theory: publicity joins politics and morality and drives enlightenment. But Kant admits only property-owning private people to the public. His philosophy of history also relies on social conditions that it cannot guarantee. Habermas shows the strain between the idea and its social basis. The unit ends before Hegel and Marx.

**Prerequisites:** basic early-modern European history (absolutism, religious civil war, French Revolution); the idea of a social contract; Kant's distinction between the empirical ("phenomenal") and the intelligible ("noumenal") realm. Terms used below: physiocrats = French thinkers who held that society follows its own natural laws and who defended absolutism; *public éclairé* = enlightened public; *législateur* = lawgiver; publicity = openness of political action to the critical public.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- "Opinion" first meant uncertain judgment and reputation among the multitude. Both meanings were opposed to the rationality that "public opinion" later claimed (pp. 89–90).
- Hobbes made conscience a private "opinion" with no political weight. Habermas says his devaluation of religious conviction, although not meant to give opinion a claim to truth, led to an upward evaluation of all private convictions (pp. 90–91). Locke's "Law of Opinion" was a social-control law of reputation, not yet a public opinion (pp. 91–92).
- The strict meaning of *opinion publique* (French for "public opinion"), opinion purified by critical discussion, arose when the physiocrats (French thinkers who held that society follows its own natural laws and who defended absolutism) tied it to the *public éclairé* (enlightened public). Britain's "public spirit" and Burke's "general opinion" led the same way (pp. 93–96).
- Rousseau and the physiocrats polarized the term. Rousseau made "public opinion" the sole legislator but dropped rational debate and needed a guiding *législateur* (lawgiver). The Revolution and Bentham joined the critical and the legislative functions (pp. 96–101).
- On Habermas's account, Kant's publicity (openness of political action to the public's critical judgment) held good as the one principle that could guarantee the convergence of politics and morality. Habermas says Kant conceived of the public sphere at once as the principle of the legal order and the method of enlightenment (pp. 103–104).
- Kant's public is the "reading public" of scholars and educated mixed company. Its use of reason must be free, while private (office) use may be restricted (pp. 105–107).
- Kant admits only property-owning people who are "their own masters" to the political public. Habermas shows this works only if free competition really gives equal chances to acquire property (pp. 109–111).
- Kant's system covers the gap between the empirical and the intelligible only by assuming a natural social basis. When that is doubted, politics must create the basis, and the public sphere takes on a task the system cannot accommodate (pp. 111–116).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter plan (p. 89)
Habermas says the self-interpretation of the bourgeois public sphere's function crystallized in the idea of "public opinion." Section 12 gives its prehistory. Section 13 treats its classic formulation in Kant's doctrine of right. Section 14 (not in this unit) treats Hegel and Marx, who revealed the idea as problematic, and mid-19th-century liberalism, which had to admit the ambivalence of its idea and ideology (p. 89).

### 12. Public Opinion – Opinion publique – Öffentliche Meinung: prehistory of the phrase

**Meanings of "opinion" (pp. 89–90).**
- English and French "opinion" took over the Latin *opinio*: a judgment that is uncertain and not fully demonstrated. This matches the philosophical use from Plato's *doxa* to Hegel's *Meinung* (p. 89).
- A second meaning matters more here: "reputation," what one represents in the opinion of others. The word carried such a collective connotation that social attributes were superfluous. Shakespeare lacked "common," "general" or "vulgar opinion," let alone "public opinion" (pp. 89–90).
- In French, mores and conventions were simply *les opinions* (p. 90).
- "Public opinion" is a late-18th-century coinage for the critical reflection of a public competent to judge. Both older meanings were antithetical to the rationality it claimed. In English the contrast between opinion and truth or reason was less sharp than the French antithesis, fixed in the 17th century, between opinion and critique (p. 90).

**Hobbes (pp. 90–91).**
- Hobbes identified "conscience" with "opinion." He was guided by the religious civil war. In *Leviathan* (1651) he projected a state based solely on the prince's *auctoritas*, independent of subjects' convictions (p. 90).
- Subjects were excluded from the public sphere embodied in the state apparatus. So their conflicting convictions were banned from politics. Religion became private conviction, and conscience became opinion (p. 90).
- Hobbes put believing, judging and opining on one "chain of opinions"; conscience is "nothing else but man's settled judgment and opinion" (p. 90).
- Habermas: Hobbes did not mean to give opinion the claim to truth he took from conscience. Yet he commented on a development in which privatized religion and property freed civil society's private people from Church and estates, and so made their private opinions more important. His devaluation of religious conviction led to an upward evaluation of all private convictions (p. 91).

**Locke (pp. 91–92).**
- Locke set a "Law of Opinion" beside divine and state law. It judges virtues and vices, and virtue is measured by public esteem (p. 91).
- "Law of Opinion and Reputation" revives the sense of reputation, but cleansed of mere opining and deceptive appearance. It is also called "Philosophical Law" and "Law of Private Censure": an informal web of folkways whose indirect control works better than formal sanctions (p. 91).
- It already has an element of awareness, owing to its origin in privatized religious faith and secularized morality. Yet Locke does not speak of "public opinion," for these reasons (pp. 91–92):
  - It does not arise in public discussion but binds "by a secret and tacit consent."
  - It is not applied to the laws of the state; it rests on the consent of private men without authority to make law.
  - It is not tied to education or property. Contributing to it needs only the utterance of the "habits" that public opinion would later oppose as prejudices.
- Through its identification with conscience, "opinion" gained a status that freed it from the polemical link with prejudice (p. 92).

**Bayle, Encyclopedists, Rousseau's first use (pp. 92–93).**
- French kept the prejudice connotation. For Bayle the *Régime de la Critique* replaced the Law of Opinion. Critique became the weighing of *pour et contre* by a *raison* applicable to everything and destructive of opinion (p. 92).
- But Bayle treated criticism as strictly private. Reason stayed inward and outwardly subordinate. Critique that crossed into politics degenerated into pamphleteering, while in Britain a political press grew from the pamphlet (p. 92).
- The Encyclopedists took "opinion" in the polemical sense of uncertainty and vacuity. Reason and critique shake off the yoke of scholasticism, opinion and authority. A German editor translated this loosely as "public opinion" (p. 92).
- One year earlier Rousseau, in the *Discourse on the Arts and Sciences*, first used *opinion publique*, but in the old sense. The critics are "enemies of public opinion" because they undermine faith and virtue (p. 93).

**British line: "public spirit," Bolingbroke, Burke (pp. 93–95).**
- In English the route ran via "public spirit." Steele moved it from an individual's sacrificial attitude to an objective entity, a general opinion of the age, hard to separate from its instrument, the press (p. 93).
- Bolingbroke (*Craftsman*, 1730) called the public spirit of the people, guided by the opposition, a "Spirit of Liberty" against corruption. His claim: "if all men cannot reason, all men can feel" (p. 93).
- Habermas: this concept kept Locke's immediacy, the people as reliable common sense. It already had Enlightenment features. In it, "anticipated Rousseauism" fused with public criticism: a direct sense of right, and opinion articulated into judgment by the public clash of arguments (pp. 93–94).
- Burke made the needed distinctions. In a letter to the Bristol electors he called "general opinion" the "vehicle and organ of legislative omnipotence" (p. 94).
- Habermas: Burke's public no longer has mere opinion. Its views come from private reflection on public affairs and from public discussion. Burke says that in free countries men "sift, examine, and discuss" public matters. He claims more public wisdom is found in shops and manufactories than in princes' cabinets where none dares to have an opinion (p. 94).
- The Oxford Dictionary dates "public opinion" first to 1781 (p. 95).

**French line: physiocrats, Mercier (pp. 95–96).**
- Around mid-century *opinion publique* barely differed from opinion. It meant the people's opinion based on tradition and *bon sens*. Rousseau appealed to its naturalness; the Encyclopedists tried to dissolve it by ideology critique (p. 95).
- Only when the physiocrats ascribed it to the *public éclairé* did it get its strict meaning: an opinion purified by critical discussion, a true opinion. The contradiction between opinion and critique vanished (p. 95).
- The physiocrats asserted that civil society follows laws of its own against state intervention, yet acted as apologists of absolutism. Marx called their doctrine a bourgeois reproduction of the feudal system (p. 95). The monarch watches over the *ordre naturel* and gets his insight from the *public éclairé* (p. 95).
- Mercier, who seems to have been the first to extract the rigorous concept, distinguished governors from scholars: scholars determine public opinion, governors put its conclusions into practice. Good books "govern Europe" and teach government its duties (pp. 95–96).
- Habermas: *l'opinion publique* was the enlightened outcome of common reflection on social order. It did not rule, but an enlightened ruler must follow its insight (p. 96).
- This is a doctrine of the dual authority of public opinion and Prince, *ratio* and *voluntas*. In Britain public spirit could compel lawmakers to legitimize themselves. In France the critical function stayed separate from the legislative function (p. 96).
- Habermas: Turgot's rationalization of sovereignty (Le Harpe's remark) did not yet give democratic legislative authority to the private people. *Auctoritas facit legem* no longer held, but its opposite was not yet achieved (p. 96).

**Rousseau (pp. 96–99).**
- Rousseau's *ordre naturel* is transcendent to existing society. The break between nature and society tore each individual into *homme* and *citoyen*. The social contract demands self-surrender without reservation, and the *homme* is absorbed by the *citoyen* (pp. 96–97).
- Habermas: this is an unbourgeois idea of an intrusively political society with no autonomous private sphere. The general will does not emerge from competition of private interests; that would be the liberal *volonté de tous*. The *volonté générale* is a kind of saving instinct of humanity (p. 97).
- Rousseau anchored the constitution's spirit in the hearts of citizens, in opinion (p. 97).
- Habermas: Locke's Law of Opinion became sovereign through Rousseau. Opinion publique was elevated to sole legislator, and rational-critical debate in the public sphere was eliminated (p. 97). *Bon sens* suffices to perceive the common welfare. Long debates bring particular interests forward. The general will is a consensus of hearts more than of arguments (pp. 97–98).
- Rousseau also uses *l'opinion* for the opinion of the *public éclairé*, voiced in press and salons. He sets against it the opinion of simple morals (p. 98).
- Even this opinion needs guidance. In social control, a censor is its spokesman ("Public opinion is the sort of law whose censor is the minister"). In legislation, the *législateur* cannot rely on force or discussion and must use an indirect influence "which can compel without violence and persuade without convincing" (p. 98).
- Habermas: so Rousseau's "democracy of unpublic opinion" ultimately postulated manipulative exercise of power. In the passage Habermas cites, Rousseau says the general will is always right but the judgment guiding it is not always enlightened, so matters must be shown as they are, sometimes as they are to appear (pp. 98–99).
- **Question (Habermas):** why did Rousseau not call the sovereign opinion simply "opinion"? **Answer (Habermas):** direct democracy needs the sovereign physically present, as in the Greek polis. The attribute *publique* comes from the *place publique* and the citizens assembled for acclamation, not from rational-critical debate (p. 99).

**Revolution, Bentham, Guizot (pp. 99–101).**
- The physiocrats wanted absolutism plus a critical public sphere; Rousseau wanted democracy without public debate. Both claimed *opinion publique* (p. 99).
- The Revolution joined the critical and legislative functions. The 1791 Constitution joined popular sovereignty to the parliamentary constitutional state, which gives a constitutional guarantee to a public sphere in the political realm. Bergasse said that before public opinion "all authorities become silent" (p. 99).
- Bentham first connected, in monograph form, public opinion and the principle of publicity (p. 99). Bentham: the more temptations political power faces, the more it needs permanent control by public opinion. The public as a whole is a tribunal worth more than all others; everyone felt that it might err but was incorruptible, tended to become enlightened and decided the fate of public men (p. 100).
- Public opinion in turn needs publicity of parliamentary debate to stay informed. Parliament's deliberations are part of the public's deliberation in general. Publicity inside and outside parliament secures critical debate, which turns domination from will into reason (p. 100).
- In Britain, public opinion prevailed against dead statutes chiefly since George III, and what was best there was brought about through constant violation of the laws. Bentham called the "regime of publicity" imperfect and newly tolerated (pp. 100–101).
- Guizot gave the classic formula of the "rule of public opinion." The representative system compels citizens to seek reason, justice and truth through (1) discussion, (2) publicity and (3) liberty of the press (p. 101).

**Germany (pp. 101–102).**
- Forster seems to have given currency to *öffentliche Meinung* in the early 1790s (letters of 1793). He said that Germany has 7,000 authors yet no *Gemeingeist* and no public opinion, and that the words were strange there (p. 101).
- Habermas: the concept of the public sphere as an element in the political realm was fully formed in Britain and France before its import into Germany (p. 101).
- Wieland's definitions added nothing new. Public opinion breaks through where prejudices yield to truth and amounts to the most exact weighing of reasons pro and con. It arises among the informed and spreads in classes that matter. The "lowest classes" are excluded for lack of leisure (p. 102).
- Rousseauan elements in Wieland were later used by political Romanticism to equate public opinion with the mute *Volksgeist*. Wieland's own concept aimed to bring priestly deception and cabinet secrecy before critical public debate (p. 102).

> **Intuition note.** "Public opinion" did not grow straight out of "opinion." Habermas's account shows several steps, among them: privatized conviction gained weight, and the contradiction between opinion and critique vanished once the physiocrats tied *opinion publique* to the *public éclairé* (p. 95). Rousseau and the physiocrats then claimed the same term in opposite ways: acclamation without debate vs. debate without legislative power (p. 99).

### 13. Publicity as the bridging principle between politics and morality (Kant)

**Politics as morality (pp. 102–104).**
- Habermas: the public's criticism of absolutism saw itself as unpolitical. It aimed to rationalize politics in the name of morality. In the 18th century political philosophy was reduced to moral philosophy, and "moral" included the emerging "social" (p. 103).
- Kant (*Perpetual Peace*, App. I): "true politics can never take a step without rendering homage to morality." He repeats two postulates: every state's constitution republican, and relations among states pacifist within a cosmopolitan federation. In a perfectly just order, compulsion works only so that "reason alone has force." This is the opposite of *auctoritas non veritas facit legem* (p. 103).
- Habermas: Hobbes could use that formula because peace required monopolizing public power and neutralizing civil society as private. Kant rehabilitated moral reasoning two centuries later, when private people had formed a public with political functions articulating state and society. So Kant's publicity held good as the one principle that could guarantee the convergence of politics and morality, and he conceived of the public sphere at once as the principle of the legal order and the method of enlightenment (pp. 103–104).

**Enlightenment and the public (pp. 104–106).**
- Kant defines tutelage as inability to use one's understanding without direction from another. It is self-incurred when the cause is lack of resolution and courage (p. 104).
- Enlightenment is for the individual a maxim: think for oneself. For humanity it is an objective tendency toward a perfectly just order. Both are mediated by the public sphere. The individual finds escape hard, but that the public enlighten itself is "more possible," and with freedom enlightenment is "almost sure to follow" (p. 104).
- Kant: we would not think much or correctly if we did not think "in common with others" (p. 104).
- Like the Encyclopedists, Kant first saw enlightenment as a matter for scholars. The conflict of the faculties pits the "lower" faculty (philosophers, who follow reason alone and have "the public presentation of truth" as their function) against the "higher" faculties (theology, law, medicine). The higher faculties rest on authority, are state-controlled and apply science (pp. 104–105).
- The public sphere's center is the academy but it is not merely academic. Philosophers debate before the government and before the "people." The people's position is ambiguous: under tutelage, yet claiming maturity. Anyone who can use reason in public qualifies, not only philosophers (p. 105).
- Kant: public use of reason is the use made "as a scholar before the reading public." Private use is that made in a civil post or office, where one must obey. Public use "must always be free" and alone brings enlightenment. Private use may be narrowly restricted. Each person is called to be a "publicist" (pp. 105–106).
- Habermas: the "world" in which the public is constituted designates the realm of the public sphere (Weltkenntnis, cosmopolitanism). It does not mean the transcendental totality of phenomena. It means humanity as a species in the form in which its unity appears: the critically debating reading public within the bourgeois strata, men of letters and salons with "mixed companies" (including business people and women) who "argue" (p. 106).

**From human beings to citizens (p. 107).**
- Where communication concerns commonwealth affairs, the public of "human beings" becomes one of "citizens." Under the republican constitution the political public sphere is the organizing principle of the liberal constitutional state. Civil society is the sphere of private autonomy, with equality before the law (p. 107).
- Laws trace to the "popular will that has its source in reason." Kant calls them "public" laws, against "private" ones such as custom and mores, which tacitly claim to bind. A public law requires "the will of the entire people" (p. 107).
- Habermas: Kant follows Rousseau except in one decisive point: popular sovereignty can be realized only on the condition of public use of reason. Kant argued that restricting the public sphere is the effective cause of all secret societies; the famous statement that the freedom of the pen is "the only safeguard of the rights of the people" was made in this connection (p. 107).

**Publicity as truth test and legal norm (pp. 107–108).**
- In the *Critique of Pure Reason* Kant gives public consensus a pragmatic role as a test of truth. The "touchstone" distinguishing conviction from mere persuasion is external: the possibility of communicating it and finding it valid for all human reason (pp. 107–108).
- In the philosophy of right, the agreement of all judgments, vouched for by publicity (which in Kant only lacks the name "public opinion"), gains a constitutive significance beyond its pragmatic value. Political actions agree with law and morality only so far as their maxims are capable of, or need, publicity. Laws were to be validated before public opinion as universal and rational. Rule of legal norms replaces domination as law of nature, and politics could in principle be turned into morality (p. 108).

**The dilemma and the philosophy of history (pp. 108–109).**
- **Question (Habermas):** how can politics and morality agree before a juridical condition exists? **Answer:** the distributive sameness of all wills is not enough. A collective oneness of will is needed. Kant expected the beginning of a juridical condition only from political force. The public's indirect assumption of power was not seen as itself political, and the moral self-interpretation of the public sphere imposed on even its political efforts an abstinence from political coercion (pp. 108–109).
- Kant's resolution is a philosophy of history. Even without the active efforts of inwardly free individuals, outwardly free conditions come about through the constraints of nature. Progress consists in more products of legality, not in more morality (p. 109).
- If nature uses "antagonism within society" to develop humanity's endowments in a society that administers justice universally, then the "perfectly just civil constitution" can only be a "pathologically enforced social union," a moral whole in appearance only (p. 109).
- Kant's problem: given rational beings who need universal laws yet are each secretly inclined to exempt themselves, establish a constitution in which their conflicting private intentions check one another, so that their public conduct is as if they had none. Habermas calls this a variation of Mandeville's "private vices, public benefits" (p. 109).

**Sociological conditions: the property qualification (pp. 109–111).**
- The public depends on social relations among freely competing commodity owners within their private autonomy. Only property-owning private people are admitted to the public, because their autonomy is rooted in commodity exchange (pp. 109–110).
- Kant: the qualification (besides being an adult male) is to be one's own master (*sui iuris*) with some property, which may include any skill, trade, fine art or science. One must earn one's living by selling what is one's own, not by letting others use one. Kant admits that the line is hard to draw (p. 110).
- Habermas: this separates owners from what later became free wage labour. Wage workers must sell their labour power as their sole commodity. On Kant's view, only owners were their own masters and only they should vote (p. 110).
- Habermas: the restriction fits publicity only if free competition really gives equal chances to acquire property. Kant says wealth may become unequal over generations, but the superior may not prevent subordinates from rising by talent, industry and luck. Otherwise he could coerce without being subject to counter-coercion (p. 110).
- The propertyless are excluded without violating publicity. They are not citizens but potential ones, with the same claim to legal protection but without a part in legislation (p. 111).

> **Intuition note.** Kant's restriction of the vote is compatible with publicity only if an assumption about the market holds: that anyone with ability and luck can become an owner. Habermas shows how much the argument rests on this fiction of justice immanent in free commerce. The claim (not stated outright by Habermas in these words) is that if the market does not give equal chances, the restriction loses its justification.

**Bourgeois and homme; phenomenal and noumenal (pp. 111–112).**
- Kant shared the liberals' confidence that the social preconditions would come about by themselves and might be near, as an *ordre naturel* on the horizon (p. 111).
- Habermas: the "fiction of a justice immanent in free commerce" made plausible the merging of *bourgeois* and *homme*, the self-interested owner and the autonomous individual. The citoyen could be seen under both legality and morality. Citizens behaved outwardly as if inwardly free (p. 111).
- So cosmopolitan citizenship was empirically conceivable. The *res publica phaenomenon* could actualize the *res publica noumenon*, uniting two legislations: owners' drives and spiritually free human beings. This matches the solution of the third antinomy: each effect is free regarding its intelligible cause and necessary in its empirical occurrence (p. 112).

**Where the system breaks (pp. 112–114).**
- Habermas: Kant could not consistently maintain this distinction, since practical-reason laws cannot depend on empirical conditions. If the natural basis of the juridical condition is problematic, then creating it becomes the content and task of politics. The public sphere then gets a new function that the Kantian system cannot accommodate (p. 112).
- Political agents (prince, party, leader or citizen) aiming at a juridical condition must exert positive influence on the arbitrary will of others. This happens as a rule by force. If it is moral, it must aim at the public's universal end, welfare, and be checked for empirical success. Political maxims then need publicity because they "must accord with the public's universal end, happiness." The task of politics is "to make the public satisfied with its condition" (pp. 112–113).
- Earlier in the same essay Kant says maxims must come from the pure concept of the duty of right, "regardless of what the physical consequences may be" (p. 113).
- Habermas: given the presupposition of an existing natural basis, Kant could separate state welfare from citizens' welfare and morality from legality. But he did not consistently rely on it. Statements exempt morality from progress and limit progress to legality. Yet Kant also admits that the race is progressing morally and that various evidence suggests "considerable moral progress" in our age (p. 113).
- If the juridical condition has to be brought about by a politics in agreement with morality, progress in legality depends on progress in morality. The *res publica phaenomenon* becomes a product of the *res publica noumenon* (p. 113).

**The "Platonic ideal" (pp. 114–115).**
- Kant calls the *res publica noumenon* a "Platonic Ideal," an "eternal norm" that "averts all war." The corresponding civil society is its representation in experience, won painfully through hostilities and wars (p. 114).
- Habermas: for Kant an "ideal" is an idea *in individuo*, further from reality than an idea, with only a regulative function. Plato erroneously gave such ideas constitutive significance. It is "astounding" that Kant calls the *res publica noumenon* a Platonic ideal. Habermas says it is no mere verbal lapse, since Kant states in the indicative that it averts all war (p. 114).
- But defining the ideal, Kant says that trying to realize the ideal in an example is impracticable (pp. 114–115).

**Two versions of Kant's political philosophy (pp. 115–116).**
- **Official version (Habermas):** a cosmopolitan order arises from natural necessity alone. Moral politics is then legal conduct from duty under positive laws, with the rule of law guaranteed by publicity. The public's ability to function is posited along with the natural basis (p. 115).
- **Unofficial version:** politics must first push to actualize a juridical condition. The order comes from natural necessity and moral politics. Making laws positive needs a collective will agreed on the public's welfare. The public sphere is where the intelligible unity of everyone's empirical ends is brought about and legality is to issue from morality (p. 115).
- In this second version the philosophy of history guides the public, since reason's laws coincide with welfare there, and it is itself to become public opinion. Kant: such a philosophical history "must be regarded as possible and … as contributing to this end of nature" (pp. 115–116).
- Kant says "a certain commitment of heart" to the good "must step by step ascend the throne," and he considers difficulties of publicity for maxims of world progress. Philosophers should teach publicly, though they were decried as dangerous. "The prohibition of publicity impedes the progress of a people toward improvement" (p. 116).
- Habermas: publicity is the vehicle linking the intelligible unity of consciousness with empirical consciousness. Its universality is that of empirical consciousness in general, and Hegel's philosophy of right would name it public opinion (p. 116).
- Habermas: the public sphere fit easily into Kant's categories only as long as the division of empirical and intelligible, phenomenal and noumenal, can count on the social conditions of the liberal model, the bourgeois–homme–citoyen relation (p. 116). The sentence breaks off here at the end of this part.

> **Intuition note.** Kant's idea works only if society is organized so that selfish owners, by competing, produce results as if they were free moral persons. When this assumption is doubted, "publicity" has to do double work. It must both create the conditions of justice and rest on them, which his system cannot support.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Kant's property rule is justified only if free competition gives everyone equal chances to acquire property (p. 110). Imagine a village of 10 adults: 6 own property and 4 do not. Only owners vote, so 6/10 = 60% of adults are in the public.

- **Case A (the condition holds).** Each year one non-owner of ability and luck becomes an owner. Voters after year 1: 6 + 1 = 7 (70%); year 2: 8 (80%); year 3: 9 (90%); year 4: 10 (100%). The exclusion is temporary and tied to talent, industry and luck, as Kant argues (pp. 110–111).
- **Case B (the condition fails).** No non-owner can ever acquire property. Voters stay at 6 forever (60%). Then, on Habermas's reading of Kant, the exclusion is no longer compatible with publicity (p. 110). Kant's own warning then applies: without equal chances, the better-off could coerce others without facing counter-measures (p. 110).

The example shows why the "natural basis" assumption carries so much weight (pp. 110–112).

## Concepts
- [[public-sphere]] — here the sphere of private people debating public affairs critically, which interprets itself through "public opinion" (p. 89, p. 104)
- [[public-opinion]] (new) — opinion purified by critical discussion among a *public éclairé*; late-18th-century coinage, unlike older "opinion" as uncertain judgment or reputation (pp. 89–90, 95)
- [[law-of-opinion]] (new) — Locke's informal law of reputation that measures virtue and vice and enforces by private censure, not public debate (p. 91)
- [[publicity-principle]] (new) — Kant's principle that political actions agree with law and morality only so far as their maxims are capable of, or need, publicity (p. 108). Habermas says Kant conceived of the public sphere as the principle of the legal order and the method of enlightenment (p. 104)
- [[public-use-of-reason]] (new) — Kant's term for argument by a person as a scholar before the reading public. It contrasts with the restricted "private use" in an office (pp. 105–106)
- [[volonte-generale]] (new) — Rousseau's general will, more a consensus of hearts than of arguments, and not the outcome of competing private interests (pp. 97–98)
- [[bourgeois-homme-citoyen]] (new) — the liberal merging of owner, autonomous human being and citizen, which made it possible to see the citizen under both legality and morality; Habermas ties it to a fiction of justice in free commerce (p. 111)
- [[res-publica-phaenomenon-noumenon]] (new) — Kant's empirical republic vs. its intelligible ideal, whose relation Habermas finds strained (pp. 112–114)

## Canonical instances

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Locke's Law of Opinion (reputation as informal control) ↔ communication-theory: spiral of silence — both describe opinion as social pressure working through fear of the judgment of others (p. 91).
- [metaphor] Mercier's scholars who shape public opinion and governors who apply it ↔ communication-theory: opinion leaders and two-step flow — experts shape what a less informed public takes up (pp. 95–96).
- [structural-analogy] Kant's communicability test ("valid for all human reason") ↔ communication-theory: communication as a way of validating belief — truth tested by whether it survives exchange with others (p. 108).

## Questions this raises
- If Kant's publicity requires equal chances to acquire property, what empirical evidence would show that this condition holds or fails?
- Rousseau's "opinion publique" lacks debate; the physiocrats' lacks legislative power. Can one real institution give a public opinion both critical and legislative functions (cf. the 1791 Constitution, p. 99)?
- Kant's unofficial version makes the philosophy of history itself a part of public opinion (p. 116). What does it mean for a theory to be a factor in the process it describes?
- How do Hegel and Marx, announced for section 14, respond to this ambivalence (p. 89)?
