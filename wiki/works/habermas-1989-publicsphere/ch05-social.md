# The Social-Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere

## L0 — One line
When state and society interpenetrated, the private sphere and the reasoning public that grew out of it lost their basis, and critical publicity gave way to manipulative publicity and culture consumption.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Habermas asks what became of the bourgeois public sphere once the separation of state and society, on which it rested, dissolved. From the 1870s, market concentration, crises and mass suffrage led to state intervention, while private organisations took over public functions. The result was a repoliticized social sphere (a fused zone of state and society) that is neither private nor public. The family lost its economic and educational roles and kept only a consumption-centred pseudo-privacy. The debating public became a culture-consuming public, and publicity became manipulative. Parliament could no longer be the place where discussion finds the "truth" of laws. The chapter gives the social-structural half of the explanation of the public sphere's decline.

**Prerequisites:** the liberal state/society distinction; the difference between private and public law; the idea of a bourgeois public sphere of private people debating (assumed from earlier chapters, not restated here); basic economic history of 19th-century capitalism.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The bourgeois public sphere was itself part of the private realm. It depended on the separation of state and society, so a growing interlocking of the two undermined it (pp. 141–142).
- State intervention did not by itself dissolve the separation. The decisive step came when societal powers took on public functions and the state took on social functions, a dialectic of "societalization" of the state and "state-ification" of society (pp. 142, 145).
- Concentration and crises since the 1870s made the liberal market model untenable. Weaker groups used political means, and the state took on formative and service functions (pp. 143–148).
- Law shows the fusion. Private law underwent a "publification" and public law a privatization (pp. 148–151).
- The family was disengaged from social labour and became a consumer unit with a pseudo-private interior. The world of work became a quasi-public sphere of its own (pp. 151–159).
- The reading, debating public became a culture-consuming public. The market now shapes the content of culture, discussion itself is sold as a commodity, and the new media discourage "talking back" (pp. 159–171).
- Habermas rejects the view that the consuming public is only a fringe of semiliterate masses around an educated centre that still continues, to a degree, the old tradition. Culture consumption almost without exception increases with status, and the public splits into specialists and consumers (pp. 172–175).
- Organised interests and parties now exercise and balance power among themselves. Publicity becomes manipulative, the generality of the norm can no longer be maintained as a principle, laws can no longer be vindicated as to "truth", and the parliamentary public takes on a plebiscitary character (pp. 175–180).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 16. The tendency toward a mutual infiltration of public and private spheres

**Why the public sphere was fragile (pp. 141–142).**
- The bourgeois public sphere developed between state and society, but it remained part of the private realm (p. 141).
- The separation of state and society first meant the disengagement of social reproduction from political power, which the High Middle Ages had welded together. A market economy created the "social" sphere, and production was released from public-authority functions (p. 141).
- Private society became a sphere of private autonomy only as it was freed from mercantilist regulation (p. 141).
- Hence the rise of state interventionism from the last quarter of the 19th century did not *per se* interlock public and private. A "neomercantilist" policy could restrict private people's autonomy without touching the private character of their commerce with each other (pp. 141–142).
- Society's private character became questionable only when the powers of society themselves took on functions of public authority. Habermas calls this a kind of *refeudalization* of society (p. 142).
- The new interventionism was embraced by a state that, thanks to constitutionalization of a political public sphere (limited in Germany), tended to adopt civil society's interests. Interventions thus carried impulses from the private sphere (p. 142).
- Interventionism arose when conflicts of interest could no longer be settled privately and were moved to the political level. Its counterpart was the transfer of public functions to private corporate bodies (p. 142).
- This dialectic of a progressive "societalization" of the state with an increasing "state-ification" of society destroyed the basis of the bourgeois public sphere, namely the separation of state and society. A repoliticized social sphere emerged to which "public" and "private" could not be usefully applied (p. 142).
- The liberal public sphere, a portion of the private realm, disintegrated with it. The changed political functions (Chapter VI) have their source in this change in the relation of public sphere and private realm (Chapter V) (pp. 142–143).

*Intuition note.* The public sphere stood on the border between two realms. When the border dissolved, it lost its place, not merely some of its power. Habermas locates the source of the downfall in this structural change in the relation between public sphere and private realm (pp. 142–143).

**Economic background: the end of the liberal era (pp. 143–145).**
- From the great depression beginning in 1873 the liberal era closed. Advanced countries turned from free trade to protectionism, and oligopolistic mergers increased. The Aktiengesellschaft in Germany and the trust in the US were vehicles of concentration. Antitrust legislation in the US and anticartel legislation in Germany followed (p. 143).
- Habermas says Germany's industrial capitalism developed "spontaneously" only with imperialism. It had to secure politically privileged foreign markets at once. Given the added functions that capitalism of this phase demanded of the state machinery, Germany could no longer catch up with the Western European–North American parliamentary constitutional state (p. 143).
- Restriction of competition prevailed internationally in the last third of the century, through mergers or price and production agreements (p. 143).
- The interplay of expansive and restrictive tendencies meant there was never a real chance of market liberalization. Contrary to classical economics, the liberal era was a mere episode, a "vast secular boom" (1775–1875) (pp. 143–144).
- Say's Law (automatic equilibrium of production and consumption) was a function of concrete historical circumstances, not of the system as such. Moreover, after a crisis equilibrium was not automatically restored at the highest level the productive forces allowed (p. 144).
- Society could no longer pretend to be free of power. The liberal model, really one of petty commodity exchange, had only horizontal exchange relations (p. 144).
- Under imperfect competition and dependent prices, social power concentrated in private hands. Vertical relations of one-sided dependency or mutual pressure appeared, and society looked like a nexus of coercive constraints, which made a strong state more urgent (p. 144).
- Habermas follows Neumann against the "nightwatchman" self-image. The state had always been as strong as bourgeois interests required (p. 144).
- While the state was liberal, one such interest was that commodity exchange and social labour stay private. The centralization of government power (a problem for both Marx and Tocqueville) did not yet touch the public/private relationship. Military expansion for privileged markets at first only strengthened an existing function. Only when new functions accrued to the state did the barrier begin to lose firmness (p. 145).

**Mass suffrage and organised interests (pp. 145–146).**
- Power was concentrated both in the private sphere of exchange and in a public sphere promising universal access. This strengthened the economically weaker parties' tendency to use political means (p. 145).
- Franchise extension examples: British reforms of 1867 and 1883, Napoleon III's universal suffrage, and Bismarck's franchise in the North German Federation and the Empire (p. 145).
- Pauperized strata, and the classes threatened by them, sought political influence to compensate for violated equality of opportunity in the economy (if it ever existed) (p. 145).
- The attempt to keep private interests out of the public sphere failed once the conditions of privatization were themselves drawn into the conflict of organised interests (p. 145).
- Unions acted in the labour market and, via socialist parties, on legislation. Entrepreneurs and "state-friendly" forces answered by exchanging private societal power for political power. Examples: Bismarck's Socialist Law, and his Social Security Insurance, which showed state intervention yielding to pressure from below (pp. 145–146).
- Masses with political rights translated economic antagonisms into political conflicts. Interventions sometimes favoured the weaker strata and sometimes rejected them, and it was not always clear which side gained. In general they were guided by the interest in the system's equilibrium, which the free market could no longer secure (p. 146).
- Habermas cites Strachey's conclusion, which he calls only *prima facie* paradoxical: the struggle of democratic forces against capitalism has saved the system, by making wage earners' lives tolerable and keeping open the market for final products (p. 146). He also cites Galbraith's analysis of countervailing powers as explaining this mechanism (p. 146).

**New state functions (pp. 146–148).**
- State budget size indicates growth, but a qualitative analysis shows new functions were added (p. 146).
- Traditional order-maintaining functions: police, judiciary, cautious taxation, military (pp. 146–147). New "formative" functions (p. 147):
  - protection, compensation and subsidies for weaker groups (workers, tenants, consumers; redistribution);
  - preventing or guiding long-term changes in social structure (e.g. strengthening the middle classes);
  - influencing private and regulating public investment, within a broader function of controlling the business cycle.
- Concentration made the economy accessible to econometric modelling of the national economy, introduced in Great Britain, the US and Canada shortly before the Second World War (p. 147).
- The state also took over services formerly private. It entrusted private persons with public tasks, coordinated private activity in an overall plan, or produced and distributed itself (p. 147).
- Public services expanded because growth changed the relation of private to social costs. As mass buying power rose, public costs of private consumption were added to public costs of private production (p. 147).
- The formula of collective provision for life's necessities hides the multiplicity of functions and the organised private interests behind them (p. 148).
- The state intervened deeply because societal interests became political dynamics that acted back on their own sphere (p. 148).
- Habermas says the "democratic influence" on the economic order could not be denied. The unpropertied masses secured that their share of national income did not fall over the long run, though by mid-century it did not seem to have risen essentially (p. 148).
- Areas protected by interventionism should be distinguished from a merely state-regulated private sphere. Private institutions took on a semipublic character, and economic units a quasi-political one. State and societal institutions fused into one functional complex not differentiable by public/private criteria (p. 148).

*Intuition note.* Intervention was not simply the state imposing itself on society. Social groups used the state to fight their conflicts, and the results fed back into society. So each side became partly the other.

**The legal expression (pp. 148–151).**
- In a social-welfare state, relationships multiplied that purely private or purely public law could not order, which required *social legislation* (p. 148).
- Socialist critique (reported by Habermas): private-law autonomy can be enjoyed by all only to the extent that equal economic opportunities permit legally equal chances to be realised (p. 148).
- The separation of producers from the means of production turned the legally equal relation of capitalist and wage earner into factual subordination, and private-law concepts hid a quasi-public authority (p. 149).
- Karl Renner argued that ownership of the means of production, in its actual functioning, belongs in public law. It gives the capitalist a "delegated public power of command" (p. 149).
- Since the First World War at the latest, legal development took account of societal development up to a certain point. First came a "publification of private law", later the reciprocal view of a privatization of public law. A quoted author says elements of public and private law become so interwoven that they can no longer be recognised or separated (p. 149).
- Property rights were restricted by interventionist policy and by guarantees restoring material equality. Collective contracts replaced individual ones, and tenant protections restricted the landlord, and consumers received special guarantees. Considerations of public safety at times constrain owners so far that one could speak of a kind of super-ownership by the public (p. 149).
- **Liberal jurists' complaint:** property remains formally with owners, yet they are in effect expropriated without compensation or legal protection. A quoted liberal jurist says forms of "socialization" arise by confiscatory legislation that even doctrinaire Marxism had not foreseen (pp. 149–150).
- Freedom of contract, which presumed independent determination of terms, was restricted. Contracts became standardized, which curtailed the economically weaker partner, while collective contracts aimed at equal market position (p. 150).
- Wage agreements between employers' associations and unions lost the character of strict private law and functioned as surrogate law. A quoted author says such agreements are less like exercising private autonomy than like legislation by delegated authority (p. 150).
- Private autonomy became derivative, and factual contractual relationships gained the effect of classical legal ones (p. 150).
- Contracts between public authority and private persons (*do ut des*) are marked by inequality and dependence. By the classical model they are pseudo-contracts with a quasi-public character (pp. 150–151).
- Conversely, the state's "flight" out of public law, by handing tasks to private-law enterprises or using private-law instruments itself, privatized public law. A service-providing township may contract with "customers" under private law. Neither monopoly, lack of contractual freedom, nor an administrative agency's involvement requires public law (p. 151).
- Public interest and private-law form fused into a new sphere, from the state permeating society and society the state. It can be conceived neither as purely private nor as genuinely public, nor located in either legal realm (p. 151).

### 17. The polarization of the social sphere and the intimate sphere

**The world of work becomes a sphere of its own (pp. 151–154).**
- As state and society interpenetrated, the conjugal family was cut off from social reproduction. The intimate sphere, once the centre of the private sphere, moved to its periphery as the private sphere was deprivatized (pp. 151–152).
- In the liberal era, occupation and family were structured in concordance. Now they diverge. A cited author says the family became more private and the world of work and organization more "public" (p. 152).
- The large industrial enterprise depends directly on capital concentration, and the large bureaucracy indirectly. Both created forms of social labour that depart from private occupation. Calling the firm private and the bureaucracy public lost its power to differentiate them (p. 152).
- Whoever controls a large enterprise, private power of control became objectified. The "world of work" is a sphere between private and public in the consciousness of workers and also of those with more power (p. 152).
- The separation of ownership and control, analysed especially for large stock companies, shows the material deprivatization of formally preserved owner autonomy. Management and a few large shareholders hold the control (pp. 152–153).
- Through self-financing, such firms often became independent of the capital market and, in the same degree, of the mass of shareholders (p. 153).
- Whatever the economic effect, the sociological outcome is that the large enterprise, in whatever form, ceases to be a sphere of private individual autonomy (p. 153).
- Rathenau already said large enterprises become *social institutions* (*Anstalten*). Legal institutionalism elaborated this, and Burnham and Drucker presented similar doctrines. Habermas calls them ideological but says their diagnosis of the "disappearance of the private" in social labour "hit the mark" (p. 153).
- Large enterprises gave employees status guarantees (jurisdictions, social security, integration efforts). The subjective change was greater. The category of "functionaries" (*Dienstleistende*) erases the old line between those who worked in their own private sphere and those who worked in others'. It is a depersonalized relation to an institution, not to persons (p. 153).
- A quoted author (no name is given on these pages) describes firms that build housing and organise concerts, education and care for the old. The firm's *oikos* can pervade a town, which he labels "industrial feudalism". Large administrations lose their sociological public character as they become big enterprises (p. 154).
- So American studies of the "organization man" can treat private firms, semipublic corporations and bureaucracies alike (p. 154).
- Leisure time is now the preserve of the private, and the "job" begins with occupational activity (p. 154).
- This was a *deprivatization* only from the standpoint of the property-owning private person. For workers and employees it was a *privatization*: they were no longer subject only to a patriarchal regiment but to a psychological arrangement of human relations producing "pseudo-private well-being" (p. 154).

**The family as consumer unit (pp. 154–156).**
- The family's change is less the loss of productive for consumptive functions than its progressive disengagement from the whole functional complex of social labour (p. 154).
- The bourgeois family, though no longer a production community, rested on family property that functioned capitalistically. Its upkeep was the task of the private person as commodity owner and head of family, and exchange relations influenced family relations (pp. 154–155).
- With family property replaced by individual incomes, the family lost its functions *for* production and its ability to support itself in emergencies or old age (p. 155).
- The classical risks (unemployment, accident, illness, age, death) are now largely covered by welfare-state guarantees, addressed to the individual, not the family (p. 155).
- The catalogue of risks was widened to cover services such as finding shelter, work and counselling, and compensation was supplemented by prevention. A quoted author says that prevention as social policy is in fact the same as intruding into new, previously private areas (p. 155).
- The family lost upbringing, education, protection, care, guidance and transmission of tradition, and its power to shape conduct in the bourgeois family's innermost provinces. Even the family was deprivatized by public guarantees of its status (p. 155).
- It became a consumer of income and leisure. Private autonomy now lies less in the power of disposal than in the capacity to enjoy services one is entitled to. The resulting intensified privacy is an *illusion*, in an interior domain shrunk to the family as a community of consumers (p. 156).
- Loss of control had a secondary, burden-reducing effect, since consumption could be indulged "more privately" (p. 156).
- Schelsky (reported): a polarization between large firms enriched by "public" substance and groups withdrawn into a constricted, super-private existence, which amounts to a growing split between private and public life (p. 156).
- The family also lost power as an agent of internalization. Paternal authority was levelled, and members were socialized more by extrafamilial authorities. The family, increasingly disengaged from the reproduction of society, kept only the illusion of an inner space (pp. 156–157). Habermas calls this domain *pseudo-privacy* (p. 157).

**Housing, suburbs and cities (pp. 157–159).**
- The hollowing out is visible in architecture. The closed home (front yard, fence, individualized rooms) is no longer the norm, and the salon and reception rooms are disappearing. Loss of the private sphere and of ensured access to the public is typical of today's urban dwelling (p. 157).
- Whyte's American suburb: conformity pressure from neighbours produces "a lay version of Army post life". Doors inside and barriers against neighbours disappear, and thin walls serve communication that is hard to tell from social control (p. 157).
- Privacy has to be produced (Whyte's example of a resident moving his chair to the front of the apartment) (p. 158).
- As private life became public, the public sphere took forms of private closeness. The *neighborhood* is the pre-bourgeois extended family in new guise, and rational-critical debate fell victim to this refeudalization (p. 158).
- Discussion as sociability gave way to a fetishism of community involvement. A quoted author says that even watching television together helps make one more of a real person (p. 158).
- Bahrdt (reported): the traditional urban block allowed both internal division of dwellings and a meaningful city order. Traffic needs have overtaken it. The result gives neither a protected private sphere nor free space for public contact (p. 158).
- Bahrdt: urbanization polarizes social life into public and private, and each depends on the other. Without a protective private sphere the individual is sucked into a public realm that is thereby denatured. If the distance constitutive of the public sphere is eliminated, it turns into a mass (pp. 158–159).
- Bahrdt: the city dweller no longer gets an overview of city life that is "really public" for him, withdraws into an ever-larger privacy, and public space becomes an ill-ordered arena for vehicle traffic (p. 159).
- Habermas concludes that private people who withdraw from property-owner roles into "purely personal" leisure fall directly under semipublic authorities, without an institutionally protected domestic domain. Leisure supplies the key to this floodlit privacy. It tends to take the place of the literary public sphere as reference point of family-formed subjectivity (p. 159).

*Intuition note.* Privacy used to be protected by property and family functions. Once these are gone, home life is exposed to firms, media and neighbours. The "private" that remains is mostly leisure and consumption, where, Habermas says, people come under the influence of semipublic authorities.

### 18. From a culture-debating to a culture-consuming public

**Why the literary public was political and why it collapsed (pp. 159–162).**
- The literary public sphere is replaced by the pseudo-public or sham-private world of culture consumption (p. 160).
- Bourgeois culture was not merely ideology. Debate in salons, clubs and reading societies was not subject to the cycle of production and consumption, and was "political" in the Greek sense of freedom from survival constraints. Only so could the idea later degraded to ideology (humanity) develop (p. 160).
- Equating the property owner with the human being presupposed a separation inside the private realm. On one side stood affairs pursued individually for one's own reproduction, on the other the interaction that united private people into a public. This threshold was levelled when the literary public spread into consumption (p. 160).
- Leisure that merely complements the job is another arena of private affairs and does not itself constitute a public sphere (pp. 160–161).
- When market laws permeated the sphere reserved for private people as a public, debate tended to be replaced by consumption. Public communication broke up into individual acts of reception, even if uniform in mode (p. 161).
- The privacy once oriented to a public as audience became a travesty. A patented culture industry now circulates literary patterns through the mass media and produces in consumers the illusion of bourgeois privacy (p. 161).
- The uncoupling of the intimate sphere from property as capital seemed to allow its idea to be realised, but it created new dependence. Autonomy derived from public status guarantees of privacy could be realised only if citizens controlled those conditions through a political public sphere. This was not to be expected (p. 161).
- Hence there was neither institutional support for individuation on the model of the Protestant Ethic nor an alternative route (political ethics) in sight (pp. 161–162).
- The literary public sphere became a conduit for social forces channelled into the family's inner space through a public sphere transformed by the mass media into a sphere of culture consumption. The mass media hollowed out the inner domain and patched together a pseudo-public sphere that works as a zone of familiarity reaching beyond the family (p. 162).

**Institutions of the reading public weaken (pp. 162–164).**
- Since the mid-19th century the supporting institutions weakened. The family lost its function as a circle of literary propaganda. The *Gartenlaube* imitated the earlier high-bourgeois literary tradition, and family periodicals commercially stabilised a reading culture that had almost become ideology. They still presupposed the family as a sounding board for literature. That presupposition no longer holds (p. 162).
- Avant-garde literary periodicals never sought links with a culturally interested bourgeoisie. Literary family periodicals became obsolete and were replaced by advertiser-financed illustrated magazines (pp. 162–163).
- With the family's loss of link to letters, the salon also declined. Substitutes have one trait in common, abstinence from literary and political debate. Noncommittal group activities replaced convivial discussion, and no public formed around them (p. 163).
- Going together to the cinema or listening to radio or TV lacks the audience-oriented privacy of the reader. Critical debate depended on reading in the closed-off home, while leisure of the consuming public needs no further discussion. Private appropriation removed the ground for communicating about what is appropriated (p. 163).
- Rational debate continues in formally organised, compartmentalised forms (adult education, forums, panel discussions), but it takes on the form of a consumer item (pp. 163–164).
- Earlier, culture was commercialised but the conversation about it was excluded from the market: one paid for books, theatre, concert and museum, but not for the talk about them. Today "the conversation itself is administered" (professional dialogues, panels, round tables) (p. 164).
- Debate bound to prearranged rules makes consensus about the subject superfluous by consensus about form. Problems become questions of etiquette, and conflicts once fought in public polemics are reduced to personal incompatibilities. Such debate serves as a tranquilizing substitute for action and increasingly loses its publicist function (p. 164).

*Intuition note.* Earlier, one paid for books, theatre and concerts but not for the conversation about them. Once the conversation itself is administered and sold as a package, it becomes a consumer item (p. 164).

**The market and the content of culture (pp. 164–167).**
- Earlier, the market let works become autonomous products and people judge them by taste and free choice. Its function was confined to distribution, and exchange value did not yet affect the works' quality (pp. 164–165).
- Now market laws have penetrated the works and become formative laws. Sales strategy guides production. Mass culture raises sales by adapting to the relaxation and entertainment needs of strata with relatively little education, not by guiding a wider public to appreciate a culture unharmed in substance (p. 165).
- Late in the 18th century the educated public expanded to include the self-employed petty bourgeoisie through trade societies and reading societies. The "people" were brought up to the level of culture, and culture was not lowered to that of the masses (pp. 165–166).
- Two market functions must be distinguished: economic easing of access, and adapting content so as to ease access psychologically (Meyersohn speaks of lowering the entrance requirements into leisure). When culture becomes a commodity in content, it is emptied of elements requiring training (p. 166).
- Habermas says commercialization and complexity are inversely related because products are prepared as consumption-ready. Mass-culture consumption leaves no lasting trace, and its experience is not cumulative but regressive (p. 166).
- **Book market.** Paperbacks give high-quality literature to a small stratum of students and readers and keep an exclusively economic easing of access. Their content is undisturbed, so the permanent appears in the guise of the transitory (Kayser). Readers' Circle (*Lesering*) books show the transitory in the guise of the permanent (pp. 166–167).
- Book clubs (formed after the First World War in Anglo-Saxon countries; Habermas says they control the largest market share) cheapen books and cut publisher risk. They bypass the retail trade, reduce consumers' selection, and tie contact with editors to mass taste. This lowers entrance requirements psychologically, so literature is tailored to easy reception (p. 167).
- Noncumulative experience (a social-psychological criterion) goes with the destruction of the public sphere (a sociological one). Book clubs remove fiction from criticism and administer clients outside the literary public sphere, and the weakened role of criticism may be connected (p. 167).
- In West Germany more than a third of potential readers read no books, and more than two-fifths buy none. The replacement of the debating public by a mass of consumers is only inadequately reflected in book-market breadth, since other media carry the process (p. 168).

**The press (pp. 168–170).**
- The first newspaper with a mass edition over 50,000 was Cobbett's *Political Register* (1816), organ of the Chartist movement. The masses were pressed to participate politically but denied the education to do so as bourgeois journal readers did (p. 168).
- The penny press (runs of 100,000 and 200,000 in the early 1830s) and the weekend press supplied "psychological facilitation". Parallel cases are Girardin in Paris and Day's *New York Sun*. Fifty years later came Pulitzer's *New York World* and yellow journalism, reaching nearly a million copies. Cartoons, news pictures and human-interest stories came from the weekly press (p. 168). By the end of the century the "American" mass press was dominant on the continent too (pp. 168–169).
- The mass press commercialised participation in the public sphere. It lost its political character once psychological facilitation became an end in itself for a consumer attitude. The early penny press paid for sales with depoliticized content (p. 169).
- The press that submitted political issues to critical discussion lost influence, and a culture-consuming public inheriting from the literary rather than the political public sphere came to dominate (p. 169).
- In the daily press: layout and illustration ease reading but also restrict spontaneity by serving "ready-made" material. Editorial opinion recedes behind agency reports, and critical debate disappears behind internal selection decisions (p. 169).
- Habermas uses an American categorisation: "delayed reward news" (public affairs, social problems, economics, education, health) is pushed back by "immediate reward news" (comics, corruption, accidents, sports, human interest) and is read less (pp. 169–170).
- News takes narrative form, and the distinction between fact and fiction is often abandoned. Entertainment literature in turn aims at clichéd "realistic" duplication of reality (p. 170).

**The new media (pp. 170–172).**
- Radio, film and television join information and rational-critical argument with belles-lettres under the heading of *human interest*, offering a palatable substitute for reality that favours impersonal indulgence over public use of reason (p. 170).
- They reduce to a minimum the distance a reader keeps to the printed letter, which required private appropriation and made rational-critical exchange possible. The form of communication itself changed, and the media act more penetratingly than the press (p. 170).
- Under the pressure of "Don't talk back" the public's conduct changes. The programmes curtail recipients' reactions. They draw the eyes and ears under their spell but place the public under "tutelage", depriving it of the chance to speak and disagree (pp. 170–171).
- Critical discussion tends to give way to exchanges about tastes and preferences between consumers. The talk about what is consumed becomes part of consumption (p. 171).
- Habermas concludes that the world fashioned by the mass media is a public sphere in appearance only, and the privacy it promises is also an illusion (p. 171).
- In the 18th century, letters and psychological novels cultivated a subjectivity oriented toward a public sphere. The mass media strip these literary forms and use them as marketable forms for human-interest and biographical items, transferring an illusion of untouched private autonomy to conditions that have removed its basis (p. 171).
- Political matters are also personalised. The public sphere is privatized in the consuming public's consciousness and becomes a sphere for publicizing private biographies. Sentimentality toward persons and cynicism toward institutions result, and these curtail subjective capacity for rational criticism of public authority (pp. 171–172).
- Book reading frequency has, Habermas says, decreased rapidly and may be considered an established fact. Letter-writing appears to have fallen at least as much (p. 172).
- Media editors' letter exchanges and advice services partly replace it. The media serve as addressees for personal problems, a "regeneration of the private realm" from public support services. An inner life oriented to a public tends to give way to reifications related to the inner life, and publication heightens the consciousness of privacy, so the media sphere has the traits of a secondary realm of intimacy (p. 172).

*Intuition note.* The 18th-century forms of intimacy (the letter, the novel) oriented people toward a public sphere. The media keep the look of intimacy while removing the practice, so people feel close to others but are not engaged in public debate.

**Who consumes? (pp. 172–175).**
- Habermas rejects a "widespread prejudice" that the old public still continues, to a degree, at its centre (especially the higher ranks of the new middle class) and is overwhelmed only at the periphery by semiliterate masses. If so, consumption habits would spread more among lower strata (pp. 172–173).
- The data say otherwise. Regular reading of weekend magazines, illustrated and boulevard papers, regular radio and television, and cinema going are more prevalent among higher-status groups and city dwellers. Almost without exception such consumption rises with status (occupation, income, schooling) and urbanization (p. 173).
- The public cannot simply be projected backward as if new strata were added at the margin of the bourgeois reading public. Nor did a mass public push out the old public from below (the working class) or outside (the rural population) (p. 173).
- Habermas suggests one may extrapolate to a certain extent from US television, where early buyers had education not matching their income. If a generalization be permitted, the first consumer strata were neither the established educated nor the lower strata but upwardly mobile groups whose status needed cultural legitimation. This trigger group was followed by the higher stratum, and lower-status groups last (pp. 173–174).
- A stratum of "intellectuals" split from the educated bourgeoisie. *Pamela* could be said to have been read by "everyone" who read at all. With naturalism the intimate relation between artists and public loosened, and the left-behind public lost its critical power over producers (p. 174).
- Hauser (quoted) dates the intelligentsia's origin about the mid-19th century. After victory over the Revolution and Chartism's defeat, the bourgeoisie felt secure and felt no need for criticism, so the cultural elite lost its sense of mission and felt isolated (p. 174).
- A century later the intellectuals are socially integrated as well-paid cultural functionaries. The avant-garde remains as an institution, with continuing alienation between specialist minorities and the great public of mass media (pp. 174–175).
- The sounding board of an educated stratum trained in public reason has shattered. The public splits into specialist minorities who use reason nonpublicly, and a mass of consumers whose receptiveness is public but uncritical, lacking the communication form specific to a public (p. 175).

### 19. The blurred blueprint: developmental pathways in the disintegration of the bourgeois public sphere

**Culture of integration and the intermediate sphere (pp. 175–176).**
- The literary public sphere lost its specific character. Mass-media culture integrates information with debate and journalism with literary forms under human interest (entertainment plus "advice"). It also assimilates advertising, and could serve as a super-slogan for public relations for the status quo. The public sphere assumes advertising functions, and the more it is a vehicle for propaganda, the more unpolitical and pseudo-privatized it becomes (p. 175).
- The bourgeois model presupposed strict separation, with the public sphere itself part of the private realm. As the two intermesh, the model becomes inapplicable. In the repoliticized social sphere, society absorbed by the state and state sectors taken over by society intermesh without rational-critical debate by private people (p. 176).
- The public is largely relieved of this task by associations of organised private interests and by parties fused with public authority, which stand "above" the public whose instruments they once were (p. 176).
- Power is now exercised and balanced directly between private bureaucracies, special-interest associations, parties and public administration. The public is included only sporadically, and even then to contribute acclamation (p. 176).
- As wage earners and welfare recipients, people must have claims advocated collectively. As consumers and voters, they make individual decisions that come under the influence of economic and political agencies. There is an interest in influencing them: to raise sales, and to raise a party's votes or the weight of organisations' pressure (p. 176).
- Latitude for private decisions is set by buying power, group membership and status. The more the intimate-sphere/literary-public relation is reversed and privacy undermined through publicity, the more decisions within the latitude can be influenced (pp. 176–177).
- Culture consumption thereby serves economic and political propaganda. The tendency is toward absorption of the plebiscitary "political" public sphere by one depoliticized through culture consumption (p. 177).

**Marx and the mediating function (p. 177).**
- Habermas reads Marx as sharing the perspective of propertyless, uneducated masses who entered the bourgeois public sphere to turn economic conflict into political conflict. In Marx's view, as Habermas puts it, the masses would use the public sphere's platform not to destroy it but to make it what liberal pretense claimed it was (p. 177).
- **Habermas's counter-claim:** in reality the masses' occupation of the political public sphere interlocked state and society. This removed the public sphere's former basis without supplying a new one (p. 177).
- The mediating function passed to institutions arising from the private sphere (associations) or the public sphere (parties). They exercise and balance power with the state apparatus, as an internal matter, and seek via autonomous mass media the agreement or acquiescence of a mediatized public. Publicity is generated "from above" to create an aura of goodwill (p. 177).
- Original publicity linked rational-critical debate to the legislative basis of domination and to critical supervision. Now it permits a domination exercised through the domination of nonpublic opinion, serving manipulation as much as legitimation. "Critical publicity is supplanted by manipulative publicity" (pp. 177–178).

**The law (pp. 178–180).**
- The liberal concept of the legal norm implied universality and truth. Justice equalled rightness (*Richtigkeit*) (p. 178).
- Generality was guaranteed only while society's undisturbed private autonomy excluded special interests and restricted norms to general conditions of compromise. "Truth" was guaranteed only while a parliamentary public sphere could discover through public discussion what was practically necessary in the general interest (p. 178).
- This formal universality guaranteed "truth" as rightness in the material sense of bourgeois class interest. That was part of the dialectic of the concept, rooted in the public sphere's own dialectic (p. 178).
- Once the separation was overcome and government intervened through planning, distribution and administration, the generality of the norm could no longer be maintained as a principle. Regulated matters are concrete, involving specific groups and transitory situations, and laws take on the character of administrative dispositions. The line between general law and specific measure is blurred (pp. 178–179).
- Forsthoff (reported) names three processes undermining the old separation and interlocking of legislature and administration (p. 179):
  - the legislator himself takes implementing steps (specific regulatory measures);
  - the lawmaker delegates law-making to administration (enabling legislation);
  - the legislator, facing a matter needing regulation, sets no norms and gives administration free rein.
- Dissolving the private sphere shook the basis of a relatively homogeneous public of rational-critical private citizens. Competition between organised private interests invaded the public sphere, and the display of competing interests replaced discussion (p. 179).
- Consensus from rational-critical debate gave way to compromise fought out or imposed nonpublicly. Such laws cannot be vindicated as to "truth", though universality is often preserved, because the parliamentary public sphere has collapsed (pp. 179–180).
- A quoted author describes parliamentary speeches as no longer meant to convince differing delegates but aimed directly at the active citizenry, which gives the public sphere a plebiscitary character (p. 180).
- The concept of law was stripped of universality and truth. Since the 1860s in Germany the *double concept of law* has prevailed: "law" in the material sense is any enactment by the proper authorities, and "law" in the formal sense is anything passed by parliamentary procedure. Neither captures the connection of public sphere and rule of law that Kant formulated (p. 180).
- Rational justification of political domination can no longer be expected from the principle of publicity. The mediatized public is called on more often for acclamation, but is too remote from the exercise and balancing of power for rational justification to be demanded or achieved (p. 180).

*Intuition note.* Law earlier claimed to be right because it came from open debate among private people about general interests. When laws are bargains between organised groups, debate cannot supply that claim. The public is then asked only to agree.

## Toy example
*Our own illustration, with invented numbers. It is not Habermas's measure.*

Idea tested: Habermas contrasts critical discussion with "exchanges about tastes and preferences" (p. 171). Suppose we code 20 utterances from each of two evening conversations:
- **R** = gives a reason and answers a reason given earlier;
- **T** = states only a taste or preference;
- **O** = other.

Group A (discussing a novel they read alone):
- R = 12, T = 3, O = 5, so 12 + 3 + 5 = 20.
- Share of R: 12 / 20 = 0.60. Share of T: 3 / 20 = 0.15.

Group B (talking while watching a TV programme together):
- R = 2, T = 14, O = 4, so 2 + 14 + 4 = 20.
- Share of R: 2 / 20 = 0.10. Share of T: 14 / 20 = 0.70.

Reading: Group A's talk looks like the "critical discussion" of a reading public. Group B's looks like talk that has become part of consumption. The gap in R-share is 0.60 − 0.10 = 0.50. Habermas's claim is structural: the media's form discourages answering back (p. 170–171). The numbers only show how one could check that claim.

## Concepts
- [[public-sphere]] — the realm of private people reasoning on general matters. In this chapter it is a part of the private realm that depended on state/society separation (pp. 141–142).
- [[refeudalization]] — societal powers taking on public functions (p. 142); also the new "neighborhood" closeness in which private and public cannot be clearly distinguished (p. 158).
- [[repoliticized-social-sphere]] (new) — the sphere of fused state and society, to which public/private distinctions cannot usefully be applied (p. 142).
- [[societalization-and-state-ification]] (new) — the twin processes of the state adopting social interests and society taking on public functions, which destroyed the basis of the bourgeois public sphere (p. 142).
- [[publification-privatization-of-law]] (new) — private law taking on public elements and public law using private-law forms (pp. 149, 151).
- [[pseudo-privacy]] (new) — the illusion of privacy in a family reduced to a consumer unit and exposed to extrafamilial authorities (pp. 156–157).
- [[culture-consuming-public]] (new) — the successor to the culture-debating public, which receives individually and uncritically; discussion itself becomes a consumer item (pp. 159–164).
- [[psychological-facilitation]] (new) — lowering "entrance requirements" through content adapted to low-education consumers, as distinct from purely economic access (pp. 166, 168–169).
- [[culture-industry]] (new) — the term appears for a "patented" production of literary patterns and the illusion of privacy (p. 161).
- [[manipulative-publicity]] (new) — publicity generated from above to win goodwill or acquiescence, replacing critical publicity (p. 178).
- [[plebiscitary-public-sphere]] (new) — a public addressed for acclamation, with parliament speaking to citizens rather than convincing delegates (pp. 177, 180).
- [[double-concept-of-law]] (new) — the German doctrine separating a material and a formal concept of law, which no longer ties law to publicity and universality (p. 180).

## Bridges (candidates)

## Questions this raises
- Habermas calls his own data suggestive and extrapolates cautiously (US television, pp. 173–174). What would count as evidence for or against the claim that culture consumption rises with status?
- If critical publicity depended on a private sphere protected by property and family functions, can a public sphere be rebuilt without that basis? The chapter says the needed political control over the conditions of private existence was "not to be expected" (p. 161).
- How does the move from general laws to specific administrative measures (pp. 178–179) relate to the claim that parliamentary debate can no longer find "truth" (p. 179)?
- Habermas contrasts his view of the mass public with Marx's expectation (p. 177). What exactly did the masses' entry change that Marx's account missed?
