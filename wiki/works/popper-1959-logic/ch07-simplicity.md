# Simplicity

## L0 — One line
Popper proposes that the epistemologically useful sense of "simplicity" is degree of falsifiability (testability), so simpler theories are prized because they tell us more, have greater empirical content and are better testable.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Philosophers often say we should prefer the "simplest" law or curve, but rarely say what simplicity is or why it is valuable. Popper sets aside aesthetic and practical senses. For the sense that matters to a theory of knowledge, he argues that the questions philosophers have raised about simplicity can all be answered if simplicity is equated with degree of falsifiability. On his account, fewer adjustable parameters go with a lower-dimensional theory, which is more easily falsified and tells us more. This also explains why simple theories are desirable without any "economy of thought". Conventionalist "simplicity", by contrast, is partly aesthetic and partly practical, and, in Popper's view, a theory rescued by ad hoc additions is complex in the highest degree.

**Prerequisites:** curve fitting (a curve through points; a *parameter* is a freely adjustable number in a formula); falsifiability and "dimension of a theory" from earlier chapters; the idea of conventionalism; the angle sum of a triangle.

## L2 — Key ideas
- "Simplicity" is used loosely and uncritically, e.g. by Machians and by Poincaré, who pick the "simplest" convention without saying what that is (pp. 121–122).
- Simplicity of expositions, proofs or tasks is aesthetic or pragmatic and is dropped as extra-logical (p. 122).
- What remains is the epistemological concept: it is expected to measure the degree of law-likeness or regularity of events. Popper finds most attempts to define it unsuccessful: Schlick answers negatively, Natkin's curvature definition misses the crucial point, Wittgenstein gives no reasons, and Weyl discusses and rejects a probability-based attempt (pp. 123–125).
- Popper's thesis: equate simplicity with degree of falsifiability. A first-degree law is more easily falsified than a second-degree one, so it counts as simpler (pp. 126–127).
- Simple statements are prized because they tell more, have greater empirical content and are better testable (p. 128).
- Popper says apparent contradictions (logarithmic and sine laws are called simple although their shapes are not) can be cleared up by tracking parameters, invariance and formal vs. material dimension reduction. The same distinction meets the ellipse–circle objection to Weyl. For Euclidean geometry he argues that simplicity and falsifiability coincide (pp. 128–130).
- On Popper's view, conventionalist "simplicity" is partly aesthetic and partly practical. From his standpoint, a system held fast and rescued by auxiliary hypotheses has falsifiability zero and is complex in the highest degree (p. 131).
- Popper's stance on the word: he does not quarrel about words. He suggests that some assertions about simplicity by great scientists and philosophers can be better understood if they sometimes had testability in mind (pp. 131–132).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 121–122)
Weyl called the problem of simplicity of central importance for the epistemology of natural science, yet it seems that interest in it has declined (p. 121). **Machians' view (Mach, Kirchhoff, Avenarius):** replace causal explanation by the "simplest description". Popper notes this seems to presuppose that theories are simpler than singular statements, which few have tried to explain (p. 121). **Poincaré's view:** theory choice is convention, so choose the simplest convention. Popper asks: "But which are the simplest?" (p. 122).

### 41 Elimination of the aesthetic and pragmatic concepts (p. 122)
"Simplicity" has many senses; Schrödinger's theory is methodologically simple but could be called complex in another sense. Popper excludes the simplicity of presentations, of expositions, of proofs ("more elegant") and of tasks done "by simpler means" (easier, less training). These express aesthetic or pragmatic preferences. They are extra-logical and the word can be eliminated (p. 122).

### 42 The methodological problem of simplicity (pp. 122–125)
**Question:** is there a concept of simplicity important to the logician, one that ranks logically non-equivalent theories? (p. 122)
- **Schlick (negative answer):** simplicity indicates preferences partly practical, partly aesthetic. Yet a scientist who fits observations with a very simple formula is at once convinced he has found a law. Schlick calls simplicity "wholly relative and vague", so it cannot sharply separate law from chance (p. 123).
- Popper's reading: simplicity is expected to measure degree of law-likeness or regularity. Feigl voices a similar view (p. 123).
- **Simplest-curve problem (inductive logic):** through finitely many points one can draw unlimited curves, so observations do not fix the law. The usual advice is to choose the simplest. **Wittgenstein:** induction is assuming the simplest law that harmonizes with experience (p. 124).
- Popper's criticism: the hierarchy (line simpler than parabola, circle than ellipse) is assumed tacitly. No reason is given for that hierarchy, nor for thinking simple laws better apart from aesthetic and practical reasons (p. 124).
- **Natkin (via Schlick and Feigl):** a curve is simpler if its average curvature is smaller (Schlick's account), or if it deviates less from a straight line (Feigl's); the two accounts are not equivalent. Popper: this seems to fit intuition but somehow misses the crucial point. It would make asymptotic parts of a hyperbola much simpler than a circle, and it gives no reason to prefer simplicity so defined (p. 124).
- **Attempt to base simplicity on probability (Weyl discusses and rejects it):** twenty points lying on a straight line suggest a linear law, because of the line's simplicity or because it would be extremely improbable for arbitrary observations to lie near a line if the law were different. But for any other function satisfying the twenty points the same could be claimed. So the class of functions must be offered a priori by mathematics because of its mathematical simplicity, and must not have as many parameters as there are observations (pp. 124–125).
- Popper agrees with Weyl's emphasis on a priori class and parameters (to be developed in §43). He objects that Weyl does not say what "mathematical simplicity" is, nor what logical or epistemological advantage the simpler law has (p. 125). A footnote credits Jeffreys and Wrinch (1921) with measuring simplicity by paucity of adjustable parameters (p. 125, n. 7).
- Since the epistemological concept is not yet precise, Popper's precisification may be rejected as being about a different concept. He replies that he attaches no importance to the word. His concept helps answer the questions philosophers have raised under this heading (pp. 125–126).

*Intuition note.* Many writers expect "simple" to measure how law-like a hypothesis is, but few had explained what it means or why it is valuable, and attempts to define it were mostly unsuccessful. Popper's task is to give a precise meaning that does the job people expect.

### 43 Simplicity and degree of falsifiability (pp. 126–128)
**Thesis:** the epistemological questions about simplicity can all be answered if simplicity is equated with degree of falsifiability (p. 126).
- Theories of lower dimension are more easily falsifiable (shown earlier). A first-degree function is more easily falsified than a second-degree one, yet the latter is still among the best-falsifiable algebraic laws. This matches Schlick's remark that a first-degree function seems simpler though the second-degree one is also a good law (p. 127).
- Universality and precision rise with falsifiability. So Popper says we may *perhaps* identify a theory's strictness with its degree of falsifiability, which does what Schlick and Feigl expected of simplicity (p. 127).
- Law vs. chance: Popper says probability statements about chance-like sequences are of infinite dimension, hence complex rather than simple. They are falsifiable only under special safeguards (p. 127; he cites sections outside this unit).
- A more universal statement replaces many less universal ones and is therefore often called simpler. The dimension of a theory may be said to make Weyl's parameter idea precise (pp. 127–128).
- Formal vs. material reduction of dimension (§40) meets one objection to Weyl. Ellipses with given axis ratio and given eccentricity have as many parameters as circles, yet are obviously less simple (p. 128).
- **Why simplicity is desirable:** no "principle of economy of thought" is needed. Simple statements are prized because they tell more, have greater empirical content and are better testable (p. 128).
- **Note \*1 (later addition):** Kneale wrote that the simplest hypothesis is the one we can hope to eliminate most quickly if false. Popper finds no such view in Weyl. Weyl rejected the "improbable accident" argument because the same holds for any curve. Popper says this is correct, but it no longer holds if we consider potential falsifiers and their degree of composition instead of verifying instances (p. 126, n. \*1). **Jeffreys's view** gives the simpler law greater prior probability. Popper's gives it greater prior improbability (p. 127, n. \*1).
- **Note \*2, two schemata:** Jeffreys–Wrinch: simplicity = paucity of parameters = high prior probability. Popper: testability = high prior improbability = paucity of parameters = simplicity. They coincide in part but are in direct opposition on probability vs. improbability (p. 128, n. \*2).

*Intuition note.* A law with few free numbers is compatible with fewer possible results, so more results could refute it. On Popper's view this is why it counts as "simple" and also tells us more.

### 44 Geometrical shape and functional form (pp. 128–129)
A logarithmic curve's shape is not particularly simple, yet a logarithmic law is usually called simple. The same holds for the sine, whose shape is "perhaps not so very simple" (pp. 128–129). Resolution (p. 129): keep in mind the link between number of parameters and falsifiability, distinguish formal from material reduction of dimensions, and remember the role of invariance: the number of parameters depends on which transformations the description must be invariant under.
- For a geometrical *shape*, we demand invariance under displacements (and possibly similarity transformations), since a figure is not tied to a position. Then the one-parametric curve $y=\log_a x$, lying anywhere in a plane, has five parameters (allowing similarity transformations). It is not particularly simple (p. 129).
- For a *law* with non-interchangeable axes (e.g. pressure on $x$, height above sea level on $y$), rotations, displacements and similarity transformations are irrelevant. The same holds for sine oscillations along the time axis (p. 129).

### 45 The simplicity of Euclidean geometry (pp. 129–130)
Euclidean geometry is held simpler than any non-Euclidean geometry of constant curvature (p. 129). Formulated as an empirical hypothesis, it is also more falsifiable. This requires identifying geometrical entities with physical ones, e.g. straight lines with light rays (p. 130).
- Any significant deviation of a light-ray triangle's angle sum from 180° falsifies the Euclidean hypothesis (p. 130).
- A Bolyai–Lobatschewski hypothesis with given curvature is compatible with any measurement not exceeding 180°. Falsifying it also needs the triangle's absolute size, hence a further unit such as area. It needs more measurements, tolerates more variation, and is falsifiable to a lesser degree (p. 130).
- Euclidean geometry is the only metric geometry of definite curvature allowing similarity transformations. Its figures are invariant under more transformations, so they can be of lower dimension (p. 130).

### 46 Conventionalism and the concept of simplicity (pp. 130–131)
Popper agrees with the conventionalist that no theory is unambiguously determined by experience (p. 131). But conventionalists treat theories as stipulations, not falsifiable systems, so their "simplicity" differs from degree of falsifiability. It is partly aesthetic and partly practical. Schlick's remark that simplicity can only be defined by an arbitrary convention applies to it, not to Popper's concept (p. 131). Popper says conventionalists must have overlooked the conventional character of their own concept of simplicity; otherwise they would have noticed that their appeal to simplicity could never save them from arbitrariness, once they had chosen arbitrary convention (p. 131). In Popper's view, a system held fast as established forever and rescued by auxiliary hypotheses whenever endangered has falsifiability zero and is complex in the highest degree. This leads back to the methodological rules of §20 and to the principle of parsimony in the use of hypotheses (p. 131).

### Addendum, 1972 (pp. 131–132)
Nothing depends on the word "simplicity", and Popper did not seek its essence. In this chapter he tried to show how far degrees of simplicity can be identified with degrees of testability. He suggests that some assertions about simplicity by great scientists and philosophers can be better understood if, when speaking about it, they sometimes had testability in mind. This clarifies even some of Poincaré's examples, though it clashes with his views (pp. 131–132). Two further points: (1) theories can be compared in testability only if at least some of the problems they are supposed to solve coincide; (2) ad hoc hypotheses cannot be compared this way (p. 132).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Compare the linear law $y=ax+b$ (2 parameters) with the quadratic law $y=ax^2+bx+c$ (3 parameters). Observations: $(0,1),(1,3),(2,6)$.
- Linear: points 1 and 2 give $b=1$, $a=3-1=2$, so $y=2x+1$. It predicts $2(2)+1=5$ at $x=2$, but 6 is observed. Three points falsify it.
- Quadratic: $c=1$. From $(1,3)$: $a+b+1=3$, so $a+b=2$. From $(2,6)$: $4a+2b+1=6$, so $2a+b=2.5$. Subtract: $a=0.5$, $b=1.5$. It fits all three, so three points could not have falsified it.
- A fourth point $(3,11)$: the quadratic predicts $0.5\cdot 9+1.5\cdot 3+1=10\neq 11$, so it is falsified.

The law with fewer parameters is refuted by fewer observations: higher falsifiability, i.e. "simpler" in Popper's sense (p. 127).

## Concepts
- [[simplicity-epistemological]] (new) — simplicity as the hoped-for measure of degree of law-likeness or regularity. Popper equates it with degree of falsifiability (pp. 123, 126)
- [[falsifiability]] — the possibility of refuting a theory by observation (defined in earlier chapters, not on these pages). Here it comes in degrees (p. 126)
- [[degree-of-falsifiability]] (new) — how easily a theory can be refuted; goes together with degree of universality and precision, and is higher for lower dimension (pp. 126–127)
- [[dimension-of-a-theory]] (new) — tied to the number of freely adjustable parameters (the dimension concept gives precision to Weyl's parameter idea). Lower dimension means more easily falsified (pp. 127–128)
- [[simplest-curve-problem]] (new) — which curve to choose through finitely many data points (p. 123)
- [[conventionalism]] (new) — theories are conventions chosen for "simplicity". Popper says that this is partly aesthetic and partly practical (pp. 122, 131)
- [[ad-hoc-hypothesis]] (new) — auxiliary hypothesis added to rescue a theory. A system held fast and always rescued this way has falsifiability zero (p. 131)
- [[underdetermination]] — experience does not fix a unique theory. Popper agrees on this point (pp. 123, 131)
- [[logical-improbability]] (new) — prior improbability. Popper ties it to high testability, against Jeffreys (p. 128, n. \*2)

## Canonical instances
- [[curve-through-points]] — unlimited curves fit finite observations (p. 123)
- [[ellipse-vs-circle-parameters]] — equal parameter counts but unequal simplicity; an objection to Weyl that the formal vs. material reduction distinction can meet (p. 128)
- [[logarithmic-and-sine-laws]] — simple as laws, not as shapes (pp. 128–129)
- [[euclidean-vs-hyperbolic-triangle-test]] — angle-sum test: as a light-ray hypothesis, Euclidean geometry is more falsifiable than the Bolyai–Lobatschewski alternative (p. 130)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Paucity of parameters as simplicity ↔ statistics: model complexity and overfitting. A model with as many parameters as data points can fit anything (cf. Weyl, p. 125).
- [structural-analogy] Jeffreys: simpler means higher prior probability ↔ Bayesian confirmation: Popper takes the opposite stance, simpler means higher prior improbability (p. 128, n. \*2).
- [metaphor] Simplicity as "tells more" ↔ information: more content, less probability.

## Questions this raises
- Does "fewer parameters = more falsifiable" survive when the curve families are not nested? The text compares only some families.
- If theories can be compared in testability only when some of their problems coincide (p. 132), how are theories from different domains compared?
- Popper rejects "economy of thought". Is "tells more" fully independent of aesthetic or practical preference?
- How should one choose between Jeffreys's prior probability and Popper's prior improbability for simpler laws?
