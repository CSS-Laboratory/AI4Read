# Some Observations on Quantum Theory

## L0 — One line
Popper argues that Heisenberg's formulae are statistical scatter relations that do not forbid exact single measurements; reading them as limits on precision is an extra assumption, and he traces the rise of indeterminism to a misunderstanding of formally singular probability statements.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Popper asks whether quantum theory forbids exact measurements of position and momentum. He answers that Heisenberg's formulae follow from the theory, but their reading as limits on precision does not. Read statistically, they say that selecting particles by position makes their momenta scatter. Testing that claim needs exact single measurements. Popper blames the confusion on mixing up statistical and "formally singular" probability statements. He also argues that indeterminism is a metaphysical doctrine: successful frequency statements do not entitle us to it, and no empirical hypothesis could compel us to stop searching for laws. Later starred notes withdraw his section 77 "decisive experiment" and revise some views. They keep section 76 as important.

**Prerequisites:** probability as relative frequency in a class; the idea of a single-event probability (Popper's section 71, not in this unit); the uncertainty relation Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π; falsifiability.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Heisenberg's programme was to remove unobservables from atomic theory. Popper says it failed: the formalism still computes exact paths between two measurements. Heisenberg treats their reality as a matter of personal belief, and Schlick calls them senseless (pp. 211–216).
- The Heisenberg formulae are derivable from the statistically interpreted wave equation. Their reading as limits on measurement precision is a separate, additional assumption (pp. 218–219).
- Objective reading: the *statistical scatter relations*. Physically selecting particles by position makes momenta scatter, and vice versa. No aggregate is more homogeneous than a "pure case" (pp. 220–223).
- Popper says the belief that a single measurement can contradict a probability statement about a single event seems logically untenable (perhaps except for probabilities 1 or 0). So he holds that alleged proofs that exact measurements contradict the theory show only an apparent contradiction (p. 224).
- Exact "non-predictive" measurements of position-cum-momentum are indispensable: without them the statistical scatter predictions could not be tested. The theory would be metaphysical if they were impossible (pp. 226–227).
- Attributing the uncertainty of a single-event probability directly to the particle fails, and reading the Heisenberg formulae directly as uncertainty of our knowledge leads to the subjectivism of Jeans. Popper treats the wave-packet reduction puzzle as a mere change of reference class (pp. 229–232).
- Section 77's imaginary experiment is retracted in Popper's later notes, which call it based on a mistake. Section 76 is still endorsed (pp. 211, 232, 238).
- Indeterminism and determinism are both metaphysical. No empirical hypothesis could compel us to stop searching for laws. Prohibitions should be falsifiable laws, not barriers to research (pp. 243–247).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 209–211)
Popper wants to use his analysis of probability on "obscure points" of quantum theory. He admits that physicists will be suspicious of a logical approach. He notes that quantum physicists themselves take part in epistemological debates, which may suggest that they feel some unsolved problems lie in the no-man's-land between logic and physics (p. 209).

He lists four conclusions, which he says he will try to show (pp. 209–210):
1. Heisenberg's formulae are to be read as formally singular probability statements, hence statistically. They then assert relations between ranges of statistical scatter ("statistical scatter relations").
2. Measurements more precise than the uncertainty principle allows are not incompatible with the formulae or their statistical interpretation. Quantum theory would not necessarily be refuted by them.
3. The limit on attainable precision is therefore not a logical consequence of the formulae. It is a separate, additional assumption.
4. This assumption actually contradicts the statistically interpreted formulae. Imaginary experiments can be described that show more exact measurements are possible. Popper holds that this contradiction causes the difficulties of quantum physics.

He accepts the mathematical formulae and Born's physical interpretation. His target is the indeterminist metaphysic, which he calls more sterile than the earlier determinist one (p. 211). He admires the creators of the theory (p. 211).

*Later note (p. 211, n.\*1):* Popper has since changed his interpretation of quantum theory and of probability. He now argues for indeterminism independently of quantum theory (Postscript). Section 77 rests on a mistake, he says. He still regards the chapter as important, especially section 76.

### 73. Heisenberg's programme and the uncertainty relations (pp. 211–216)
**The programme.** Heisenberg began from an epistemological programme: remove "unobservables", i.e. magnitudes inaccessible to experiment. Bohr's electron orbits and revolution frequencies were such magnitudes (pp. 211–212). Popper compares this with Einstein's removal of the unobservable ether motions from the Lorentz–Fitzgerald contraction hypothesis (p. 212). Both older theories assumed that nature hides some events from us for ever (p. 212).

**The uncertainty principle (as Popper reports Heisenberg).** Every measurement exchanges energy between object and apparatus and changes the object's state. This matters for atomic objects but can be neglected for macroscopic ones. So a measurement cannot give the state just after it, and cannot serve as a basis for prediction (pp. 212–213). One can arrange not to disturb one magnitude, such as momentum, but only by disturbing a correlated one, such as position, more. Two such magnitudes cannot be measured simultaneously with precision, though each can be measured separately (p. 213). The limit is

$$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ \geq\ \frac{h}{4\pi},$$

with similar relations for the other coordinates. Here $h$ is Planck's quantum of action (p. 213). Popper adds that it is then in principle impossible to predict a path; he quotes March to the effect that in the new mechanics the concept of a path has no definite meaning (p. 214).

*Intuition note.* The claim is that sharpening a position measurement makes momentum less precise, and the reverse. The product of the two error ranges has a lower bound.

**First difficulty.** The relations apply only to magnitudes *after* a measurement. Position and momentum *before* it can in principle be found with unlimited precision by combining measurements (p. 214):
- (a) two position measurements;
- (b) a position measurement preceded by a momentum measurement;
- (c) a position measurement followed by a momentum measurement.

These calculations give the exact path between the two measurements. For Heisenberg they are useless for prediction and untestable, because any test disturbs the path. He says that whether to attribute physical reality to the calculated past is a purely personal matter (p. 214). Schlick says an unverifiable statement about an electron's position has no sense, so one cannot speak of a "path" between observations (pp. 214–215).

Popper replies that the formalism nevertheless calculates this "senseless" path, so Heisenberg's programme has failed. Only two readings are possible (p. 215):
- *Subjective:* the particle has exact position and momentum, but we cannot measure both together. Then nature still hides the "path".
- *Objective:* the particle has no path, only exact position with inexact momentum, or the reverse. Then the formalism still contains the metaphysical path, since it can be calculated for periods when it cannot be observed.

Popper says defenders vacillate between the two, and cites Schlick, who follows the objective statement with a subjective remark (pp. 215–216). Heisenberg's remark that a sharp division of the world into object and subject is no longer possible fuses the two readings without gain, Popper says (p. 216). Popper's verdict: Heisenberg has not purged quantum theory of its metaphysical elements (p. 216).

*Notes:* In n.4 (p. 214) Popper now calls his claim about the electron's past before the first measurement mistaken, like section 77. The word "smeared" is Schrödinger's. Popper believes the question of a smeared versus merely unknown path is fundamental (p. 216, n.\*1).

### 74. Outline of the statistical interpretation (pp. 216–218)
Quantum theory developed along two roads. Heisenberg started from the particle theory. Schrödinger started from de Broglie's wave theory and gave each electron a wave-packet, a group of oscillations that reinforce each other in a small region. Schrödinger showed that the two theories are mathematically equivalent (pp. 216–217).

Born resolved the paradox of particle versus wave. The wave equation gives the *probability of finding the particle in a region*, determined by the square of the wave amplitude (p. 217). Several features of the problem suggested a statistical reading. One is Einstein's photon hypothesis, which treats light effects as mass phenomena. Another is that atomic experiments yield only statistical questions and answers (p. 217).

Quantum formulae differ from classical mechanics only for atomic processes. Classical laws hold as relations between statistical averages, i.e. as macro-laws (p. 217).

Some expositions (Dirac, March) explain the statistical interpretation by the limits on precision set by the uncertainty relations. Popper rejects this. The logical relation is the reverse: the uncertainty formulae can be derived from Schrödinger's statistically interpreted wave equation, but not the equation from the formulae. So the interpretation of the formulae must be revised (p. 218).

### 75. A statistical re-interpretation of the uncertainty formulae (pp. 218–224)
**Formulae versus interpretation.** Since Heisenberg it has been held that measurements beyond his limit would contradict quantum theory. Popper calls this false (p. 219). The formulae $\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \ge h/4\pi$ are logical conclusions of the theory; Weyl gives a strict deduction (p. 219, n.2). Their reading as limits on measurement precision does not follow. So more exact measurements cannot logically contradict the theory. Popper distinguishes the "Heisenberg formulae" from their "uncertainty relation" interpretation (p. 219).

He notes that no proof of the impossibility of such measurements can ever be furnished. At best the principle could be deduced from the hypotheses of quantum mechanics and refuted empirically together with them. He directs this at Jeans (p. 219, n.1).

**Statistical argument.** The derivation uses the wave equation, which can be read statistically. A wave-packet describing one particle is then a *formally singular probability statement* (cf. section 71). That is a probability statement about a single particle or event; the amplitude gives the probability of detecting it at a place (p. 219). Statements derived from such statements, like the Heisenberg formulae, must also be read as probability statements, and ultimately as statistical assertions (pp. 219–220).

**Objective statistical interpretation (Popper's proposal).** It is offered as the fundamental reading (p. 220):
- Take an aggregate of particles and physically select those with position $x$ to precision $\Delta x$. Their momenta $p_x$ scatter randomly. The range $\Delta p_x$ is greater the smaller $\Delta x$ is.
- Select those with momenta in a range $\Delta p_x$. Their positions scatter in a range $\Delta x$ that is greater the smaller $\Delta p_x$ is.
- A physical selection having both properties can be carried out only if $\Delta x\cdot\Delta p_x \ge h/4\pi$.

These are the "statistical scatter relations" (p. 220).

*Later note (n.\*1, p. 220):* Popper keeps the objective interpretation. He now says that talk of an aggregate of particles should be replaced by talk of an aggregate or sequence of repetitions of an experiment with one particle or one system.

**Physical selection versus measurement** (pp. 221–222). A *physical selection* physically separates particles, e.g. a screen with a slit passes only those within $\Delta x$. It is contrasted with a merely imagined selection, such as the class of all particles that pass through some range $\Delta p$ without being screened off. Every physical selection can serve as a measurement (here "measurement" includes indirect measurement by calculation, n.4). But not every measurement is a physical selection. Example: a Geiger counter records arrivals of a monochromatic electron ray. Time intervals between impacts measure positions in the $x$ direction, but without selecting particles by position (pp. 221–222).

**Physical meaning of the scatter relations** (p. 222). One cannot make an aggregate more homogeneous without limit. A monochromatic ray has equal momenta. Screening it through a narrow slit increases the scatter of $p_x$ as the slit narrows. Conversely, making a slit-selected ray parallel and monochromatic destroys the position selection; in the ideal case the width would be infinite. A selection as homogeneous as the formulae permit, with equality, is a **pure case** (term from Weyl and von Neumann, n.5). The scatter relations then say: there is no aggregate of particles more homogeneous than a pure case (pp. 222–223).

*Intuition note.* Popper reads the Heisenberg formula as a law about distributions of many results, not as a ban on knowing one particle's values. It says how scattered the momenta of a position-selected group must be.

**Against Weyl and March.** The mathematical derivation of the formulae from the equations must be matched by a derivation of their interpretation from the interpretation of the equations. March gets this backwards. Weyl derives the formulae from a statistically read wave equation yet reads them as precision limits. He also says Born's interpretation needs a "correction" (p. 223). Popper agrees that a conflict exists, but says it is sharper than Weyl thinks. The limit on precision cannot be derived from the statistical wave equation. Popper says he has still to demonstrate that neither the possible experiments nor the actual results agree with Heisenberg's interpretation; that fact, he says, can then be regarded as a decisive argument, a kind of *experimentum crucis*, for the statistical interpretation (pp. 223–224). (This promised demonstration is in section 77, which a later note withdraws.)

### 76. Inverting Heisenberg's programme; applications (pp. 224–232)
**Single measurements cannot contradict statistical laws.** If the formulae peculiar to quantum theory are probability hypotheses, prohibitions of single events are hard to deduce from them, except perhaps for probabilities 1 or 0. The belief that single measurements can contradict them seems logically untenable, just as untenable as the belief that one could find a contradiction between $_\alpha P_k(\beta)=p$ (say, that throw $k$ is a five has probability 1/6) and the facts $k\in\beta$ ("a five") or $k\in\bar\beta$ ("not a five") (p. 224).

So any alleged proof that exact position and momentum measurements contradict the theory must use formally singular probability statements. Translated "word for word" into statistical language, it shows no contradiction, only an apparent one (p. 224). Appendix v gives an example.

**What is true.** It is wrong that the theory *rules out* exact measurements. It is right that, if the formulae are read statistically, no precise *singular predictions* can be derived from the formulae peculiar to the theory. (Conservation of energy and of momentum are not counted among them.) The reason is that, by the scatter relations, we cannot produce precise initial conditions by physical selection (pp. 224–225). Popper's theorem: from quantum theory we obtain only frequency predictions, not singular ones. It holds only for the "constructive" technique of producing initial conditions (p. 225). This accounts for Heisenberg's and Bohr's imaginary experiments: statistical scatter makes the path after measurement unpredictable (p. 225).

Popper says his view and Heisenberg's are "diametrically opposed", not a quarrel about words (p. 225).

**The difficulty resolved.** Experiments (a), (b), (c) of section 73 can be described statistically. Example for (c): a slit selects particles by position. Then the momentum of those travelling in a definite direction is measured. This fixes the path between the two measurements (p. 226).

On Popper's interpretation these measurements are not superfluous. They do not give initial conditions, but they are indispensable for testing the statistical predictions. The scatter relations assert that momenta scatter when positions are sharply fixed. That is testable only if one can measure and calculate the individual scattered momenta just after the selection (p. 226).

*Later note (n.\*1, p. 226):* Popper still fully agrees with this paragraph. The single momenta become "unpredictable" in a sense that lets us predict that they will scatter. Their measurement can be as precise as we like, and calculating the particle's past back to the slit is essential for claiming a test. Only the precision of the prediction becomes blurred, never the precision of a measurement (pp. 226–227).

So the statistically interpreted theory does not rule out exact single measurements. It would be untestable, hence "metaphysical", if they were impossible. Popper fulfils Heisenberg's aim by the opposite method: the formalism is correct *because* its magnitudes are not metaphysical. The scatter relations are frequency predictions about paths. Paths must therefore be measurable, as throws of five must be ascertainable, if we are to test the frequencies (p. 227).

*Intuition note.* To check a claim that results scatter, you must record the individual results accurately. So exact single measurements are part of what makes the statistical theory empirical.

**Positivist influence and the concept of path** (pp. 227–229).
- **March's view (as quoted):** for the physicist a body has reality only when observed (p. 227).
- **Popper's reply:** the hypothesis that an unobserved body moves along a path is unverifiable but falsifiable. From it we predict where the body will be observed, and that prediction can be refuted (p. 228).
- **Schlick and Bohr:** they hold that spatio-temporal concepts fail at atomic dimensions (p. 228).
- **Popper's reply:** the scatter relations are statements about the scatter of position-cum-momentum, i.e. about paths. The "surreptitious" use of spatio-temporal concepts in practice is therefore justifiable (pp. 228–229).

*Later note (n.\*2, p. 228):* Popper inserted a clause saying that what he has said here is sufficient, because he no longer believes in the argument of section 77. The point rests on the need for calculated past paths in testing the statistical predictions, which is independent of section 77.

**Subjective versus objective readings** (pp. 229–230). Every formally singular probability statement can also be read subjectively, as an indefinite prediction about our knowledge. The attempt to read it objectively fails if one substitutes a *singular* objective reading, attributing the uncertainty directly to the single event (p. 229). Reading the Heisenberg formulae subjectively endangers physics as an objective science.

- **Jeans' view:** the wave picture represents not objective nature but only our knowledge of nature. Schrödinger's waves are then subjective probability waves (p. 229).
- **Popper's reply:** this lets subjectivist probability theory invade physics, so the arguments he rejected in section 62, such as Bernoulli's theorem as a "bridge" from ignorance to statistics, become unavoidable. Quantum statements must be inter-subjectively testable like any physical statement. His analysis preserves objectivity and spatio-temporal description (pp. 229–230).

Schrödinger's own singular objective interpretation identified the particle with the wave-packet. This forced a "smeared" electron charge, incompatible with the atomic structure of electricity. Born's statistical interpretation solved this, but the link between the two readings stayed obscure. So the peculiar character of other formally singular statements, like the uncertainty relations, went unrecognized (p. 230).

*Later note (n.\*3, p. 229):* Popper has changed his mind on the singular objective reading. Schrödinger's theory may now be interpreted as objective and singular and also probabilistic. His main argument for an objective interpretation stands.

**Einstein's semi-translucent mirror** (pp. 231–232). A photon $k$ meets a mirror that reflects part of the light. Let $\beta$ be "passes through" and $\bar\beta$ "reflected". Then

$$ {}_\alpha P_k(\beta) = {}_\alpha P_k(\bar\beta) = \tfrac12 .$$

This is equivalent to the hypothesis that half of the class $\alpha$ of photons pass and half are reflected. If we then find that photon $k$ was reflected, the probabilities seem suddenly to become 0 and 1. Popper says this is the same as his section 71 example. The original probabilities stay $1/2$. All that changes is the *reference class*: $\bar\beta$ replaces $\alpha$. Within the new class, $\bar\beta P(\beta)=0$ and $\bar\beta P(\bar\beta)=1$ (n.\*5, p. 231).

Heisenberg describes a reduction of wave packets as a physical action spreading faster than light from one half of the packet to the other. Popper says this adds nothing. Saying the logical consequences of choosing a new reference class spread faster than light is as helpful as saying that twice two turns into four with super-luminal velocity. The fact that no signals can be sent this way, which Heisenberg adds, does not help either (p. 232). The lesson is that the statistical and formally singular probability concepts must be distinguished, and that interpretation problems of quantum theory need a logical analysis of probability statements (p. 232).

*Later note (n.\*4, p. 231):* this is now a famous problem, the discontinuous reduction of the wave packet. Some leading physicists told Popper in 1934 that they agreed with his trivial solution, yet the problem still bewilders discussion.

### 77. Decisive experiments (pp. 232–242)
*Later note (n.\*\*, p. 232):* Popper has withdrawn the imaginary experiment of this section (pp. 238–242), which he says is based on a mistake. The mistake was first noted by von Weizsäcker, by Heisenberg and by Einstein. Popper no longer calls it "decisive". His arguments up to p. 238 are unaffected. He says the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen experiment can replace it. *The material below reports the original argument.*

**Points (1) and (2) are done:** the formulae can be read statistically, and the precision-limit reading does not follow, so exact measurements cannot contradict the theory (pp. 232–233; point (3) also covered, n.\*1, p. 233).

**Objection (Popper's imagined opponent).** The real core of Heisenberg's theory is the impossibility of exact singular predictions (p. 233). Example: a short shutter selects a group of electrons by position, and momenta scatter widely. We test this by measuring single momenta, e.g. by exciting atoms in a plate, so we know both position and momentum. But any such measurement disturbs the system. The theory would not be logically contradicted if we knew the momenta before disturbing them (so long as this gave no way to effect a forbidden selection), but there is no way to know them without disturbing (pp. 233–234). The opponent adds: an apparatus that measures single momenta could also produce aggregates limited in position and with equal momentum, which the scatter relations forbid. So measurement without selection is "logically permissible" but "my instincts revolt" (p. 234).

**Popper's reply** (pp. 234–235):
- A statistical theory never yields exact singular predictions, only "indefinite" (formally singular) ones. But it does not rule them out either.
- No strict proof has been given that a possible predictive measurement implies a possible corresponding physical selection. The claim that exact single predictions are impossible is *equivalent* to the hypothesis that predictive measurements and physical selections are inseparably linked (the "hypothesis of linkage"). This is an additional hypothesis.
- Point (4) remains: to show that statistically interpreted quantum theory (with conservation laws) plus the linkage hypothesis is self-contradictory. The physical considerations to follow are not premises of the logical analysis, which is independent of them (p. 235).

*Intuition note.* The disagreement is narrowed to one extra assumption: that every measurement good for prediction could also be turned into a way of producing the state. Popper suspects this is a deep-seated presumption, and says no strict proof of it has been given.

**Simpler experiments** (pp. 235–237). These concern all particles in a small space-time region ($\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\,\Delta t$), not definite single particles.
- A monochromatic beam in the $x$ direction has known momentum, with transverse components zero. We *imagine* a group at position $x$ within $\Delta x$ by focusing attention, with no physical selection. We then know where it will be later, and a photographic plate tests this. Only a *separate* physical existence of this group would contradict the theory (p. 236).
- A beam passed through a small slit $\Delta y$: for one definite direction after the slit, the momentum component of the particles that went that way is calculable. Position and momentum, hence paths, are predictable and testable (pp. 236–237).
- A group selected by position in the direction of travel spreads, and the packet widens. The momentum of an imagined sub-group at a given position is greater the farther ahead it is. A moving film strip and a filter or a transverse electric field test this prediction (p. 237).

The precision of these tests is not limited by the uncertainty relations. Those are meant to apply mainly to "predictive" measurements, not to "non-predictive" ones. The filter case is type (b), a momentum selection followed by a position measurement (pp. 237–238).

*Later note (n.\*3, p. 238):* Popper's claim that case (b) allows calculating the path *before* the first measurement is false (criticized by Einstein). Non-predictive measurements fix the path only *between* two measurements. The last paragraph of appendix vi is mistaken.

**The imaginary experiment (withdrawn)** (pp. 238–242). Popper says that Bohr and Heisenberg tried to justify the precision-limit interpretation by showing that no imaginary experiment can give more exact predictive measurements, but this method cannot exclude that one might be devised later (p. 239). His own experiment is built on the Compton–Simon and Bothe–Geiger experiments, using the non-statistical conservation laws of energy and momentum (p. 239).

Setup (Figure 2, pp. 239–241):
- Two beams cross. Beam $A$ is monochromatic, a selection by momentum $\mathbf a_1$. Beam $B$ passes a narrow slit $Sl$, a physical selection by position, with momentum $\mathbf b_1$.
- Imagine narrow partial rays $[A]$ and $[B]$ meeting at $P$. Choose a direction $PX$. For $[A]$-particles deflected towards $X$, the conservation laws let us calculate $\mathbf a_2$ and also $\mathbf b_2$, the momentum of the particle they collided with.
- Each particle recorded at $X$ tells us that a second particle travels from $P$ towards $Y$ with momentum $\mathbf b_2$. From the impact time and known velocity we also learn when the collision at $P$ happened. A counter at $Y$ can test this.

Popper claimed that the precision of these predictions and tests is, in principle, not limited by the uncertainty principle. Predictions about the $B$-particle reduce to measurements at $X$, which can be made as precise as desired (pp. 241–242). Such precise single predictions can be made only if we can gain knowledge of the particle's state without being able to create that state at will, so that we learn it after the event. The impacts at $X$ come at irregular intervals, and making them regular would contradict the theory (p. 242). He used a gun image: we can aim and fix the force of the bullet, but cannot choose the moment of firing (p. 242). He then concluded that the experiment and Heisenberg's interpretation are incompatible, and that, if it can be carried out, it can be regarded as a kind of *experimentum crucis* (p. 242).

*Later note on EPR (n.\*4, p. 241):* Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen use a weaker but valid argument. If Heisenberg's interpretation is right, only position or momentum of the first particle at $X$ can be measured at will. Either choice, even made after the collision, yields the second particle's position or its momentum. It is unreasonable to think that the choice disturbed the second particle. So it "has" both a precise position and a precise momentum.

### 78. Indeterminist metaphysics (pp. 243–247)
**Two tasks are compatible** (p. 243). A scientist seeks laws for single predictions ("causal" or "precision statements") and also frequency hypotheses. They are not mutually incompatible. Some precision statements are macro-laws derivable from frequency assumptions. Well-confirmed frequency statements in a field do not entitle us to conclude that no precision statements are possible there. Popper says the opposite belief, that where fortuity rules regularity is excluded, has been drawn again and again (and refers to section 69).

**Reduction runs one way** (p. 243). It is logically possible that all known precision statements might be reduced, as macro-laws, to frequency statements. The converse is impossible. Frequency statements cannot be deduced from precision statements (section 70), because they need their own specifically statistical assumptions. Probabilities can be calculated only from probability estimates. The logical situation favours neither determinism nor indeterminism. Even if physics worked with frequency statements only, we would not be entitled to assert that there are no precise laws (pp. 243–244).

**Independent of section 77** (p. 244). Even if the uncertainty relations were not refuted by the experiment, they could only be tested and corroborated as frequency statements. So no indeterminist conclusion follows from their corroboration.

*Later note (n.\*2, p. 244):* Popper still believes this. We cannot conclude from successful frequency predictions about penny tosses that single tosses are undetermined. But one may argue for indeterminist metaphysics by pointing to difficulties it could dissolve.

**Causality is metaphysical** (pp. 244–245). Whether the world is ruled by strict laws is, for Popper, a metaphysical question. Laws are always hypotheses, which may be superseded or deduced from probability estimates. Denying causality amounts to urging the theorist to stop searching, and no proof backs that.

Popper disagrees with Schlick, who said the causal law is testable in the same sense as any natural law (p. 244). The causal principle differs in character from a natural law. The belief in causality is a "metaphysical hypostatization" of a methodological rule: the scientist's decision never to abandon the search for laws (p. 245). Popper says the belief in causality seems more fertile than the indeterminist metaphysics advocated by Heisenberg, whose comments, he says, have had a crippling effect on research (p. 245).

**No empirical hypothesis can compel us to stop searching** (p. 245). Heisenberg's formulae, and similar statements corroborated only through statistical consequences, do not necessarily lead to indeterminist conclusions. This alone does not prove that no other empirical statement could justify such conclusions. Popper's argument runs:
1. A statement supposed to be free from metaphysical elements can have indeterminist conclusions only if they are falsifiable.
2. They can be shown false only by formulating laws and deducing corroborated predictions.
3. So, taken as empirical hypotheses, we ought to try hard to falsify them, which means searching for laws and predictions.
4. Hence an exhortation to abandon the search would undercut the empirical character of these hypotheses. It is self-contradictory to think an empirical hypothesis could compel us to abandon the search.

*Later note (n.\*4, p. 245):* this holds as a reply to a positivist but is misleading as it stands. A falsifiable statement may have logically weak consequences that are not falsifiable (cf. section 66).

**Heisenberg's determinist habits and the constants c and h** (p. 246). Heisenberg gives a causal explanation of why causal explanations are impossible: causality fails owing to interference, a causal interaction (n.\*5). Some authors treat $c$ and $h$ as setting in-principle limits on research and dismiss further questions as "pseudo". Popper sees a real analogy but a different moral. The constancy of light velocity only asserts that we shall find no faster signals. The Heisenberg formulae only assert that we shall find no "super-pure" cases and cannot produce any. Such laws challenge us to search for the forbidden, because empirical statements are tested by trying to falsify them (p. 246).

**Historical diagnosis** (pp. 246–247). Physicists long held a determinist metaphysics. Deducing the (statistical) light spectra from a mechanical atom model failed, and this was inevitable, since statistical laws cannot be deduced from a non-statistical model. Around 1924 (Bohr, Kramers, Slater) it seemed that probabilities replaced strict laws in each single atom. Determinism was wrecked mainly because probability statements were expressed as formally singular statements. Indeterminism, backed by the uncertainty principle, sprang from the same misunderstanding of such statements (p. 247).

**Conclusion:** we should look for strict laws, i.e. prohibitions that can founder upon experience. We should abstain from prohibitions that set limits to the possibilities of research (p. 247).

## Toy example
*Our own illustration; the numbers are invented, not real physics.*

**Part 1: single-event probability versus class frequency.** Take a fair die. Statement S: "the probability that throw $k$ is a five is 1/6." In 600 throws about 100 fives are expected. If throw $k$ turns out to be a five, S is not contradicted, and if it is not a five, S is not contradicted either. It says nothing about which outcome occurs (cf. p. 224).

**Part 2: scatter claims need exact single values.** A "slit" selects groups of 5 particles. After each selection we measure each particle's momentum exactly (arbitrary units).
- Narrow slit: momenta 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. Mean = 25/5 = 5. Deviations: −4, −2, 0, 2, 4. Squares: 16+4+0+4+16 = 40. Variance = 40/5 = 8. Scatter (standard deviation) = √8 ≈ 2.83.
- Wider slit: momenta 4, 5, 5, 5, 6. Mean = 25/5 = 5. Deviations: −1, 0, 0, 0, 1. Squares: 1+0+0+0+1 = 2. Variance = 2/5 = 0.4. Scatter ≈ 0.63.

The statistical claim "narrower slit means more momentum scatter" is checked by comparing 2.83 with 0.63. Each of those ten momenta was an exact single measurement. If no exact single values were available, the claim could not be tested. This mirrors Popper's point that exact measurement is needed to test scatter predictions (pp. 226–227).

## Concepts
- [[heisenberg-formulae-vs-uncertainty-relations]] (new) — the mathematical inequality $\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \ge h/4\pi$ versus its reading as a limit on measurement precision (p. 219)
- [[statistical-scatter-relations]] (new) — objective statistical reading: selecting by position makes momenta scatter, and vice versa (p. 220)
- [[formally-singular-probability-statement]] (new) — a probability statement about a single event or particle, e.g. ${}_\alpha P_k(\beta)=p$ (pp. 219, 224)
- [[physical-selection]] (new) — physical separation of particles, e.g. by a slit, as opposed to a merely imagined selection (p. 221)
- [[pure-case]] (new) — a selection as homogeneous as the Heisenberg formulae permit, with equality (p. 222)
- [[predictive-vs-non-predictive-measurement]] (new) — measurements used to derive predictions versus those used to test them (p. 237)
- [[hypothesis-of-linkage]] (new) — auxiliary hypothesis that predictive measurements and physical selections are inseparably linked (p. 235)
- [[metaphysical-element]] (new) — here, a magnitude or doctrine that no possible test could touch, e.g. unobservable orbits or paths (pp. 211, 227)
- [[indeterminist-metaphysics]] (new) — the doctrine that there are no strict laws for single processes. Popper argues that well-corroborated frequency statements do not entitle us to it, and that no empirical hypothesis can compel us to abandon the search for laws (pp. 243–247)
- [[falsifiability]] — here applied to path hypotheses and to indeterminist conclusions, which must be falsifiable if empirical (pp. 228, 245)
- [[corroboration]] — even if the experiment of section 77 did not refute them, the uncertainty relations could only be tested and corroborated as frequency statements (p. 244)

## Canonical instances
- [[lorentz-fitzgerald-contraction]] — earlier theory using unobservables, cleaned by Einstein; analogy to Bohr and Heisenberg (p. 212)
- [[semi-translucent-mirror]] — Einstein's mirror: apparent "jump" in probabilities is a change of reference class (pp. 231–232)
- [[die-throw-five]] — the single-throw probability 1/6 used to show that single events cannot contradict probability statements (p. 224)
- [[slit-selection-experiment]] — slit selection by position followed by momentum measurement, showing the need for non-predictive measurements (p. 226)
- [[compton-type-collision-experiment]] — Popper's Figure 2 experiment, later withdrawn (pp. 239–242)
- [[epr-argument]] — Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen reasoning, given in Popper's later notes (p. 241, n.\*4)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Change of reference class after observing the photon's fate ↔ Bayesian confirmation: conditioning on new information — here the probabilities are frequency ratios, not degrees of belief.
- [structural-analogy] Popper's test of path hypotheses by predicting later observations ↔ falsifiability and demarcation: untestable claims count as metaphysical until a falsifiable consequence is found.
- [metaphor] The argument that no empirical hypothesis can forbid searching for laws ↔ Lakatos-style research programmes: a methodological decision rather than a statement.

## Questions this raises
- What exactly is the difference between "predictive" and "non-predictive" measurement, and does the later retraction undermine section 76's claim that paths are needed for testing?
- Popper later says Schrödinger's theory may be both objective, singular and probabilistic (p. 229, n.\*3). How does this fit his original "formally singular, hence statistical" reading?
- If indeterminism is metaphysical, what kind of argument ("difficulties it might dissolve", p. 244, n.\*2) can support or reject it?
- Does the reference-class account of the mirror example fully dissolve the reduction-of-the-wave-packet problem, or only its trivial part?
