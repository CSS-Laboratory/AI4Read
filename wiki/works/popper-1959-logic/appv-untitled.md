# Examination of an Objection: The Two-Slit Experiment

## L0 — One line
Popper answers an imaginary two-slit objection by re-interpreting it statistically, replacing the single atom with a cluster of atoms; in the third case this gives no interference, so the supposed contradiction does not arise.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
An objector imagines an atom radiating through two slits. Exact position should give interference and exact momentum should not, so measuring both exactly would predict both outcomes. The objector means this to refute Popper's claim that arbitrarily exact simultaneous measurements are compatible with quantum theory. His reply reads each case statistically. Position-selection and momentum-selection give the two known outcomes. Observing the atom's full path means it recoils into many positions and directions, so it is not a pure selection and gives no interference. Popper adds that the objection would not solve the wave–particle dualism anyway, because the two measurements are not mutually exclusive. A footnote says his present view is that the two-slit experiment is to be treated differently.

**Prerequisites:** position and momentum; wave interference (a pattern made by overlapping waves) vs. particles; coherent = waves with a fixed phase relation; recoil = the atom's push-back when it emits a photon; pure selection = a group picked by one exact quantity; complementarity = the wave–particle dualism problem; Popper's statistical reading of quantum theory (section 76); "ensemble" = a large set of similar systems.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Objection (not Popper's view):** an exact simultaneous measurement of position and momentum would predict both interference and no interference (p. 297–298).
- Popper's reply replaces the single atom by a cluster of atoms: in the position and momentum cases this is a pure selection (p. 298–299).
- Pure position selection gives coherent spherical waves and interference. Pure momentum selection gives a parallel monochromatic beam and no interference (p. 298–299).
- For the third case, the atom's recoils spread it over a volume and over directions, so no pure selection and no coherence arise (p. 299).
- So in the third case Popper finds no interference patterns, not two contradictory predictions (p. 299).
- Popper holds that argument (a) would not explain complementarity anyway, because the two measurements can be combined in inexact form (p. 299–300).
- His own interpretation handles intermediate cases but was not meant to solve the wave–particle dualism (p. 300).
- A footnote gives Popper's present view that the two-slit experiment is to be treated differently (p. 297).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Footnote *1 (p. 297).** Footnote: Popper's present view is that the two-slit experiment is "to be treated differently". He still finds the interpretation proposed in this appendix "of some interest". The remarks under (e) seem to him still to contain a valid criticism of the attempt to explain the particle–wave dualism by "complementarity", an attempt that, it seems, some physicists have more recently abandoned. He refers to appendix *xi and Postscript ch. *v, §*110.

**(a) The objection (p. 297–298).** This is the objector's thought experiment. It is meant to refute Popper's claim that arbitrarily exact simultaneous (non-predictive) measurements of position and momentum are compatible with quantum theory.
- Setup: a radiating atom A; its light passes two slits, Sl1 and Sl2, and falls on a screen S (p. 297).
- Objector, citing Heisenberg: one can measure exactly either the position of A or the momentum of the radiation, not both (p. 297).
- Exact position blurs momentum. Light is then assumed to leave A in spherical waves. A point-like source emits coherent light, so an interference pattern appears (p. 297–298).
- Exact momentum (e.g. by measuring recoils) blurs position. Radiation is then corpuscular, "needle-radiation". There are scintillations on the screen and no interference. A non-point-like source does not emit coherent light (p. 297–298).
- If both were exact, the atom would have to emit continuous coherent waves (interference) and also an incoherent photon beam. Photons do not interact, so there would be no interference. Two contradictory predictions follow (p. 298).

**(b) Position case, read statistically (p. 298).** Replace the single atom by a cluster of atoms emitting coherent light. A second screen with a very small aperture sits exactly where atom A was. Light from the cluster is selected by position at the aperture and then spreads as continuous spherical waves. The single atom becomes a statistical case of "pure positional selection" (p. 298).

**(c) Momentum case (p. 299).** Likewise, exact momentum with blurred position becomes a pure selection by momentum. This is a monochromatic photon beam travelling along parallel lines from a non-point-like source. The results are correct: interference in (b), none in (c) (p. 299).

*Intuition note.* Popper treats "measuring exactly" as picking out a subset of a large group. The picked-out group is sharp in one quantity only. Each known experimental result then follows from the subset, not from one atom having both exact values.

**(d) The third case (p. 299).** Suppose the atom's path, meaning both position and momentum, were observed exactly.
- The atom emits single photons and recoils each time, into a new position and a new direction (p. 299).
- Over time it ranges over a considerable volume of space, so the atom may not be replaced by a point-like cluster. It is replaced by a cluster spread over a volume and radiating in all directions (p. 299).
- This is not a pure case and gives no coherent radiation, so no interference patterns (p. 299).
- Popper says similar objections may be re-interpreted statistically along the lines of this example (p. 299).

*Intuition note.* The objection claimed the third case would predict interference as well as its absence. In Popper's reading the exactly followed atom behaves like a spread-out, incoherent source. So, on his reading, only the "no interference" result follows.

**(e) Complementarity (p. 299–300).**
- Argument (a) would be insufficient to elucidate complementarity (wave–particle dualism). It claims the atom emits either coherent waves or incoherent photons, and that the two experiments exclude each other, so no contradiction arises (p. 299).
- Popper replies that the experiments are not mutually exclusive. A not too exact position measurement can be combined with a not too exact momentum measurement. The atom then emits neither completely coherent waves nor completely incoherent photons (p. 299–300).
- His statistical interpretation can treat such intermediate cases easily, though it was never meant to solve the dualism (p. 300).
- He supposes that a really satisfactory solution will hardly be possible within statistical quantum physics (Born's interpretation, 1925–26). He thinks it might perhaps be found in quantum physics of wave-fields ("second quantization", Dirac and others, 1927–28) (p. 300).

## Toy example (ours; an analogy, not real physics)
Take an ensemble of 4 recorded (position, momentum) pairs in arbitrary units: (0,+1), (0,+2), (0,−1), (0,−2).
- All positions equal 0, so this is a pure selection by position. The momenta take 4 different values (spread).
- Now take (0,+1), (1,+1), (2,+1), (3,+1). All momenta equal +1, so this is a pure selection by momentum. The positions take 4 values.
- Finally take a tracked path with recoils: (0,+1), (1,−1), (0,+2), (−1,−2). Distinct positions: {0, 1, −1} = 3. Distinct momenta: 4. Neither quantity is constant, so this is not a pure selection.

This mirrors Popper's point. The exactly followed atom belongs to the third kind of ensemble, not to either of the first two.

## Concepts
- [[statistical-interpretation-of-qm]] (new) — Popper's own interpretation, applied here by re-reading the imaginary experiment statistically and replacing a single atom with a cluster of atoms (p. 298, 300).
- [[pure-selection]] (new) — an ensemble picked by one exact quantity (position or momentum) (p. 298–299).
- [[complementarity]] (new) — the so-called problem of the wave–particle dualism; as Popper describes it, argument (a) tries to meet it by treating the two experiments as mutually exclusive (p. 299).
- [[falsifiability]] — the objection is framed as an attempt to refute Popper's assertion by deriving two contradictory predictions (p. 297–298); the word itself is not used on these pages.
