# Falsifiability

## L0 — One line
A theory is empirical if it forbids at least one possible basic statement (a singular statement of fact), and it stays empirical only if scientists decide not to rescue it with conventionalist stratagems (ad hoc moves that save a theory from refutation).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Popper asks whether falsifiability can demarcate empirical theories from non-empirical ones, given the conventionalist reply that any theory can always be saved. He grants that the logical form of a system alone cannot settle the question. The demarcation therefore has two parts. The first is a methodological rule: do not save a threatened theory by ad hoc devices. The second is a logical criterion: a theory is falsifiable if it prohibits at least one basic statement (a potential falsifier). Falsifiability differs from falsification, which needs an accepted, reproducible falsifying effect. Tautologies, purely existential statements and contradictions fail to divide the possible basic statements, so they are not empirical. Consistency is a largely analogous requirement for any system.

**Prerequisites:** the difference between universal and singular statements; basic logic (negation, conjunction, entailment, tautology); Popper's earlier idea of a demarcation criterion.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Conventionalism (Poincaré, Duhem) treats natural laws as free conventions, which implicitly define its concepts (fix their meaning) and are needed to determine what a measurement is; Popper calls this a self-contained, defensible position that he rejects because it rests on a different aim of science (pp. 58–59).
- The conventionalist objection says that every theory can be made to fit reality by ad hoc hypotheses, redefinitions, or doubts about the experimenter or the theoretician. Falsifiability would then not demarcate anything (pp. 60–61).
- Popper concedes the objection for systems taken in themselves and answers with a methodological decision: never use these stratagems. Only with reference to the methods applied to a system can one ask whether it is conventionalist or empirical (p. 61).
- Popper's rule: auxiliary hypotheses are acceptable only if they do not diminish but increase the degree of falsifiability, i.e. forbid more; harmless singular statements sometimes called auxiliary hypotheses are an exception (pp. 62–63).
- Logical criterion: a theory is falsifiable if the class of its potential falsifiers (the basic statements it prohibits) is non-empty. The theory asserts only the falsity of these (pp. 65–66).
- The requirement that a falsifying hypothesis be empirical concerns only its logical relation to possible basic statements. Falsification needs accepted basic statements that contradict the theory and at the same time corroborate (i.e. successfully test) a falsifying hypothesis describing a reproducible effect (pp. 66–67).
- A falsifiable theory prohibits at least one event (a type of occurrence, such as the upsetting of a glass of water), not merely one occurrence (a class of equivalent singular statements); purely existential statements (statements that only assert that something exists) prohibit nothing (pp. 69–71).
- Tautologies and existential statements say too little, contradictions say too much. Consistency and falsifiability are to a large extent analogous requirements (pp. 71–73).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening.** Popper postpones the question whether falsifiable basic statements exist and assumes a positive answer. He asks how far the demarcation criterion applies to theoretical systems, via a critique of conventionalism (p. 57).

### 19. Some conventionalist objections
- **The conventionalist view (as Popper describes it).** Conventionalism would seem (Popper's hedge) to arise from wonder at the simplicity of physical laws. Kant said our intellect imposes its laws on nature. The conventionalist says instead that nature is not simple. Only the laws are simple, and they are our own free creations or decisions (p. 58).
- For the conventionalist, theory is a logical construction, not a picture of nature. It defines an artificial world of concepts implicitly defined by the chosen laws (p. 58).
- Hence laws are not falsifiable by observation. They are needed to determine what an observation or measurement is, e.g. a clock is "accurate" only if the movements measured with it satisfy the adopted axioms of mechanics (pp. 58–59).
- Notes: Duhem denies the possibility of crucial experiments because he thinks of them as verifications, while Popper asserts the possibility of crucial falsifying experiments (p. 58, note 1). Cornelius treats the melting point of lead as part of the definition of lead; Popper regards the statement as synthetic (p. 59, note 2).
- **Popper's assessment.** Conventionalism deserves credit for clarifying the relation of theory and experiment. Popper regards it as self-contained and defensible, and detecting inconsistencies in it is unlikely to succeed. He still finds it unacceptable. He demands no final certainty from science, whereas the conventionalist seeks "a system of knowledge based upon ultimate grounds" (Dingler) (p. 59).
- In a crisis the system will appear unshaken to the conventionalist, who explains away conflicts, perhaps by blaming our inadequate mastery of the system, or by suggesting ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses or perhaps corrections to instruments (pp. 59–60).
- Popper welcomes the falsifying experiment as a success that opens new experience. The conventionalist sees new theories as a monument to the "total collapse of science" (Dingler). The conventionalist's only selection principle is simplicity, which in practice means the classical system of the day (p. 60).
- Popper says his conflict with the conventionalists cannot ultimately be settled merely by detached theoretical discussion (p. 60).
- **Objection (an imaginary conventionalist):** theories are not verifiable, but they are not falsifiable either. Any axiomatic system can be brought into "correspondence with reality" by four routes (pp. 60–61):
  1. ad hoc hypotheses;
  2. modifying ostensive (or explicit) definitions;
  3. doubting the experimenter's reliability, perhaps calling him a liar;
  4. doubting the acumen of the theoretician.
- Therefore the falsifiable/non-falsifiable division would be ambiguous, and the criterion useless for demarcation (p. 61).

*Intuition note.* If every clash between theory and observation could be absorbed by changing something else, the imaginary conventionalist argues, falsifiable and non-falsifiable systems could not be cleanly divided, and the criterion would be useless for demarcation.

### 20. Methodological rules
- The objections of the imaginary conventionalist seem to Popper incontestable. He admits that his criterion does not give an unambiguous classification and that analysing logical form cannot decide whether a system is conventional or empirical; this shows only that the criterion cannot be applied immediately to a system of statements (already pointed out in sections 9 and 11). The question whether a given system as such is conventionalist or empirical is therefore misconceived; it can be asked only with reference to the methods applied to the system (p. 61).
- **The only way to avoid conventionalism is a decision:** never save a threatened system by any conventionalist stratagem. Black's remark: an adapted hypothesis "will please the imagination but does not advance our knowledge" (pp. 61–62).
- To formulate rules against stratagems one must know their forms, so as to meet each with an appropriate counter-move. When a system is found to have been rescued by a stratagem, we agree to test it afresh and reject it, as circumstances may require. The list of stratagems is not claimed complete. Popper mentions sociology and psychology, and psycho-analysts as often yielding to the temptation (p. 62).
- **Auxiliary hypotheses:** acceptable only if they do not diminish, but increase, the degree of falsifiability. The system then forbids more. The introduction of an auxiliary hypothesis is to be regarded as an attempt to construct a new system, judged by whether it would be a real advance in knowledge (p. 62).
  - Acceptable example: Pauli's exclusion principle (p. 62).
  - Unsatisfactory example: the Fitzgerald–Lorentz contraction hypothesis. Popper says it had no falsifiable consequences and merely served to restore agreement between theory and experiment, mainly the findings of Michelson and Morley. A later footnote (*1) says this is a mistake, as Grünbaum pointed out (1959), yet the hypothesis is less testable than special relativity and so may illustrate degrees of adhocness. Relativity predicted new effects and so opened new tests (pp. 62–63).
- The rule is qualified. Some singular "auxiliary hypotheses" are harmless, e.g. that an unrepeatable measurement may have been in error (p. 63).
- **Definitions:** changes in explicit definitions are permissible but count as modifications, so the system is re-examined as new. Undefined universal names are of two kinds (p. 63).
  1. Concepts that appear only in the highest-level statements and can be eliminated in deduction (e.g. "energy").
  2. Concepts whose meaning is established by usage (e.g. "movement", "mass-point", "position"). Here surreptitious alteration of usage is forbidden.
- **Experimenter and theoretician:** intersubjectively testable experiments are to be accepted, or rejected in the light of counter-experiments. A bare appeal to derivations to be discovered in future can be disregarded (p. 63).

*Intuition note.* Popper's reply is a methodological decision, not a logical proof: he admits that logical form alone cannot settle the matter. Under his rule an auxiliary hypothesis is acceptable only if it makes the theory say more, not less (harmless singular statements excepted).

### 21. Logical investigation of falsifiability
- Guarding against stratagems matters only for systems that would be falsifiable if treated by these rules. The falsifiability of such a system is characterized through its logical relation to the class of basic statements (p. 64).
- **"Basic statements" here** means all self-consistent singular statements of a certain logical form, "all conceivable singular statements of fact". They are not a set of accepted statements and include mutually incompatible ones (p. 64).
- **Failed attempts to define "empirical"** (pp. 64–65):
  1. Singular statements can be deduced from it. This fails because deduction needs further singular statements (initial conditions).
  2. Singular statements derivable with initial conditions. This fails because a tautological theory also allows this: from *Twice two is four* and *Here is a black raven* it follows that *Here is a raven*.
  3. More can be deduced from theory plus initial conditions than from the initial conditions alone. This excludes tautologies but not synthetic metaphysical statements. From *Every occurrence has a cause* and *A catastrophe is occurring here* we get *This catastrophe has a cause*.
- Popper concludes that the theory should allow us to deduce, roughly speaking, more empirical singular statements than can be deduced from the initial conditions alone. This is why the definition has to rest on a particular class of singular statements, the basic statements (p. 65).
- **Definition.** A theory is called "empirical" or "falsifiable" if it divides the class of all possible basic statements unambiguously into two non-empty subclasses (pp. 65–66):
  - the class of basic statements it prohibits, its **potential falsifiers**;
  - the class it does not contradict, the "permitted" ones.
- Briefly: a theory is falsifiable if the class of its potential falsifiers is not empty. A theory asserts only the falsity of its potential falsifiers. About the permitted statements it says nothing, not even that they are true (p. 66).
- **Footnote *1 (p. 65):** if $b_2$ does not follow from $b_1$ but does follow from $b_1$ together with theory $t$, then $b_1 \cdot \neg b_2$ contradicts $t$. This conjunction is a basic statement, so the criterion demands a falsifying basic statement. It is equivalent to falsifiability. As a criterion of meaning it breaks down: negations of some meaningful statements would become meaningless, and a meaningful statement conjoined with a meaningless pseudo-sentence would become meaningful. Both criticisms are said to be harmless against it as a criterion of demarcation (p. 65).
- **Footnote *2 (p. 66):** many permitted basic statements will, in the presence of the theory, contradict each other. Example: *All planets move in circles* is trivially instantiated by up to three positions of one planet, but two such instances together will mostly contradict the law (p. 66).

*Intuition note.* A theory is empirical if it rules something out among all possible observation reports. The range of "possible worlds" it allows must be smaller than all of them.

### 22. Falsifiability and falsification
- Falsifiability is solely a criterion of the empirical character of a system. Falsification needs further rules (p. 66).
- A theory is falsified **only if** accepted basic statements contradict it. This condition is necessary but not sufficient, since non-reproducible single occurrences are of no significance to science (p. 66).
- Falsification is accepted only if a low-level empirical hypothesis describing a reproducible effect is proposed and corroborated: a **falsifying hypothesis** (p. 66).
- Example: to falsify *All ravens are black*, the intersubjectively testable statement that there is a family of white ravens in the New York zoo would suffice. Such a hypothesis need not be strictly universal (note 1, p. 66).
- The falsifying hypothesis must be empirical, which concerns only its logical form. That it be corroborated refers to tests it has passed against accepted basic statements (pp. 66–67).
- Basic statements therefore play two roles: (a) the system of all possible ones gives the logical characterization of empirical form; (b) the accepted ones corroborate hypotheses (p. 67).
- **Footnotes (p. 67).** A falsifying experiment is usually a crucial experiment between two hypotheses. How often must an effect be reproduced? In some cases not even once; if the zoo's white ravens have died, the tester judges the basic statement by witnesses and documents. Popper answers the apparent regress as follows: the rules needed are merely rules for accepting basic statements that falsify a well-tested and so far successful hypothesis, and the accepted basic statements to which such a rule has recourse need not be of this character. He adds that the rule is far from exhaustive and will be expanded in chapter 5 (especially section 29).

### 23. Occurrences and events
- The requirement splits into the methodological postulate (section 20), which "can hardly be made quite precise", and the logical criterion, which is definite once "basic" is clarified (section 28) (p. 68).
- A "realistic" mode of speech is equivalent to the formal one: a basic statement describes an **occurrence**, and a theory rules out certain occurrences and is falsified if they occur (p. 68).
- **Definition of occurrence:** let $p_k$ be a singular statement ($k$ marks its individual names or coordinates). The class of all statements equivalent to $p_k$ is the occurrence $P_k$. For example, *It is now thundering here* and *It is thundering in the 13th District of Vienna on the 10th of June 1933 at 5.15 p.m.* belong to one class (p. 69).
- *$P_k$ has occurred* means that $p_k$ and all statements equivalent to it are true. *$P_k$ contradicts theory $t$* means every statement equivalent to $p_k$ contradicts $t$, so each is a potential falsifier. The definition is meant to interpret the realistic speech, not to claim that speakers think of classes (p. 69).
- **Event:** what is typical or universal in an occurrence. If $P_k, P_l, \dots$ are occurrences differing only in the individuals (spatio-temporal positions) involved, the class of them is "the event $(P)$". Example: upsetting a glass of water (p. 69).
- A falsifiable theory prohibits not merely one occurrence but always at least one event. The class of its potential falsifiers, if non-empty, contains unlimited basic statements. Basic statements belonging to one event are called "homotypic". Every non-empty class of potential falsifiers contains at least one non-empty class of homotypic basic statements (p. 70).
- **Circle diagram:** the circle's area is all possible basic statements, i.e. all possible empirical worlds. Each event is a narrow sector along a radius. Occurrences with the same individuals lie on the same concentric circle. Falsifiability then requires that every empirical theory forbid at least one radius (p. 70).
- Singular statements represent occurrences; universal statements do not represent events but exclude them (note 2, p. 70).
- **Purely existential statements:** each corresponds to an event whose basic statements all verify it, but the class of potential falsifiers is empty. It forbids no radius, so it says nothing about the possible worlds of experience. That every basic statement entails an existential statement does not show its empirical character, since a tautology also follows from every statement (pp. 70–71).
- **Self-contradictory statements** assert "too much", while tautologies and existential statements assert "too little" (p. 71). Any statement follows from a contradiction, so its class of potential falsifiers is the class of all possible basic statements and any statement whatsoever falsifies it (pp. 71–72).
- **Footnote *2 derivation (p. 71):** from Russell's primitive propositions:

$$p \to (p \vee q) \tag{1}$$

Substituting $\bar p$ for $p$, then writing $p \to q$ for $\bar p \vee q$:

$$\bar p \to (p \to q) \tag{2}$$

By "importation":

$$\bar p \cdot p \to q \tag{3}$$

With modus ponens, any $q$ follows from any statement of the form $\bar p \cdot p$. The same note separates a merely factually false statement, which "materially implies" every statement but does not logically entail it, from a logically false one, which entails every statement (p. 71).
- Popper adds that one could perhaps say this illustrates an advantage of considering possible falsifiers rather than possible verifiers: if a statement could be verified by verifying its consequences, or merely made probable that way, one would expect any accepted basic statement to confirm, verify or at least make probable any self-contradictory statement (p. 72).
- A footnote (p. 68) says Popper still regards his "syntactical" definitions of occurrence and event as adequate for his purpose but no longer believes they are intuitively adequate; Tarski pointed out to him that a "semantic" definition would be required instead.

*Intuition note.* A theory that excludes nothing says nothing about experience. One that excludes everything says too much, and everything refutes it.

### 24. Falsifiability and consistency
- Consistency can be regarded as the first requirement for every theoretical system, empirical or not (p. 72).
- Popper says it is not enough to note that a self-contradictory system must be rejected because it is "false", since we often work with actually false statements that give adequate results (e.g. Nernst's approximation). The importance of consistency shows in that a contradictory system is uninformative: any conclusion can be derived from it, so no statement is singled out (p. 72).
- A consistent system divides all possible statements into those it contradicts and those compatible with it, which include its derivable conclusions (p. 72).
- An empirical system must also be falsifiable. The two conditions are "to a large extent analogous". Inconsistent statements fail to differentiate between any two statements among all possible statements. Non-falsifiable statements fail to differentiate between any two among all possible empirical basic statements (pp. 72–73).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take a lamp observed at two times. A basic statement is a pair, giving the state at time 1 and then at time 2. There are exactly 4 possible ones: (on,on), (on,off), (off,on), (off,off). This is a stand-in for the huge class of real basic statements.

- Theory $t$: "If the lamp is on at time 1, it is on at time 2." It contradicts only (on,off). Potential falsifiers: 1 statement, a non-empty class, so $t$ is falsifiable. It permits the other 3 and says nothing about them (p. 66).
- Tautology "The lamp is on or off": potential falsifiers 0, so it is not falsifiable (p. 71).
- Contradiction "The lamp is on and not on": potential falsifiers 4 of 4. Any observation refutes it (p. 71).
- Auxiliary hypothesis $h$: "If the lamp is off at time 1, it is off at time 2." Then $t \wedge h$ forbids (on,off) and (off,on): 2 statements instead of 1. It forbids more, so by Popper's rule it is acceptable (p. 62).
- A rescue that deleted the prohibition of (on,off), e.g. by redefining "on", would leave 0 falsifiers. By the rule it would be rejected as conventionalist (pp. 61–62).

Counting falsifiers is only a toy device. Popper says degrees of falsifiability are treated in sections 31–40 (p. 62).

## Concepts
- [[falsifiability]] — a theory is falsifiable if the class of its potential falsifiers (prohibited basic statements) is not empty (p. 66)
- [[demarcation problem]] — here: separating empirical systems from conventionalist or metaphysical ones, by a logical criterion plus a methodological decision (p. 61)
- [[potential falsifier]] (new) — a basic statement that the theory prohibits (pp. 65–66)
- [[basic statement]] (new) — a singular statement of a certain logical form. The system of basic statements covers every conceivable singular statement of fact, incompatible ones included (p. 64)
- [[conventionalism]] (new) — Poincaré/Duhem/Dingler view: laws are free conventions that implicitly define concepts, so they cannot be falsified by observation (pp. 57–59)
- [[conventionalist stratagem]] (new) — ad hoc hypotheses, changing definitions, doubting the experimenter or the theoretician, used to save a system (pp. 60–62)
- [[ad hoc hypothesis]] (new) — an auxiliary hypothesis that does not increase falsifiability; Popper's example is the Fitzgerald–Lorentz hypothesis, with a footnote qualification (pp. 62–63)
- [[falsifying hypothesis]] (new) — a corroborated low-level empirical hypothesis describing a reproducible effect that refutes a theory (p. 66)
- [[corroboration]] — here: a hypothesis passing tests against accepted basic statements (p. 67)
- [[occurrence vs. event]] (new) — occurrence: a class of equivalent singular statements; event: a class of occurrences differing only in individuals involved (p. 69)
- [[logical form]] (new) — whether a hypothesis is falsifiable concerns only its logical relation to possible basic statements (p. 67), but logical form alone cannot settle whether a whole system is empirical or conventionalist (p. 61)
- [[analytic / synthetic distinction]] — Popper takes the melting-point statement about lead to be synthetic, against Cornelius (p. 59, note 2)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Falsifiable theory splits the class of possible basic statements ↔ information theory/classification: a message or classifier that excludes some possibilities carries information, one that excludes none carries none — the text makes the analogy only for consistency.
- [structural-analogy] Conventionalist rescue by auxiliary hypotheses ↔ confirmation holism (Quine–Duhem): a conflict can be absorbed by revising something other than the theory. The source names Duhem.
- [metaphor] Conventionalist stratagems ↔ degenerating research programme (Lakatos): ad hoc rescue versus predicting new effects. Popper's contrast with relativity is similar.

## Questions this raises
- If the choice between empirical and conventionalist method is a decision, what justifies Popper's decision beyond his different aim for science?
- How are "degrees of falsifiability" defined (sections 31–40), and how do they rank auxiliary hypotheses?
- What rules govern accepting a basic statement (chapter 5), given the regress worry in note *1 (p. 67)?
- Are purely existential statements and mixed theories really not scientific, if parts of an empirical theory contain "metaphysical elements" that cannot be eliminated by a hard and fast rule (p. 65, note *1)?
