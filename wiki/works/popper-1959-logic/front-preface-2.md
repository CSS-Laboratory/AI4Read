# Preface to the First English Edition, 1959

## L0 — One line
Popper argues that philosophy's genuine problems are about things, not words, and that epistemology's central problem — the growth of knowledge — is best studied through the growth of *scientific* knowledge, not through analysing ordinary or artificial languages.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This unit gathers three short pieces: the 1934 preface, an epigraph from Lord Acton, and the 1959 preface. Popper replies to "language analysts" who think philosophy has no genuine problems beyond words. He insists there is at least one shared philosophical problem, cosmology: understanding the world, ourselves, and our knowledge as part of it. He argues philosophy has no method of its own; the one method of all rational discussion is to state a problem clearly and criticize proposed solutions as hard as possible. For epistemology, the central problem is the growth of knowledge, which is best studied through the growth of scientific knowledge rather than through analysis of ordinary or artificial languages.

**Prerequisites:** familiarity with logical positivism / ordinary-language philosophy; basic idea of a logical paradox (e.g. the liar); no formal logic needed.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The 1934 epigraphs stage a dispute: Schlick fears that philosophy may never manage to pose a genuine problem, while Kant answers that a long-running dispute, especially in philosophy, is always about things rather than mere words (p. xv). Popper's later argument in effect takes Kant's side (p. xviii).
- Popper's thesis 1: philosophers are free to use any method in their search for truth; no method belongs to philosophy alone (p. xix).
- Popper's thesis 2: epistemology's central, enduring problem is the growth of knowledge, best studied via the growth of *scientific* knowledge, and this cannot be replaced by studying linguistic usage or language systems (p. xix).
- The one method he does accept is general, not philosophy-specific: state a problem clearly and try as hard as possible to refute one's own proposed solution rather than defend it (p. xix).
- Logical analysis correctly solves logical paradoxes (the liar, Russell's, Richard's), but Popper denies that traditional philosophical problems — even Kant's antinomies — share their self-referential structure (p. xx).
- Against ordinary-language epistemology: the growth-of-knowledge problem transcends common-sense knowledge, because common sense grows precisely by turning into science (p. xxii).
- Among scientific-knowledge epistemologists Popper splits two groups: builders of artificial model languages, whom he judges too poor to express real science (p. xxiii–xxv), and analysts of actual scientific discussions and problem-situations, with whom he aligns (p. xxv–xxvi).
- Closing caution: philosophy of science must not itself become a new specialist dogma; Popper's own motive is to understand the riddle of the world and of our knowledge of it (p. xxvi).
- Acknowledgments thank D. G. Nicholls for the Acton quotation, and Lakatos, Miller, Musgrave, and Bernays for corrections to later printings (p. xxvii).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Preface to the First Edition, 1934 (p. xv–xvi).** Two epigraphs frame the book. Schlick (1930) worries that even if philosophy's old problems seem solved, this is small comfort, since his deeper fear is that "philosophy will never get so far as to pose a genuine problem" (p. xv). Kant (1786) answers the opposite: he asserts that whenever a dispute has gone on for any length of time, "especially in philosophy," there was "never a problem about mere words, but always a genuine problem about things" (p. xv). Popper's own text: a scientist can attack a problem directly, because an organized structure of doctrine and an accepted problem-situation already exists; the philosopher instead faces something like a heap of ruins (though perhaps with treasure buried underneath), with no agreed problem-situation — indeed it is generally accepted that none exists (p. xv–xvi). Some philosophers nonetheless believe philosophy can pose genuine problems and want real discussion instead of depressing monologues; if they cannot accept any existing creed, their only option is to begin again from scratch (p. xvi) — describing the project of this book.

*Intuition note:* Popper is not just picking a side in an old quarrel; he is announcing that his whole book will try to show philosophy *can* pose a real problem, by actually posing one.

**Epigraph, p. xvii.** Lord Acton is quoted: "There is nothing more necessary to the man of science than its history, and the logic of discovery" — the way errors are detected, how hypothesis and imagination are used, and how claims are tested (p. xvii).

**Preface to the First English Edition, 1959 (p. xviii–xxvi).**
Popper updates his 1934 remarks for the present situation, addressing the two main schools of language analysts active at the time, whom he treats as both opponents and allies, since they keep alive some traditions of rational philosophy (p. xviii). **Language analysts (reported view):** there are no genuine philosophical problems, or philosophy's problems (if any) are problems of linguistic usage or word-meaning (p. xviii). **Popper replies:** there is at least one problem all thinking people share — cosmology, understanding the world including ourselves and our knowledge as part of it; he holds that all science is, in this sense, cosmology, and that philosophy's and science's whole interest lies in their contributions to it. Understanding how language functions is part of this task, but explaining problems away as merely linguistic puzzles is not (p. xviii).

Two theses follow (p. xix). (1) "There is no method peculiar to philosophy" — philosophers are as free as anyone else to use any method in searching for truth. (2) The central problem of epistemology has always been, and still is, the growth of knowledge, and this is best studied by studying the growth of *scientific* knowledge; the study of knowledge-growth cannot be replaced by studying linguistic usages or language systems (p. xix). Yet Popper admits one method deserves the name "the one method of philosophy" — though it belongs to all rational discussion, science included: state one's problem clearly and examine proposed solutions critically. He equates the rational attitude with the critical attitude: whenever we propose a solution, we should try as hard as we can to overthrow it rather than defend it; criticism is fruitful only if problem and solution are stated clearly enough to be discussed (p. xix). He does not deny that logical analysis can help clarify problems, nor call such methods useless — only that they are far from being the only methods available, and no more characteristic of philosophy than of any other rational inquiry (p. xix–xx).

*Intuition note:* Popper's point is narrower than it sounds — he is not banning word-analysis, only denying it is philosophy's badge or its only tool.

One further method he singles out (p. xx): a version of the historical method — finding out what others thought about a problem, why they faced it, how they formulated and tried to solve it. Ignoring past and present others brings rational discussion to an end, leaving each person talking only to himself; no doubt, Popper notes wryly, God talks mainly to Himself because he has no one else worth talking to, but a philosopher should remember he is no more godlike than anyone else (p. xx).

Popper then gives two historical reasons for the dominance of linguistic analysis (p. xx–xxii). *Reason 1:* it is correct that logical paradoxes (the liar, Russell's, Richard's) need linguistic analysis, via the distinction between meaningful and meaningless expressions. This correct belief is wrongly combined with the belief that traditional philosophical problems have a structure analogous to logical paradoxes. Popper claims logical analysis itself refutes this: the self-reference found in all logical paradoxes is absent from all so-called philosophical paradoxes, "even from Kant's antinomies" (p. xx). *Reason 2* (p. xxi): Locke, Berkeley and Hume's psychological method of analysing ideas and their origin in the senses (their "new way of ideas") was felt to need replacing by a more objective method — analysing words, propositions and usages instead of ideas and thoughts (a "new way of words"). Popper agrees this replacement was a genuine advance, urgently needed, but dissents from treating it as philosophy's *one* true method, for two reasons (p. xxi): (i) even Locke used the "new way of ideas" only for preliminaries, and Berkeley and Hume used it chiefly as a weapon against opponents, not as the basis of their own worldviews — Berkeley's religious views and Hume's political theories were not built on it, though Hume's determinism was. (ii) His "gravest objection": epistemology can be approached via (1) common-sense knowledge or (2) scientific knowledge. **Approach-(1) philosophers (reported view):** rightly hold that scientific knowledge can only be an extension of common sense, but wrongly think common-sense knowledge is the easier of the two to analyse, so they replace the "new way of ideas" with an analysis of ordinary language — of phrases like "I see," "I know," "perhaps" (p. xxi–xxii). **Popper replies:** although scientific knowledge is merely a development of common-sense knowledge, the most important and most exciting problems of epistemology stay completely invisible to anyone confined to analysing ordinary or common-sense knowledge or its formulation in ordinary language (p. xxii). Example: the growth-of-knowledge problem must transcend any study confined to common sense, since the most important way common-sense knowledge grows is precisely by turning into scientific knowledge, and the growth of scientific knowledge is the most important and interesting case of the growth of knowledge (p. xxii). Popper lists philosophers from Plato, Descartes, Leibniz, Kant, Duhem and Poincaré, and from Bacon, Hobbes and Locke to Hume, Mill and Russell, as inspired by the hope of contributing to the advance of knowledge — with Berkeley as the only likely exception he can think of (p. xxii). Ordinary-language philosophers, by contrast, have mostly lost this optimism, even defining philosophy so that it is, by definition, incapable of contributing to knowledge of the world — a kind of "self-mutilation" that does not appeal to Popper (p. xxii). He adds there is no single essence of philosophy to define; a definition of "philosophy" can only be a convention, and he sees no merit in one that would block a student of philosophy from trying to contribute to the advancement of our knowledge of the world (p. xxiii). He also calls it paradoxical that ordinary-language specialists feel sure cosmology is too alien for philosophy to touch, when metaphysical ideas have in fact shaped cosmology from Thales to Einstein — ancient atomism, Descartes on matter, Gilbert, Newton, Leibniz and Boscovic on forces, Faraday and Einstein on fields (p. xxiii). Conclusion: the common-sense approach to epistemology is too narrow, and bound to miss the most interesting problems (p. xxiii).

Popper then splits approach-(2) philosophers, "the goats and the sheep," into two groups (p. xxiii). **"Goats":** aim to study "the language of science" via artificial model languages (p. xxiii). Since no actual language of science exists, they must construct one, but in practice only build miniature toy systems, not workable models for real physics (p. xxiv). Popper judges that this group ends up combining the disadvantages of both worlds — the intricacy of their systems bears no relation to their usefulness, and practically no scientific theory of real interest can be expressed in them (p. xxiv). He criticizes three specific model languages (referenced via footnotes to appendix *vii and section 38, not named here): the first lacks identity and so cannot express equations or even primitive arithmetic; the second fails once the usual arithmetic theorems are added (e.g. Euclid's theorem that no greatest prime exists, or that every number has a successor); the third, the most elaborate and famous of the three, cannot formulate mathematics and has no expressible measurable properties (p. xxiv–xxv). These limits were imposed only because otherwise the authors' own solutions would have failed — a fact partly proved by the authors themselves — yet they claim both that their methods apply to real scientific knowledge and that their methods are exact or precise; Popper says these two claims "cannot both be upheld" (p. xxv). Conclusion: model languages are even less suited than ordinary language to the growth-of-knowledge problem, since they are poorer than ordinary language, yielding only the most crude and misleading model of the growth of knowledge — "an accumulating heap of observation statements" (p. xxv).

**"Sheep":** no fixed method; they analyse actual scientific problems, theories, procedures, and especially scientific discussions (p. xxiii, xxv). This group claims nearly all great Western philosophers as ancestors (even Berkeley, despite his hostility to science's advance); its chief representatives over the last two hundred years are Kant, Whewell, Mill, Peirce, Duhem, Poincaré, Meyerson, Russell, and (in some of his phases) Whitehead (p. xxv). Most agree scientific knowledge grows out of common-sense knowledge, but find it easier to study, since it is an enlarged version of common-sense knowledge — e.g. the Humean problem of reasonable belief becomes the problem of the reasons for accepting or rejecting theories like Newton's, Maxwell's or Einstein's, which detailed historical records let us study almost as if through a microscope (p. xxv–xxvi). This approach drops the subjective method of the new way of ideas (still used, Popper notes, by Kant) and studies scientific discussions and problem-situations, helping us understand the history of scientific thought (p. xxvi).

**Closing (p. xxvi).** Popper has argued that epistemology's key traditional problems — those of knowledge's growth — transcend both linguistic-analysis methods and require analysis of scientific knowledge. But he does not want to found a new dogma: even philosophy of science risks becoming a fashion, a specialism, yet "philosophers should not be specialists." His own motive is wanting to learn something about "the riddle of the world" and about the riddle of man's knowledge of that world; only a revival of interest in these riddles, he believes, can save science and philosophy from narrow specialization and from an obscurantist faith in expert authority suited to a "post-rationalist" and "post-critical" age (p. xxvi). Signed Penn, Buckinghamshire, Spring 1958.

## Toy example (ours)
Suppose two epistemologists tackle the question "why did the apple fall?"
- **Ordinary-language move:** analyse the sentence "I saw the apple fall" — what does "saw" mean here, is "fall" used literally? This clarifies the sentence but never tests any explanation of falling.
- **Model-language move:** build a tiny artificial language with three symbols (apple, ground, near) and rules for combining them. It can say "apple near ground" but has no way to express a rate, a mass, or an equation — so it cannot state Newton's law at all.
- **Popper's "one method":** state the problem clearly ("what law explains the acceleration of falling bodies?"), propose a solution (constant acceleration $g$), then try hard to refute it — e.g. check whether a feather falls at the same rate in air. The failure (feather falls slower) forces a revision (air resistance), which is itself then open to further criticism. This is the growth of knowledge Popper means: not clarifying words, and not a toy symbol-system, but proposing and attacking real explanatory claims.

## Concepts
- [[growth-of-knowledge]] (new) — Popper's claim that epistemology's central, lasting problem is how knowledge grows, best studied via the growth of scientific knowledge (p. xix)
- [[critical-method]] (new) — the one method of all rational discussion: state a problem clearly, then try as hard as possible to refute one's own proposed solution (p. xix)
- [[new-way-of-words]] (new) — the shift from Locke/Berkeley/Hume's method of analysing ideas and their sensory origin (the "new way of ideas") to analysing words, propositions and usages instead of ideas and thoughts (p. xxi)
- [[model-language]] (new) — Popper's term for artificial constructed languages meant to represent the language of science; he argues all three he examines are too poor to express real science (p. xxiii–xxv)
- [[self-reference-in-paradoxes]] (new) — the feature Popper says all logical paradoxes share and all philosophical paradoxes lack (p. xx)
- [[analysis]] — language analysts' method of clarifying puzzling expressions by breaking them into clearer parts; Popper grants it a role but denies it defines philosophy (p. xix–xx)
