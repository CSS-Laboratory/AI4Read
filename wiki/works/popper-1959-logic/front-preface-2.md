# Preface to the First English Edition, 1959 (with the 1934 preface)

## L0 — One line
Popper argues that philosophy has at least one genuine problem, understanding the world including our knowledge, and he does not think the study of the growth of knowledge can be replaced by the study of linguistic usages or language systems; the method he would call "the one method of philosophy" is critical discussion of clearly stated problems, which belongs to all rational discussion.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Popper's 1959 preface states his attitude to language analysts (philosophers who study word usage and meaning). They hold that philosophy has no genuine problems, or only linguistic ones. Popper holds that it has at least one: understanding the world, our knowledge included. He takes the central problem of epistemology (the study of knowledge) to be the growth of knowledge, best studied through science. He denies any method peculiar to philosophy; a method he would call "the one method of philosophy" belongs to all rational discussion: state problems clearly, criticise solutions. He judges ordinary-language analysis too narrow, and artificial model languages too poor, to reach the growth of knowledge. He favours analysing scientific problems and discussions.

**Prerequisites:** No technical background. Names of philosophers are given as in the source. Terms are defined in L2/L3.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Philosophy lacks an accepted problem-situation (the generally accepted set of open problems of a field).** In 1934 Popper contrasts the scientist, who meets an organized structure, with the philosopher, who meets something like a heap of ruins. That no accepted problem-situation exists is, he says, perhaps the one generally accepted fact. Those who believe philosophy can pose genuine problems but accept no existing creed can only begin afresh (pp. xv–xvi).
- **Against language analysts** (philosophers who study the usage and meaning of words), Popper holds that there is at least one genuine philosophical problem, cosmology (understanding the world, ourselves and our knowledge included) (p. xviii).
- **Two theses:** there is no method peculiar to philosophy; and the central problem of epistemology has always been the growth of knowledge, best studied through the growth of scientific knowledge. He does not think this can be replaced by the study of linguistic usages or language systems (p. xix).
- **A method common to all rational discussion:** Popper is ready to admit a method that might be called "the one method of philosophy", but it belongs to all rational discussion: state the problem clearly and examine proposed solutions critically. He equates the rational and the critical attitude (p. xix).
- **Why linguistic analysis seemed the true method:** among several historical reasons he discusses two. One is the correct belief that logical paradoxes need linguistic analysis. The other, which seems to have been the main reason, is the wish to replace Locke's new way of ideas by a more objective analysis of words (pp. xx–xxi).
- **Gravest objection:** most problems about the growth of knowledge must transcend any study confined to common-sense knowledge. The most important problems of epistemology remain invisible to those who analyse only ordinary knowledge or ordinary language (pp. xxi–xxii).
- **Model languages (artificial formal languages built as models of scientific language) are too poor:** the first lacks identity and so arithmetic, the second works only without the usual arithmetic theorems, the third cannot formulate mathematics. They yield only a crude, misleading model of growth, an accumulating heap of observation statements (reports of observations) (pp. xxiv–xxv).
- **The approach Popper favours:** analyse scientific problems, theories and discussions, since scientific knowledge is common-sense knowledge writ large. He adds that he does not want to advocate another dogma, and that philosophy of science threatens to become a specialism (pp. xxv–xxvi).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Preface to the first edition, 1934 (pp. xv–xvi)
- Epigraphs: Schlick says the philosophic connoisseur cannot help fearing that philosophy will never pose a genuine problem. Kant, holding the opposite view, says that whenever a dispute has raged for long, especially in philosophy, there was at the bottom never a problem about mere words but always a genuine problem about things (p. xv).
- A scientist, say in physics, can attack his problem at once: an organized structure of doctrines and a generally accepted problem-situation already exist. He may leave it to others to fit his contribution into the framework (p. xv).
- The philosopher faces something resembling a heap of ruins, perhaps with treasure buried underneath. That there is no generally accepted problem-situation is perhaps the one fact generally accepted. It has become a recurrent question whether philosophy will ever pose a genuine problem (pp. xv–xvi).
- Some still believe philosophy can pose genuine problems about things, and hope to get them discussed and to have done with the depressing monologues that now pass for philosophical discussion. If by chance they cannot accept any existing creed, all they can do is begin afresh (p. xvi).

*Intuition note.* Science has a shared map of open problems; philosophy, Popper says, lacks one. The only course he states is conditional: those who believe philosophy can pose problems but accept no existing creed can only begin afresh.

### Acton epigraph (p. xvii)
Lord Acton: nothing is more necessary to the man of science than its history and the logic of discovery: how error is detected, the use of hypothesis and imagination, the mode of testing (p. xvii). In the acknowledgments Popper thanks David Nicholls for the passage, and says the reprint gave him the opportunity to quote it (p. xxvii).

### 1959 preface: aims (p. xviii)
- Popper says the 1934 preface was, he fears, too brief. He now explains his attitude to the present situation and to the two main schools of language analysts today (p. xviii).
- Analysts matter to him as opponents and as allies, in so far as they seem to be almost the only philosophers left who keep alive some traditions of rational philosophy (p. xviii).
- **Analysts' view:** there are no genuine philosophical problems, or such problems, if any, concern linguistic usage or word meaning. **Popper's view:** there is at least one, the problem of cosmology, in which all thinking men are interested. He believes all science is cosmology, and for him the interest of philosophy, no less than of science, lies solely in its contributions to it (p. xviii).
- Understanding the functions of language is an important part of that task; explaining problems away as merely linguistic puzzles is not (p. xviii).

### Two theses and the method (pp. xix–xx)
- **Thesis 1:** philosophers are as free as others to use any method in searching for truth; no method is peculiar to philosophy (p. xix).
- **Thesis 2:** the central problem of epistemology has always been, and still is, the growth of knowledge, best studied through the growth of scientific knowledge. Popper does not think it can be replaced by the study of linguistic usages or language systems (p. xix).
- He is ready to admit a method that might be described as "the one method of philosophy". It is not peculiar to philosophy but is the one method of all rational discussion, natural sciences included: state the problem clearly and examine proposed solutions critically (p. xix).
- He equates the rational and the critical attitude. We should try as hard as we can to overthrow our own solution rather than defend it; others will supply criticism if we do not. Criticism will be fruitful only if the problem is stated clearly and the solution put in a sufficiently definite form (p. xix).
- He does not deny that something which may be called logical analysis can play a role in clarifying and scrutinizing problems and proposed solutions, nor assert that such methods are necessarily useless. His thesis is that they are far from the only useful ones and no more characteristic of philosophy than of any rational inquiry (p. xix).
- He is not interested in listing other methods, so long as the philosopher has an interesting problem and sincerely tries to solve it (pp. xix–xx).
- One method he mentions is a variant of the unfashionable historical method: find out what others thought and said about the problem, why they faced it, how they formulated it and tried to solve it. It is part of rational discussion. If we ignore what others think or thought, rational discussion must come to an end. He fears that philosophizing by talking to oneself may be a symptom of that decline (p. xx).

*Intuition note.* For Popper, a shared method means trying to refute proposals, and criticism needs clear and definite statements. Language analysis may help with clarity, but he says it is not the only tool.

### Why linguistic analysis seemed the true method (pp. xx–xxi)
- Popper says there are several historical reasons for the widespread belief; he discusses two (p. xx).
- **Reason 1: logical paradoxes.** He calls correct the belief that logical paradoxes (the liar, "I am now lying"; those of Russell, Richard and others) need linguistic analysis for their solution, with its distinction between meaningful ("well-formed") and meaningless expressions (p. xx).
- This correct belief is then combined with the mistaken belief that the traditional problems of philosophy arise from philosophical paradoxes of analogous structure. Popper says this mistake can be shown very easily, by logical analysis: a characteristic self-reference present in all logical paradoxes is absent from all so-called philosophical paradoxes, even Kant's antinomies (p. xx).
- **Reason 2, which seems to have been the main one (p. xxi).** Popper describes the new way of ideas of Locke, Berkeley and Hume (analysing ideas and their origin in the senses) as a psychological, or rather pseudo-psychological, method. It was felt that it should be replaced by a more objective, less genetic analysis of words, usages, propositions and sentences. Popper readily admits that this was an advance, and an urgently needed one (p. xxi).
- He finds it understandable that those who once saw the new way of ideas as the one true method may thus have turned to the belief that the *new way of words* is. He strongly dissents and makes two comments (p. xxi).
- **First comment:** the new way of ideas should never have been taken as the main method of philosophy, let alone its one true method. Locke introduced it merely for preliminaries to a science of ethics. Berkeley and Hume used it chiefly as a weapon for harrying opponents. Berkeley did not base his religious views on it, nor Hume his political theories, though Hume based his determinism on it (p. xxi).

### The gravest objection (pp. xxi–xxii)
- Epistemology may be approached from (1) ordinary or common-sense knowledge, or (2) scientific knowledge. Popper says those who favour (1) rightly think scientific knowledge can only be an extension of common-sense knowledge, but wrongly think common-sense knowledge is easier to analyse. They then analyse ordinary language, e.g. the phrases "I see", "I know", or even "perhaps" (pp. xxi–xxii).
- **Popper's reply:** he agrees that scientific knowledge is merely a development of common-sense knowledge. But the most important and exciting problems of epistemology must remain completely invisible to those who confine themselves to analysing ordinary knowledge or its formulation in ordinary language (p. xxii).
- Example: the growth of knowledge. Most problems connected with it must necessarily transcend any study confined to common-sense knowledge. The most important way common-sense knowledge grows is by turning into scientific knowledge. It seems clear that growth of scientific knowledge is the most important and interesting case of growth (p. xxii).
- Almost all problems of traditional epistemology are connected with growth. He is inclined to say even more: from Plato to Descartes, Leibniz, Kant, Duhem and Poincaré, and from Bacon, Hobbes and Locke to Hume, Mill and Russell, the theory of knowledge was inspired by the hope of contributing to the advance of scientific knowledge. The only possible exception he can think of is Berkeley (p. xxii).
- Most philosophers who take ordinary-language analysis as philosophy's characteristic method seem to have lost this optimism; their attitude, it seems, has become one of resignation, if not despair. They not only leave advancement to scientists, it seems, but define philosophy so that it cannot contribute to knowledge of the world. Popper says the self-mutilation this requires does not appeal to him (p. xxii).

*Intuition note.* Popper's point is that common-sense knowledge grows most importantly by becoming scientific knowledge. A study confined to common-sense knowledge must therefore miss most problems of growth. He claims this limits the first approach.

### Definition of philosophy and cosmology (p. xxiii)
- There is no essence of philosophy to be distilled into a definition. A definition of the word can only be a convention. He sees no merit in an arbitrary definition that may well prevent a student from contributing to our knowledge of the world (p. xxiii).
- He finds it paradoxical that analysts of ordinary language believe they know enough cosmology to be sure it differs in essence from philosophy, so that philosophy cannot contribute. He says they are mistaken: purely metaphysical, and therefore philosophical, ideas have been of the greatest importance for cosmology (p. xxiii).
- His examples run from Thales to Einstein: ancient atomism, Descartes on matter, forces (Gilbert, Newton, Leibniz, Boscovic) and fields of force (Faraday, Einstein) (p. xxiii).
- Conclusion: analysing knowledge through ordinary language is too narrow and bound to miss the most interesting problems (p. xxiii).

### Two groups within the scientific approach (pp. xxiii–xxvi)
Popper is far from agreeing with all who analyse scientific knowledge. He sorts them into two groups, jokingly the goats and the sheep (p. xxiii).

**Group 1: model-language builders (pp. xxiii–xxv).**
- Their aim is to study *the language of science* by constructing artificial model languages (p. xxiii).
- They too replace the pseudo-psychological method by linguistic analysis. Popper suggests, with "perhaps", that the hope for "exact" or "formalized" knowledge explains why they choose the language of science rather than ordinary language. There seems to be no such language, so they construct one. Full-scale models being difficult, they build miniature ones (p. xxiv).
- In Popper's opinion they get the worst of both worlds. They miss the most exciting problems, those of advancing knowledge. The intricacy of the outfit bears no relation to its effectiveness, and practically no scientific theory of any interest can be expressed in these systems (p. xxiv).
- Of the three most widely known model languages, as Popper describes them (pp. xxiv–xxv):

| Language | Limit |
|---|---|
| First | lacks even identity, so cannot express an equation, not even primitive arithmetic |
| Second | works only as long as we do not add the means to prove the usual arithmetic theorems (e.g. no greatest prime; every number has a successor) |
| Third (most elaborate and famous) | mathematics cannot be formulated; no measurable properties are expressible |

- For these and many other reasons the three are too poor to be of use to any science, and essentially poorer than ordinary languages, even primitive ones (p. xxv).
- The limits were imposed because otherwise the authors' solutions would not have worked. Popper says this is easily proved and partly proved by the authors themselves (p. xxv).
- The authors seem to claim (a) that their methods can solve problems of the theory of scientific knowledge, and (b) that the methods are exact. Popper replies that they are applicable with any precision only to discourse of an extremely primitive kind, and that the two claims cannot both be upheld (p. xxv).
- So the method of artificial model languages is incapable of tackling the growth of knowledge, and even less able to do so than analysis of ordinary languages, because these languages are poorer. It yields only the most crude and most misleading model of growth: an accumulating heap of observation statements (p. xxv).

**Group 2: no chosen method (pp. xxiii–xxvi).**
- Its members do not confine themselves to the language of science or any language. They philosophize in many ways because they have many problems (pp. xxiii–xxiv).
- Popper turns to this last group in the preface. They analyse scientific problems, theories and procedures and, most important, scientific discussions. The group can claim among its ancestors almost all the great philosophers of the West. It can even claim Berkeley, although in an important sense he was an enemy of rational scientific knowledge and feared its advance (p. xxv).
- Its most important representatives of the last two hundred years were Kant, Whewell, Mill, Peirce, Duhem, Poincaré, Meyerson, Russell and, at least in some phases, Whitehead (p. xxv).
- Most would agree that scientific knowledge results from the growth of common-sense knowledge. All discovered that it can be more easily studied, because it is "common-sense knowledge writ large, as it were". Example: it replaces the Humean problem of reasonable belief by the problem of reasons for accepting or rejecting scientific theories (pp. xxv–xxvi).
- We have detailed reports of the discussions on whether Newton's, Maxwell's or Einstein's theory should be accepted. These act like a microscope for studying problems of reasonable belief in detail and objectively (p. xxvi).
- This approach, like the other two, drops the pseudo-psychological or "subjective" method of the new way of ideas (still used by Kant). It suggests analysing scientific discussions and problem situations, and so can help us understand the history of scientific thought (p. xxvi).

*Intuition note.* Popper treats science as everyday reasoning under a microscope: its discussions are recorded, so they can be studied objectively. He faults the model-language builders because their formal systems lack or cannot prove ordinary arithmetic and other mathematics.

### Closing (p. xxvi)
- Popper says he has tried to show that the most important traditional epistemological problems, those about the growth of knowledge, transcend the two standard methods of linguistic analysis and require analysis of scientific knowledge (p. xxvi).
- He does not want to advocate another dogma. The philosophy of science is threatening to become a fashion and a specialism, yet philosophers should not be specialists. He is interested in science and philosophy only because he wants to learn about the riddle of the world and of man's knowledge of it (p. xxvi).
- Only a revival of interest in these riddles can, he believes, save the sciences and philosophy from narrow specialization and from an obscurantist faith in the expert's special skill, personal knowledge and authority. Such a faith suits our post-rationalist and post-critical age, which is proudly dedicated to destroying the tradition of rational philosophy and of rational thought itself (p. xxvi).

### Acknowledgments, 1960 and 1968 (p. xxvii)

The second English edition adds four short Addenda to the appendices, with corrected minor mistakes and a few linguistic improvements. Lakatos, Miller and Musgrave reported misprints and suggested index entries. Paul Bernays checked the axiomatization of the probability calculus, especially appendix *v. Popper keeps sole responsibility for any mistake (p. xxvii).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show why a definite claim can be overthrown while a vague one is hard to overthrow (compare p. xix).
- Vague claim: "Heavy things fall fast." It is so vague that almost no timing result clearly contradicts it.
- Definite claim: "A 2 kg ball dropped from 20 m lands at least 0.5 s before a 1 kg ball dropped at the same moment."
- Suppose a test gives landing times 2.0 s (2 kg ball) and 2.0 s (1 kg ball). The time gain is 2.0 − 2.0 = 0 s. Since 0 < 0.5, the definite claim is overthrown.
- A critic could do this only because the claim was stated definitely.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Model languages too poor for science ↔ computational modelling: a formal model may gain precision by losing the ability to express real theories.
- [structural-analogy] Heap-of-observation-statements growth model ↔ naive data accumulation: contrasts accumulating data with revising theories.
- [metaphor] Scientific discussions as a microscope ↔ history/sociology of science: recorded debates as data on reasonable belief.

## Questions this raises
- What exactly makes a solution "sufficiently definite" for criticism, and how is that tested?
- Does the growth of knowledge show a structure (e.g. replacement of theories) that the heap-of-observations model misses?
- How far does the claim that scientific knowledge is common-sense knowledge writ large hold, given Popper's own point that the study of ordinary knowledge misses most growth problems?
- Is Popper's treatment of the analysts' and model-language builders' views fair, or only a summary of their weaknesses?
