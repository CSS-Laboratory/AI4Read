# Appendix *xii — The Experiment of Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen

## L0 — One line
In a 1935 letter, Einstein rejects Popper's imaginary "super-pure case" experiment, argues that the quantum ψ-function is not a complete description of a single system, and says statistical conclusions can come from a deterministic theory plus ignorance.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Popper prints Einstein's letter of 11 Sept 1935. It covers three topics, which I put as questions. Does Popper's experiment of section 77 work? Einstein says no, because a filter must "smear" position. Is the ψ-function a complete description of a physical system? Einstein, using the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen set-up, says no. Can a deterministic theory yield statistical conclusions? Einstein says yes, as in classical statistical mechanics, when the initial state is unknown. Popper calls the letter a brief and decisive disposal of his experiment; on the third point he still disagrees: he holds that we draw such conclusions from conjectures about equidistribution (guesses that the possible cases are evenly spread), and so from probabilistic premises.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a quantum state (ψ-function) and of measuring position or momentum; elementary probability; Popper's section 77 and appendix *xi (not restated here).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Einstein "largely" agrees with Popper's paper but does not believe that a "super-pure case" (one allowing a photon's position and momentum to be predicted with "inadmissible" precision) can be produced by a shutter plus filters (p. 482).
- His reason: a filter that selects one quasi-monochromatic (nearly single-colour) wave-group gives it spatial extension, so it necessarily smears position (p. 482).
- Einstein dislikes the fashionable "positivistic" clinging to the observable. He agrees with Popper that theory is invented, not fabricated from observations (p. 482).
- EPR argument: measurements on A yield different ψ-functions for B, though it is unreasonable to assume that B's physical state depends on a measurement on the separated system A. So ψ is not a complete description (p. 483).
- Einstein's reality criterion: what can be predicted by a freely chosen measurement that does not interfere with the system must exist in reality. Einstein says one can hardly avoid concluding that B has definite position and momentum (p. 484).
- Einstein expects the present statistical description to be a passing phase (p. 484).
- Einstein and Popper disagree about statistical conclusions. Einstein: they follow from a deterministic theory plus ignorance of the initial state. Popper: we draw such conclusions from equidistribution conjectures, hence from probabilistic premises (pp. 481, 484).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The unit has no numbered sections. I follow the order of the letter.

**Popper's introduction (p. 481).** The letter "disposes of" Popper's imaginary experiment of section 77. It also describes the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen (EPR) experiment (*Physical Review* 47, 1935). Between these two topics are remarks on theory and experiment and on positivism in quantum theory. The last two paragraphs concern subjective probabilities and "drawing statistical conclusions from nescience" (ignorance). Popper says he still disagrees with Einstein there. Popper believes we draw such conclusions from conjectures about equidistribution (often natural, perhaps not consciously made), and so from probabilistic premises (p. 481).

**Presentation (p. 482).** Einstein's executors asked that the original text be published with any translation. Popper therefore reproduces the handwritten letter (pp. 489–92 in the book). The translation contains bracketed insertions, some of which give the German word.

**1. Popper's section 77 experiment (p. 482).** *Einstein:* he "largely" agrees with Popper's paper. A footnote (unattributed in the source; I read it as Einstein's) gives the main point: ψ characterizes a statistical aggregate of systems rather than one single system. The footnote says this view makes it unnecessary to distinguish between "pure" and "non-pure" cases (p. 482, n. x).
- He does not believe a "super-pure case" is possible. Such a case would allow predicting a photon's position and momentum (colour) with "inadmissible" precision (p. 482).
- Popper's means, a fast-shutter screen with selective glass filters, he holds to be ineffective in principle (p. 482).
- His argument: take a short light signal (precise position) and analyse it into quasi-monochromatic wave-trains $W_n$. Let the filters cut out all but $W_1$. This wave-group is quasi-monochromatic, so it has considerable spatial extension. The filter therefore necessarily smears position (p. 482). Earlier he said such a filter would smear position "just like a spectroscopic grid" (p. 482).

*Intuition note:* Einstein's point is that a wave-group of nearly one colour has considerable spatial extension. So a filter that keeps only one such group turns a short signal into a long one: on his argument, selecting a colour costs precision of position.

**2. Positivism and theory (p. 482).** Einstein dislikes the "positivistic" tendency to cling to the observable. He calls it trivial that atomic magnitudes cannot be predicted with any desired precision. Like Popper, he thinks theory cannot be fabricated from observations but can only be invented (p. 482).

**3. The EPR experiment (pp. 482–484).**
- *Question (p. 482–483):* is the statistical character of experimental findings merely the result of interfering with a system from without (measuring it), while the systems themselves, described by ψ, behave deterministically? Einstein says Heisenberg "flirts" with this view without adopting it consistently. He also puts the question thus: should ψ, whose changes in time are deterministic by Schrödinger's equation, be regarded as a complete description of physical reality, with the (insufficiently known) interference from without then alone responsible for the merely statistical character of predictions? The answer "at which we arrive" (Einstein reporting the joint paper) is that ψ should not be regarded as a complete description of the physical state of a system (p. 483).
- *Set-up (p. 483):* a composite system of parts A and B interacts briefly. The ψ-function of the composite is known before the interaction (e.g. a collision of two free particles). Schrödinger's equation then gives it after the interaction.
- An optimal measurement is made on A afterwards. It can be done in various ways, e.g. momentum or position. Quantum mechanics then gives ψ for B, and different ψ-functions for different measurements on A (p. 483).
- *Step:* Einstein finds it unreasonable that B's physical state depends on a measurement on A once A is separated from B. So two different ψ-functions belong to one and the same physical state of B (p. 483).
- *Step:* a complete description must be unambiguous, apart from superficialities such as units and coordinates. So ψ is not a complete description (p. 483).
- *Orthodox reply, as Einstein anticipates it:* there is no complete description, only a statistical description of an aggregate. Einstein answers that the orthodox theorist ought to say so clearly. He does not believe we shall have to be satisfied for ever with so "loose and flimsy" a description (pp. 483–484).
- *Further point (p. 484):* some precise predictions for B, depending on the freely chosen measurement on A, may be related as position and momentum measurements are. Einstein says one can "hardly avoid" concluding that B has a definite momentum and a definite position. Reason: if, upon freely choosing to do so [the translation adds: that is, without interfering with it], I am able to predict something, then this something must exist in reality. He thinks a description that is statistical in principle can only be a passing phase (p. 484).

*Intuition note:* The incompleteness step assumes it is unreasonable for B's state to depend on what is done to the separated A, so different ψ-functions for B must belong to one state. A further step, the reality criterion, says that what can be predicted without interfering exists in reality. Einstein draws from it that B has definite momentum and position.

**4. Statistical conclusions from a deterministic theory (p. 484).** Einstein repeats an earlier objection (an allusion to a previous letter, per Popper's note). He does not believe Popper is right that statistical conclusions cannot be derived from a deterministic theory. He cites classical statistical mechanics (gas theory, Brownian movement). His example: a material point moves with constant velocity in a closed circle. One can calculate the probability of finding it, at a given time, within a given part of the periphery. What is essential is only that one does not know the initial state, or does not know it precisely (p. 484).

*Intuition note:* In Einstein's example the probability comes from not knowing the initial state, while the motion itself is deterministic. In his introduction (p. 481) Popper says he still disagrees: he holds that such conclusions are drawn from conjectures about equidistribution, and so from probabilistic premises.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A point moves at constant speed on a circle of circumference 360 units, completing one lap per hour. Its start is unknown. Suppose we conjecture that all starting positions are equally likely (Popper's "equidistribution" premise). At a given time, the position is the start shifted by a fixed amount. So it is also uniform. The arc from 0 to 90 then has probability $90/360 = 1/4$. The motion is fully deterministic. The number 1/4 comes from ignorance plus the uniformity premise. If the premise were dropped, no number would follow.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Popper's equidistribution premise ↔ probability theory: a prior that is uniform over unknown states — both turn ignorance into probabilities only through an added assumption.
- [metaphor] Einstein's "theory can only be invented" ↔ falsifiability: both reject theory built by induction from observation.

## Questions this raises
