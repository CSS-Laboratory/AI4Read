# On Objective Disorder or Randomness

## L0 — One line
Popper holds that randomness can be described objectively as a type of order, by constructing "ideally random" infinite sequences whose every beginning segment is as random as its length allows.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can disorder be characterised objectively, or is it only our ignorance of some order? Statistical tests only check for specific regularities, which tempts one to say randomness is subjective. Popper resists this. Von Mises' definition of randomness allows a sequence to start very regularly and become random only "in the end", and such deferred randomness cannot be tested. Popper instead constructs sequences whose finite initial segments are random-like from the start. Such sequences can be shown to have frequency limits, so the limit axiom can be dropped. In 1967 he no longer thinks a difficulty-free frequency theory important, but still values modelling randomness as a type of order. He also notes that these sequences satisfy his earlier formal probability systems.

**Prerequisites:** infinite 0/1 sequences and relative frequency; limits of sequences; the idea of a frequency theory of probability (probability as long-run relative frequency; a gambling system is a rule that picks a subsequence in advance; entropy means molecular disorder; the limit axiom is an axiom about frequency limits, which Popper eliminates) (von Mises' "collective").

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- An objective theory of probability, and its use for entropy, needs an objective characterisation of randomness as a type of order (p. 369).
- Statistical tests check only given, specific regularities. No test excludes all regularity, which makes "randomness = ignorance of order" tempting. Popper rejects that view (pp. 369–370).
- Von Mises' no-gambling-system criterion allows a "collective" to start extremely regularly. Such deferred randomness cannot be tested empirically (p. 370).
- Dilemma: "from the very beginning" seems to need long sequences, which seems to retract "from the very beginning". Popper's solution is the ideally random sequence, whose degree of randomness grows with length as fast as mathematically possible (p. 371).
- Such sequences have frequency limits, so the limit axiom is eliminated by reducing limit behaviour to finite random-like behaviour (p. 371).
- Popper's aim differs from that of von Mises, Copeland, Wald and Church, who defined randomness by excluding "all" gambling systems. Their admissible sequences are not necessarily ideal in his sense (pp. 371–372).
- He now finds the measure-theoretical approach preferable (its decisive point is tied to the propensity interpretation) and no longer thinks eliminating the limit axiom very important. In the 1967 addendum he still thinks it important that randomness can be characterised as a type of order (p. 372).
- Sets of such sequences satisfy the formal system of appendices *iv, *v and *ii; for *iv and *v the one stated exception is Postulate 1 (pp. 372–373).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Aim and the problem (points 1–4, pp. 369–370).**
- Popper says an objective probability theory, applied to entropy (molecular disorder), needs an objective description of disorder as a type of order (p. 369).
- Examples of supposed randomness: molecular velocities in an equilibrium gas (very nearly), the distribution of nebulae, and rain on Sundays. In the rain case, rain on Wednesday may not help predict rain on Sunday (p. 369).
- Randomness can be called "absence of regularity", but that is not helpful. There are no tests for regularity in general, only for some given, specific regularity. For example, one can test the formula "rain at least once in three weeks" and reject it, but one cannot test whether some better formula exists (pp. 369–370).
- **Temptation (point 4):** randomness is not an objectively describable type of order and must be read as our lack of knowledge of the order prevailing, if any order prevails. Popper thinks this temptation should be resisted, and that a theory can be developed which allows us to construct ideal types of disorder, of order, and of all degrees between them (p. 370).

**Simplest problem (point 5, p. 370).**
- He believes he has solved the construction of a one-dimensional ideal type of disorder, an ideally disordered sequence. The problem arises from any frequency theory that uses infinite sequences (p. 370).

**Von Mises' criterion and its flaw (points 6–7, pp. 370–371).**
- **Von Mises' view (as Popper reports it):** a 0/1 sequence with equidistribution is random if it admits no gambling system. A gambling system is a way to select in advance a subsequence with unequal distribution. A system may work "accidentally" for a time but it is postulated that it breaks down in the long run, more precisely in an infinite number of trials (p. 370).
- Consequence: such a sequence may start very regularly, for example 00 11 00 11 … for the first five hundred million places, as long as it becomes irregular in the end (p. 370).
- This deferred randomness cannot be tested empirically. Whenever we test randomness, we have in mind a sequence that is "reasonably random-like" from the very beginning (pp. 370–371).
- Difficulty: is 010110 random-like? It is too short to say. But if we need a long sequence to decide, we seem to retract "from the very beginning" (p. 371).

**Solution (points 8–10, p. 371).**
- Construct an ideally random sequence. For every beginning segment, short or long, it is as random as the length permits. Its degree $n$ of randomness (its $n$-freedom from after-effects) grows with length as fast as is mathematically possible (p. 371).
- The construction is given in appendix *iv, note *1, with a reference to an unpublished paper by L. R. B. Elton and Popper (p. 371).
- The infinite set of all such sequences is the "ideal type of random alternatives with equal distribution" (p. 371).
- Only strong randomness is postulated: finite initial segments pass all randomness tests. Yet these sequences can easily be shown to have frequency limits. So the limit axiom of his probability chapter is eliminated, by reducing limit-like behaviour to random-like behaviour in finite segments (p. 371).

*Intuition note:* Von Mises asks for randomness "in the end", which no finite test can check. Popper asks for randomness at every stage, so finite segments are what must pass the tests. Frequency limits are then shown to follow from this (point 10), instead of being assumed.

**Extensions (point 11, p. 371).**
- To go in both directions, assign the odd-numbered elements to the positive places (first, second, …) and the even-numbered elements to the negative places. Similar well-known methods extend the construction to the cells of an $n$-dimensional space (p. 371).

**Comparison with other frequency theorists (point 12, pp. 371–372).**
- Von Mises, Copeland, Wald and Church wanted to exclude "all" gambling systems in the widest sense for which random sequences can be proved to exist (pp. 371–372).
- Popper's aim differs. He wanted to answer the objection that randomness is compatible with any finite initial segment. He also wanted sequences that arise from random-like finite sequences by a transition to infinity. He hoped thereby to keep close to sequences that would pass statistical tests and to prove the limit theorem, and says both have now been done with the construction of appendix iv (p. 372).
- He now finds the measure-theoretical approach preferable, for mathematical and philosophical reasons. The decisive point is connected with the propensity interpretation (Postscript). So he no longer thinks eliminating the limit axiom is very important (p. 372).
- Still, a frequency theory can be built on the ideal random sequences. An empirical sequence is then random to the extent that tests show its statistical similarity to an ideal sequence (p. 372).
- The sequences admitted by the other authors are not necessarily of this kind. Any sequence ever rejected by statistical tests as non-random may later turn into an admissible random sequence in their sense (p. 372).

**Addendum, 1967 (points 13–14, pp. 372–373).**
- Popper no longer believes it is important that a frequency theory free of the old difficulties can be built. He still thinks it important that randomness can be characterised as a type of order and that models of random sequences can be constructed (p. 372).
- **Claim (14):** let $S$ be any set of random 0/1 sequences $a = a_1, a_2, \dots$; $b = b_1, b_2, \dots$, such that for $a \neq b$ the sequences $a$ and $b$ are independent (and so $ab$ is random). Let $S$ contain the all-0 and the all-1 sequences. Define (p. 373):
$$p(a,b)=\lim\frac{\sum a_n b_n}{\sum b_n},\qquad p(ab,c)=\lim\frac{\sum a_n b_n c_n}{\sum c_n},$$
$$p(\bar a,b)=\lim\frac{\sum (1-a_n)\,b_n}{\sum b_n},\qquad p(a)=\lim\frac{\sum a_n}{n}.$$
Then all Postulates and Axioms of appendices *iv and *v are satisfied, except Postulate 1 (cf. p. 353). Popper adds that the sequences also satisfy the formal system of the older appendix *ii (pp. 372–373).

*Intuition note:* Probabilities are defined as limiting relative frequencies. For $p(a,b)$ one counts how often $a$ is 1 among the places where $b$ is 1. With these definitions the sequences satisfy those postulates and axioms (except Postulate 1).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take 8 places and treat the finite sums as stand-ins for the limits.
- $a = 0,1,1,0,1,0,0,1$, so $\sum a_n = 4$.
- $b = 1,1,0,1,0,1,1,0$, so $\sum b_n = 5$.
- Products $a_n b_n = 0,1,0,0,0,0,0,0$, sum $=1$. So $p(a,b)=1/5$.
- $1-a_n = 1,0,0,1,0,1,1,0$. Times $b_n$: $1,0,0,1,0,1,1,0$, sum $=4$. So $p(\bar a,b)=4/5$. Check: $1/5+4/5=1$.
- With $u$ = the all-1 sequence, $p(a,u)=4/8=1/2=p(a)$ (finite, $n=8$). (Our observation: with the all-1 sequence as $b$, $p(a,b)$ and $p(a)$ agree.)
- On the testing point: a "period 4" rule $x_n=x_{n-4}$ is right on all 8 of places 5–12 of 001100110011. A test of that one rule says nothing about other rules.

## Concepts
- [[randomness-as-type-of-order]] (new) — randomness as an objectively describable kind of order, not mere ignorance of order (p. 369)
- [[gambling-system]] (new) — a rule selecting in advance a subsequence with unequal distribution; von Mises' test of randomness (p. 370)
- [[deferred-randomness]] (new) — a sequence that is regular at the start and random only "in the end"; untestable (p. 370)
- [[ideally-random-sequence]] (new) — a sequence whose every beginning segment is as random as its length permits; $n$-freedom from after-effects grows as fast as possible (p. 371)
- [[limit-axiom-elimination]] (new) — deriving frequency limits from finite random-like behaviour instead of postulating them (p. 371)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Popper's $p(a,b)=\lim \sum a_nb_n/\sum b_n$ ↔ probability: conditional probability as a limiting relative frequency.
- [structural-analogy] Tests only check given, specific regularities ↔ falsifiability: one can refute a given formula but cannot refute "some regularity exists".
- [metaphor] Ideal type of disorder ↔ explication: replacing a vague concept (disorder) with a constructed precise one.
