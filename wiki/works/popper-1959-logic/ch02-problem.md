# On the Problem of a Theory of Scientific Method

## L0 — One line
Popper holds that science is defined by the rules (conventions) we adopt for treating theories, with the supreme rule that no statement may be protected from falsification, and not merely by the logical form of its statements.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What is a theory of scientific method, and what kind of thing are its rules? Popper answers that the rules are decisions or conventions, like the rules of chess. They are neither pure logic nor empirical facts about scientists. If they can be justified, it is by their consequences: fruitfulness and the contradictions they help to expose. Their supreme rule is that no scientific statement may be shielded from falsification. This links method to his criterion of demarcation. He rejects the positivist view that only logic and empirical science are meaningful. He also rejects "naturalistic" methodology, which treats method as a study of what scientists actually do. Many philosophical problems can be re-read as problems of method.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of falsifiability (a statement is falsifiable if possible observations could refute it) and the demarcation criterion (the test that separates empirical science from other things) from chapter 1 (section 6); positivists (philosophers who accept only logic and empirical science as meaningful); the difference between a logical rule and a practical rule.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Epistemology (the theory of knowledge) is identified with the theory of method, which concerns choices about how to treat scientific statements. These choices depend on the aim we choose (p. 27).
- Popper sees the distinguishing mark of empirical statements in their susceptibility to revision. A purely logical analysis of theories does not capture this (p. 28).
- No conclusive disproof of a theory is possible. Demanding strict proof or disproof means we never learn from experience (p. 28).
- Positivists treat philosophical problems as "pseudo-problems". Popper replies that this is easy if one fixes a narrow meaning of "meaning". The meaning criterion then becomes an unassailable dogma (pp. 29–30).
- Naturalistic methodology treats method as an empirical science of scientists' behaviour. Popper rejects it as uncritical, and doubts that empirical methods can settle its questions, since what counts as "science" is a matter of convention, and facts claimed there are really proposed conventions (p. 31).
- Methodological rules are conventions, "rules of the game". Two examples are given, and a supreme rule ties them together (pp. 32–33).
- The demarcation criterion is justified only by its consequences. The scientist can judge it by whether its consequences fit his intuitive idea of science's goal. The philosopher will accept it only if its consequences expose inconsistencies in older theories of knowledge (pp. 33–34).
- Some metaphysical doctrines, and the problem of objectivity, can be re-read as methodological rules (p. 34).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening.** Popper identifies epistemology (the logic of scientific discovery) with the theory of scientific method. Beyond logical analysis of relations between statements, method concerns the choice of methods, which are decisions about how to handle statements. These decisions depend on the aim chosen. Popper proposes rules that ensure the testability, i.e. the falsifiability, of scientific statements (p. 27).

### 9. Why methodological decisions are indispensable
- The answer to "what are methodological rules and why are they needed?" depends largely on one's attitude to science (p. 28).
  - **Positivists** see science as a system of statements meeting logical criteria such as meaningfulness or verifiability (p. 28).
  - **Popper** sees the mark of empirical statements in their susceptibility to revision: they can be criticised and superseded. His task is to analyse science's ability to advance and how a choice is made between conflicting theory systems (p. 28).
- He admits a purely logical analysis of theories is needed. But it does not elucidate what he prizes (p. 28).
- Dogmatic defence of a successful system such as classical mechanics, until it is "conclusively disproved", is the reverse of the critical attitude he thinks proper (p. 28).
- No conclusive disproof can ever be produced, because one may always say the results are unreliable or the discrepancies only apparent. Popper says this was used for Newtonian mechanics against Einstein, and is common in the social sciences (p. 28).
- Hence insisting on strict proof or strict disproof means never benefiting from experience (p. 28).
- Footnote *1 says the words "or strict disproof" were added to the text later. It says Popper has been misread as proposing a criterion of *meaning* based on "complete" falsifiability (p. 28, note *1).
- If science is characterised only by the formal structure of its statements, we cannot exclude metaphysics that arises by elevating an obsolete theory into incontrovertible truth (p. 29).
- So Popper characterises science by its methods: what we do with and to scientific systems. He will set out rules or norms guiding the scientist (p. 29).

*Intuition note.* A theory can always be defended by blaming the experiment. So insisting on strict proof or disproof would stop us learning from experience. Popper's alternative is a critical attitude in how we treat theories, which he expresses as rules.

### 10. The naturalistic approach to the theory of method
- **Positivist view, as Popper describes it.** Positivists dislike the idea of meaningful problems outside positive empirical science, or a genuine epistemology or methodology. They call such problems pseudo-problems. They state this as a fact, not as a proposal (p. 29).
- Footnote *1 says that before the book's first publication, members of the Vienna Circle objected that a theory of method that is neither empirical science nor pure logic is impossible, and that whatever lies outside those two fields is nonsense. Footnote *2 says some positivists have since changed this attitude (p. 29, notes *1, *2).
- **Popper's reply.** Nothing is easier than unmasking a problem as meaningless: fix a conveniently narrow meaning of "meaning". If only natural-science problems count as meaningful, debate about "meaning" itself turns out meaningless. The dogma is then, in Wittgenstein's words, "unassailable and definitive" (p. 30).
- Popper notes the dispute over whether philosophy has a right to exist is old. Defenders of traditional philosophy say its main problem is the critical analysis of the appeal to "experience". The positivist shrugs, because for him experience is a programme, not a problem (p. 30).
- Since positivists admit only tautologies and empirical statements, they will conclude that methodology, if not logic, must be an empirical science of scientists' behaviour. This view is **naturalistic** (p. 30).
- **Popper's position.** A naturalistic methodology has value, and a logician of science may learn from it. But methodology should not be taken for an empirical science (p. 31).
  - He doubts that empirical methods can settle whether science actually uses a principle of induction.
  - What counts as "science" or "scientist" must remain a matter of convention or decision (p. 31).
- His alternative is to compare two systems of rules, one with and one without a principle of induction. Then ask whether the principle can be applied without inconsistency, whether it helps, and whether it is needed (p. 31).
- He dispenses with induction not because science never uses it, but because he thinks it is not needed, does not help, and gives rise to inconsistencies (p. 31).
- He rejects the naturalistic view as uncritical. Its upholders, when they think they have found a fact, have only proposed a convention, which may harden into dogma (p. 31).
- Footnote 6 (added 1934, in proof): Popper's criticism no longer applies, "as far as I can see", to Carnap's *Logische Syntax* (1934), which also treats these questions as resting on decisions (p. 31, note 6).

*Intuition note.* "What is science?" is partly a choice, so counting what scientists do cannot settle it. Method can instead be discussed by comparing proposed systems of rules.

### 11. Methodological rules as conventions
- The rules are conventions, "rules of the game of empirical science". They differ from pure logic as chess rules do. Pure logic governs transformations of linguistic formulae, so an inquiry into chess might be called "The Logic of Chess" but hardly "Logic" simply (p. 32).
- **Two example rules** (p. 32):
  1. The game of science is in principle without end. Whoever decides that statements need no further test and are finally verified retires from the game.
  2. A hypothesis that has been proposed, tested and has proved its mettle may not be dropped without "good reason". Examples are replacement by a better testable hypothesis, or falsification of one of its consequences.
- Logic may set criteria for whether a statement is testable. It is not concerned with whether anyone exerts himself to test it (p. 32).
- Section 6 promised a methodological supplement to the falsifiability definition. Empirical science is defined by its methodological rules, as chess is by its rules (p. 32).
- **Supreme rule:** the other rules must be designed so that they do not protect any statement in science against falsification (p. 33).
- The connection between rules and the demarcation criterion is not strictly deductive. The rules are built to ensure the criterion can be applied, so they follow a practical rule of a higher type. Example: theories we decide never to test again would no longer be falsifiable (rule 1) (p. 33).
- This systematic connection justifies speaking of a theory of method. Its pronouncements are mostly fairly obvious conventions, and "profound truths" are not expected of it (p. 33).
- A later footnote says Popper still leans this way. He grants that theorems such as "degree of corroboration ≠ probability" are perhaps unexpected (p. 33, note *2).
- Method may still clarify the logical situation and solve intractable problems, e.g. whether to accept or reject a probability statement (section 68) (p. 33).
- **Justification.** He proposes the demarcation criterion only because it is fruitful. He cites Menger: definitions are dogmas, and only their conclusions give new insight (p. 33).
- The scientist can judge the definition by whether its consequences fit his intuitive idea of science's goal (p. 34).
- The philosopher will accept it only if its consequences expose inconsistencies in older theories of knowledge, trace them to their assumptions and conventions, and leave the new proposals free of the same difficulties (p. 34).
- This method of detecting and resolving contradictions is also used inside science. It is the way, "if by any", that methodological conventions might be justified (p. 34).
- Popper doubts philosophers will count this as philosophy, but says it matters little (p. 34).
- Some metaphysical doctrines can be read as "hypostatizations" (turning into a thing) of methodological rules. Examples are the principle of causality, discussed in the next section, and objectivity. Objectivity can be read as the rule that only inter-subjectively testable statements enter science (p. 34).
- He suggests that the majority, and the most interesting, of theoretical philosophy's problems can be re-interpreted as problems of method (p. 34).
- Footnote 3: an unpublished work traces problems of classical and modern epistemology (Hume via Kant to Russell and Whitehead) back to the problem of demarcation (p. 34, note 3).

*Intuition note.* Method rules are chosen to keep theories open to refutation, not deduced from logic. If they can be justified at all, it is by their consequences: whether they are fruitful and whether they expose inconsistencies in older theories of knowledge.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Hypothesis H: "this alloy melts at 100 °C". Three tests give 100.1, 99.9 and 100.0 °C, so H passes and is kept (rule 2: not dropped without good reason). A fourth test gives 140 °C.
- *Protective move (excluded by the supreme rule):* "the thermometer must be wrong", declared in advance to hold whatever happens.
- *Method-respecting move:* treat the result as a challenge. Our own added rule here: repeat the test 3 more times. Results 141, 139 and 140 reproduce the discrepancy (mean = (141+139+140)/3 = 140).
- A falsified consequence is now a "good reason" to drop H. Note that none of this was a *proof*. It was a decision under rules chosen to keep H falsifiable.

## Concepts
- [[falsifiability]] — a statement's openness to refutation by possible observations. Here it is the aim the methodological rules secure (p. 27).
- [[demarcation problem]] — separating empirical science from non-science. Here the criterion is justified by its fruitful consequences (p. 33).
- [[convention]] — a decision about rules. Popper's sense is a chosen rule of the "game of science", which differs from a rule of pure logic (p. 32).
- [[methodological-rules]] (new) — conventions governing how scientific statements are treated, e.g. keep testing, do not drop a tested hypothesis without good reason (p. 32).
- [[supreme-methodological-rule]] (new) — rules must not protect any statement from falsification (p. 33).
- [[naturalistic-methodology]] (new) — methodology treated as an empirical study of scientists' actual behaviour. Popper rejects it (p. 31).
- [[meaning-criterion-dogma]] (new) — Popper's charge that a narrow positivist criterion of meaning becomes immune to attack (p. 30).
- [[inter-subjective-testability]] (new) — objectivity read as a rule: only inter-subjectively testable statements enter science (p. 34).

## Canonical instances
- [[chess-analogy]] — methodological rules are like chess rules, not like pure logic (p. 32).
