# Universals, Dispositions, and Natural or Physical Necessity

## L0 — One line
Popper accepts that laws of nature form a category logically stronger than merely true universal statements, and defines natural necessity as deducibility from a statement function satisfied in all worlds that differ from ours, if at all, only in initial conditions. In his view this changes the metaphysics of laws, not his falsificationist method.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Popper asks what makes a universal statement a law, and what this shows about induction and experience. He argues that repetition presupposes a point of view, so repetitions cannot come first. He also argues that every universal term is dispositional (it describes law-like behaviour) and so goes beyond experience. He accepts Kneale's point that laws are logically stronger than the corresponding universal statements. He then defines natural necessity through variation of initial conditions, and from it the nomic conditional (if a then necessarily b). The change is ontological only: laws are still tested by attempted refutation. Laws so understood are, he says, incompatible with induction theories; the nomic conditional cannot rescue phenomenalism or operationalism.

**Prerequisites:** material implication and the truth-table conditional; universal vs singular statements; basic idea of logical probability p(a) and p(a,b); Popper's falsifiability. Terms used: ontological (about what the world is like); phenomenalism (talk about physical objects translated into talk about observations); operationalism (scientific terms defined by experimental procedures).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Repetition is always approximate similarity, and similarity is relative to a point of view. So interests and expectations come before repetitions, which defeats both the logical and the temporal "primacy of repetitions" (pp. 440–442).
- Almost every statement goes beyond experience, even *this swan here is white*, because of the word swan. Universals are dispositional, so there is no sharp line between empirical and theoretical language (pp. 443–445).
- Dispositional terms cannot be defined in observational terms (Carnap's "constitution" fails). They come in degrees of dispositionality (pp. 444–445).
- Popper's moa example, which he takes to capture Kneale's criticism: a true strictly universal statement may be merely accidental. So defining a law as just a strictly universal statement is logically insufficient (pp. 447–448).
- Natural necessity is different from logical necessity. Laws are necessary relative to singular facts but contingent relative to tautologies; the opposite view (laws not contingent at all) is, Popper says, a particularly severe form of essentialism, a view he criticizes elsewhere (pp. 449–452).
- Definition (N°) and the nomic conditional $a \xrightarrow[N]{} b$ capture natural necessity. A claim that a statement is naturally necessary can never be established positively, only refuted (pp. 453–455).
- The change is metaphysical, not methodological: no "uniformity" principle could justify induction (pp. 453, 458–459).
- The nomic conditional cannot rescue phenomenalism or operational definitions. Dispositional terms cannot be defined operationally (pp. 461–463).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### (1) The primacy of repetitions (pp. 440–443)
Popper says all theories of induction rest on the "primacy of repetitions". It has two variants (p. 440):
- **Logical primacy** (criticized by Hume): repeated instances somehow justify a universal law.
- **Temporal/psychological primacy** (upheld by Hume): repetitions need not justify anything, but they do arouse expectations and beliefs in us.

Popper holds that both are untenable, and gives two arguments.

**First argument.**
- All repetitions we experience are *approximate*: B repeats A only by being similar to A, not identical with it (p. 441).
- Similarity is always similarity *in certain respects*, such as shading, shape or size. Figures in his diagram are similar in one respect and dissimilar in another, and there is no end to the possible kinds of similarity (pp. 441–442).
- So similarity, and with it repetition, presupposes a point of view, interest or expectation. Therefore these are logically as well as temporally prior to repetition. This destroys both doctrines (p. 442).
- For any finite set of things, with some ingenuity we can find a point of view from which all of them are similar. So anything can be called a repetition of anything (p. 442).
- He compares this with the fact (appendix *vii, fn. 9) that any finite 0–1 sequence can be extended by some mathematical rule (p. 442).

**Second argument.**
- Some laws and theories are of a different character from *All swans are white*, even if worded similarly. Ancient atomic theory (*All material bodies are composed of corpuscles*) has the "all" form, but that form is comparatively unimportant here (p. 442).
- Showing that one piece of iron consists of atoms is at least as hard as showing that all swans are white (p. 443).
- Such *structural* theories transcend all possible experience. Their difficulty is not so much to get from instances to universality as to establish the law even for a single instance (p. 443).
- Many inductivists who saw this difficulty, Berkeley among them, tried to separate observational generalizations from "abstract" theories. As a rule they said abstract theories are only instruments for prediction ("instrumentalism"). Popper rejects this because it does not solve the problem. Structural properties occur constantly in ordinary speech, and almost every statement transcends experience. There is no sharp line between "empirical" and "theoretical" language (p. 443).

*Intuition note.* To call two events *the same kind of event* you must already care about some feature. So the feature, the interest, comes before the repetition. This means repetition cannot be the raw material from which expectations are built.

### (2) Singular statements transcend experience (pp. 443–444)
- In *This swan here is white*, the word "white" names an observable property. The statement transcends experience because of "swan", which attributes properties that go far beyond observation (p. 444).
- Singular statements are interpretations of "the facts" in the light of theories. Universals entail law-like behaviour (p. 444).
- Words like "glass" and "water" characterize law-like behaviour of things. They are *dispositional words* (terms for a tendency to behave in a certain way). Every law is unverifiable, so every predicate expressing law-like behaviour transcends experience. *This container contains water* is therefore testable but not verifiable (p. 444).
- Footnote 3: for singular statements the verifiability/falsifiability symmetry is less incorrect than for universal ones, since falsifying needs another unverifiable singular statement. Still an asymmetry remains: accepting a test statement can establish falsity of the tested statement but never its truth, because truth would entail infinitely many test statements (p. 444, fn. 3).
- Hence no true universal term can be "constituted" (Carnap's aim) from purely observational terms. All universals are dispositional, so they cannot be reduced to experience. They must be introduced as undefined terms, unless defined by other non-experiential universals, as "water" may be defined as a compound of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom (p. 444).

### (3) Degrees of dispositionality (p. 445)
- Universals are dispositional in varying degrees. "Soluble" and "breakable" are more dispositional than "dissolved" and "broken", but the latter are dispositional too (p. 445).
- A chemist would not say sugar has dissolved unless he expected to recover it by evaporation. If we doubt that a bone is broken, we test its behaviour for undue mobility. A surface is red if it has the disposition to reflect red light (p. 445).
- To see the dispositional character of a property, ask which tests we would run if we doubted that it is present (p. 445).
- So the attempt to split dispositional from non-dispositional predicates is mistaken, and so is the split between theoretical and observational terms. Popper tentatively suggests that people are inclined to regard what they learned before a certain age as factual or ordinary (p. 445).

*Intuition note.* Even "broken" amounts to a disposition to behave in certain regular ways when handled. So no universal word is a pure report of what is seen right now: such words carry expectations about future behaviour.

### (4) Transcendence, degrees, and why we use theories (pp. 445–446)
- Laws transcend experience in at least two ways: by universality and by containing dispositional terms. The more abstract the dispositional terms, the more they transcend. There are layers of universality, which Popper links to "depth" (Postscript *15) (pp. 445–446).
- Because of this transcendence, laws are non-verifiable. Testability or refutability is, in general, the only thing that distinguishes them from metaphysical theories (p. 446).
- Why use transcendent laws? (a) Because there is no "pure experience", only experience interpreted in the light of expectations or theories. (b) Because the theorist wants to explain, and explanatory hypotheses must go beyond what they explain to be independently testable (p. 446).
- (a) is pragmatic or instrumentalist. Popper believes it true but less important than (b). Even if explanatory theories could be eliminated for practical prediction, the theorist's aim would be unchanged (p. 446).
- Footnote 4: Carnap thinks one can do without theories; Popper sees no reason that his model-language analysis carries over to the language of science. On Craig's elimination of auxiliary ideas he adds three points. (i) It promotes infinitely many theorems to axioms. (ii) It is guided by knowledge of the theories being eliminated. (iii) The "purified" system is no longer explanatory or testable in the relevant sense, and, one might say, has zero depth (pp. 446–447, fn. 4).

### (5)–(7) Kneale's criticism and the moa example (pp. 447–449)
- **Kneale's point, as Popper reads it** (he says he finds Kneale hard to understand): laws entail universal statements but are logically stronger. They say something like *All planets move necessarily in ellipses*. Kneale calls these "principles of necessitation" (p. 447).
- Popper finds Kneale's explanation of necessity vs contingency unclear. It seems to him that Kneale's positive theory is utterly unacceptable, yet that the criticism is most valuable (pp. 447–448).
- **Moa example.** Use "moa" as a universal name for a biological structure. Assume no moas exist except the New Zealand ones. Assume moas could live sixty years or more under favourable conditions, but a virus meant none reached fifty (p. 448).
- Then *All moas die before reaching the age of fifty* is true and strictly universal. It is not a law, because a moa *could* live longer, and only accidental conditions prevent it (p. 448).
- So calling laws "strictly universal statements" is logically insufficient and intuitively inadequate (p. 448).
- (7) In this sense laws set limits to what is possible. A law limiting moa-like organisms to fifty years would make longer life impossible. This matches Popper's earlier talk of laws as prohibitions. He thinks it quite possible, and perhaps even useful, to speak of natural or physical necessity to describe this character of laws (pp. 448–449).

*Intuition note.* Two statements can both be true of everything that ever exists, yet differ in kind. One holds because a law would prevent it from being otherwise; the other holds only because circumstances happened to be so. The moa case is the second kind.

### (8)–(9) Natural vs logical necessity (pp. 449–451)
- Logically necessary means, roughly, what would hold in any conceivable world. Newton's inverse square law may be a law in some world, but a world where it fails is conceivable (p. 449).
- **Kneale's objection:** Goldbach's conjecture may be conceivably true or false even though it may well be demonstrable (or refutable), hence mathematically necessary (or impossible). So, Kneale argues, conceivability of the contradictory is no disproof of necessity in mathematics; why, he asks, should it be one in natural science? (p. 449).
- **Popper's reply:** the objection leans on "conceivable" and uses a different sense. After a proof, the contradictory is inconceivable in the sense that it leads to inconsistency (e.g. 0 = 1). Indirect proofs work by "conceiving" a premise and deriving absurdity (pp. 449–450).
- So in logic, "inconceivable" simply means leading to an obvious contradiction. Kneale uses the word in another, also good, sense (p. 450).
- (9) An assumption is logically possible if not self-contradictory, and physically possible if it does not contradict the laws of nature. The two senses should not be run together (p. 450).
- Laws are contingent compared with tautologies. Popper says Leibniz clearly recognized this, teaching that the world is the work of God somewhat as a sonnet or fugue is the work of an artist. Choosing a form imposes "principles of impossibility", but the choice of form was itself contingent (p. 450).
- So laws restrict the logically possible singular facts, which seem highly contingent next to the laws. But laws are contingent relative to tautologies, since structurally different worlds with different laws may exist (p. 450).
- Natural necessity is like musical necessity: a classical minuet cannot have a four-beat rhythm. No law forbids a minuet in G flat minor, yet it is quite possible that none has been or will be written. This is the distinction between laws and true universal statements about historical facts (p. 451).

### (10) The opposite view: laws not contingent at all (pp. 451–453)
- Popper takes Kneale (if he understands him) to hold that laws are necessary like tautologies. He calls this as mistaken as the view that laws are merely true universal statements (p. 451).
- Popper suggests Kneale's view might be put in religious terms: God chose whether to create a physical world, but then could choose only initial conditions (p. 451).
- Popper thinks Descartes held something similar. On Descartes's view, all laws follow with necessity from one analytic principle, that to be a body means the same as to be extended; Popper notes this resembles Kneale's example, that nothing red is also green. Physics since Newton went beyond such truisms (pp. 451–452).
- Popper says this doctrine is a particularly severe form of a view he calls **essentialism** and has criticized elsewhere. It entails the doctrine of *ultimate explanations*: explanatory theories that need no further explanation and allow none. He sees no reason to believe in them and many reasons to think them false. We can learn, through critical investigation, that the world is utterly different from what we ever imagined (p. 452).
- **Logical-probability argument.** If laws are not merely universal statements, they must be logically stronger, since universal statements must follow from them. Logical necessity is defined by (cf. appendix *v):
$$p(a) = p(a,\bar a) = 1.$$
For universal statements $a$ (cf. appendices *v, *vii, *viii):
$$p(a) = p(a,\bar a) = 0,$$
and the same must hold for any logically stronger statement (pp. 452–453).
- So a law, by its great content, is as far from a logical necessity as a consistent statement can be. It is nearer to an "accidentally" universal statement than to a logical truism (p. 453).

*Intuition note.* A statement with great content rules out many possibilities. Tautologies rule out nothing. By this measure a law is about as far from a logical truism as a consistent statement can be, and much nearer to a merely accidentally universal statement.

### (11) What changes and what does not (p. 453)
- Popper accepts Kneale's criticism insofar as there is a category of laws logically stronger than the corresponding universal statements. In his opinion this is incompatible with any theory of induction (p. 453).
- It makes little or no difference to his methodology. A principle declaring certain events impossible must be tested by trying to bring those events about (p. 453).
- The change is only ontological: to conjecture that $a$ is a law is to conjecture that $a$ expresses a *structural property* of our world, which prevents certain logically possible singular events (p. 453).

### (12) Defining natural necessity (pp. 453–454)
- Tarski explains logical necessity via universality: a statement is logically necessary iff it is deducible from a "universally valid" statement function, one satisfied by every model (true in all possible worlds) (p. 453).
- Popper defines natural necessity analogously:

**(N°)**, in paraphrase: a statement counts as naturally or physically necessary iff it can be deduced from a statement function that holds in every world differing from ours, if at all, only in initial conditions (pp. 453–454).

- We can never know that a supposed law is genuine rather than dependent on initial conditions in our region. A conjecture of natural necessity stays a conjecture forever, because we cannot search all worlds that differ in initial conditions (p. 454).
- There is no positive criterion. But we apply the definition negatively: finding initial conditions where the supposed law fails shows it was not necessary, hence not a law. This fits Popper's methodology (p. 454).
- (N°) makes all laws and all their logical consequences naturally necessary. Footnote 16 adds that logically necessary statements also become physically necessary, which does not matter (p. 454).
- It agrees with the moa case: we judged *moas die before fifty* accidental because moas would live longer under other conditions (p. 454).

### (13) The nomic conditional (pp. 454–457)
- $N$ is the class of naturally necessary statements: true whatever the initial conditions (p. 454).
- Definition, with $\to$ the ordinary conditional (p. 455):
$$a \xrightarrow[N]{} b \text{ is true iff } (a \to b) \in N. \tag{D}$$
- With $N$ read as "logically necessary", (D) would define strict implication (p. 455).
- Properties (here $a$, $b$ may be statements or statement functions) (p. 455):
  - (A) $a \xrightarrow[N]{} b$ is not always true if $a$ is false, unlike $a \to b$.
  - (B) It is not always true if $b$ is true, unlike $a \to b$.
  - (A′) It is always true if $a$ is impossible or necessarily false, i.e. if $\bar a$ is necessarily true (logically or physically).
  - (B′) It is always true if $b$ is necessarily true (logically or physically).
- Popper calls it a "necessary" or "nomic" conditional, and thinks it expresses what others call subjunctive or counterfactual conditionals. He objects to Kneale's usage, which implies that $a$ is in fact false (p. 455; in fn. 17 he wonders whether Kneale saw that the form attributed to him is only a complicated way of saying $\sim\phi(a)$).
- $N$ contains laws and everything deducible from them. This includes conditionals such as *if hydrogen and oxygen are mixed in this phial under such conditions … then …*. Either the antecedent holds and the consequent is true, or the antecedent is false and the conditional is vacuously true. So these conditionals keep their truth under all changes of initial conditions (p. 456).
- $N$ could simply be defined as laws plus their consequences. There is perhaps a slight advantage in defining it through initial conditions, since this avoids counterfactual wording (p. 456).
- **Circularity.** The phrase in (N°) about worlds differing only in initial conditions implicitly contains the idea of laws, i.e. worlds with the same structure or natural laws as ours. So (N°) is circular in that sense. Popper says all definitions are, just as all derivations (syllogisms) are, since the conclusion is contained in the premises. It is not circular in a technical sense, because it relies on a clear idea: varying initial conditions (e.g. planetary distances and masses). The resulting "models" of our world need not be faithful in initial conditions, and "necessary" means true in all of them (p. 457).

*Intuition note.* *If a then necessarily b* means that the plain conditional *if a then b* holds however the starting conditions are set. A conditional with an impossible antecedent therefore comes out true trivially. This property causes trouble in (17).

### (14) Relation to his 1949 paper (pp. 457–458)
- The present treatment is intuitively an improvement owed largely to Kneale; technically the changes are slight (p. 457).
- The 1949 paper used laws, conditionals following from laws, and subjunctive conditionals as those following from laws. It also suggested that we may have to suppose that all logically possible initial conditions are realized somewhere, at some time (p. 457).
- Popper now prefers to say that among the "models" (logical constructions, not real worlds) one exists in which moas live under ideal conditions. He holds that the older real-world assumption may possibly be true, but that we are much better off without it (p. 458, fn. 20).
- With that assumption the old and new views are equivalent as to the status of laws. The older view is, if anything, more metaphysical (p. 458).

### (15) Methodology and induction (pp. 458–459)
- For a falsificationist there is little difference between saying that laws are strictly universal statements and saying that they are necessary. Either way we test by attempted refutation (p. 458).
- The inductivist should reject necessary laws, since being logically stronger they are even less accessible to induction. Yet some inductivists seem to think that the necessity of laws could justify induction, perhaps on the lines of a principle of the uniformity of nature (pp. 458–459).
- Popper says no such principle could make inductive conclusions valid or even probable (p. 459).
- A statement that laws exist may justify our *search* for laws. This is a different "justify" from establishing induced generalizations. The search is justified anyway by curiosity and hope, as hope of food justifies looking for it (p. 459).
- The necessary/universal distinction does not seem to be relevant here (p. 459).

### (16) The metaphysical significance of necessity (pp. 459–460)
- Popper believes necessary laws, in the sense of (12), are ontologically important and true. He admits this cannot be shown empirically (it is not falsifiable) or otherwise. Its metaphysical character does not prevent rational, critical discussion (pp. 459–460).
- Unlike Kneale, he treats "necessary" as a mere label to distinguish the universality of laws from accidental universality. He largely agrees with the spirit of Wittgenstein's paraphrase of Hume: there is only logical necessity (p. 460).
- The only link to logical necessity is this: the ordinary conditional $a \to b$ follows with logical necessity from a law, so it is necessary *relative to a law of nature*. It may be said that a law is in turn necessary because it is logically derivable from a law of higher universality or greater depth. Perhaps this suggested the idea of necessary connection between cause and effect (p. 460).
- Footnote 21: Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* 6.36 holds that the statement that there are natural laws clearly cannot be said. Popper replies that it can be said, though it cannot be verified or falsified. Non-verifiability does not make it meaningless (p. 459, fn. 21).

### (17) Subjunctive conditionals, phenomenalism, operationalism (pp. 460–463)
- Popper reads the modern discussion of subjunctive or counterfactual conditionals as arising mainly from difficulties of inductivism, positivism, operationalism and phenomenalism (pp. 460–461).
- **Phenomenalism.**
  - It would translate *There is a flower-pot on the window sill* as *If anybody looks appropriately, he will see a flower-pot*. The second statement is vacuously true when nobody looks, but a flower-pot need not be there (p. 461).
  - The reply (Braithwaite, fn. 25) is a modal conditional: *if anybody were looking, he would see …* (p. 461).
  - The nomic conditional supplies this, but the objection survives. If $\bar a \in N$, then $a \xrightarrow[N]{} b$ holds for every $b$. So for a place nobody can physically look at, the translation is true merely because nobody can look. By this test, there is a flower-pot at the centre of the sun, which is absurd. Popper sees no chance of rescuing phenomenalism this way (pp. 461–462).
- **Operationalism.**
  - It demands that terms like "soluble" be defined by experimental procedure. Popper says such definitions are circular (p. 462).
  - Test for "soluble in water" involves recovering the substance and identifying it, and one property to check is solubility in water. So the definition would at least have to say something like this: $x$ is soluble iff (a) it necessarily disappears in water and (b) after evaporation a substance is necessarily recovered which is again soluble in water (p. 462).
  - The reason is that experiments are never conclusive and must themselves be testable by further experiments (p. 462).
  - Fn. 27: the operational definition of length involves temperature corrections, while the usual operational definition of temperature involves measuring length (p. 462).
- Operationalists seem to have thought that solving the subjunctive-conditional problem would clear the way for operational definitions of dispositional terms. Popper says that even if the nomic conditionals are analysed, dispositional and universal terms cannot be operationally defined, because they transcend experience (pp. 462–463).

### Addendum, 1968 (p. 463)
- Popper mentions a reply by Kneale (1961) and a criticism by Nerlich and Suchting (1967), to which he replied. He no longer thinks his reply very good (p. 463).
- The root of the disagreement, he now thinks, is that most philosophers regard definitions as important, while he does not. Definitions cannot make meanings definite, and we need undefined primitive terms anyway (p. 463).
- Summary: theories and their truth are all-important; words and their meaning are unimportant (p. 463).
- So he is not very interested in defining or defining-ability of "natural necessity". He is interested in the claim that the idea is not meaningless. He is least interested in showing that a modal term can be defined by non-modal terms (a claim he regards as doubtful), and says any impression that he wanted this is wrong (p. 463).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take two possible worlds with the same law and different initial conditions.
- Law (shared structure): a gadget lives 60 years if no virus is present, and 45 years if a virus is present.
- Initial condition: $W_V$ (our toy "actual" world): virus present. $W_{NV}$: virus absent.
- $N$ = statements true in both worlds.

Check three statements.
1. $S_1$: *All gadgets die before 50.*
   - In $W_V$ the lifetime is 45 < 50, so $S_1$ is true.
   - In $W_{NV}$ the lifetime is 60, so $S_1$ is false.
   - So $S_1 \notin N$. It is true and universal but accidental, like the moa statement.
2. $S_2$: *If a virus is present, a gadget dies before 50*, i.e. $a \to b$ with $a$ = virus present, $b$ = dies before 50.
   - In $W_V$: $a$ true, $b$ true (45 < 50), so $S_2$ is true.
   - In $W_{NV}$: $a$ false, so $S_2$ is vacuously true.
   - So $S_2 \in N$ and $a \xrightarrow[N]{} b$ holds.
3. Property (A): let $a'$ = "no virus", $b$ = "dies before 50". In $W_V$, $a'$ is false, so $a' \to b$ is true. In $W_{NV}$, $a'$ is true and $b$ is false, so $a' \to b$ is false. So $a' \to b \notin N$ and $a' \xrightarrow[N]{} b$ is false although $a'$ is false in $W_V$.

Property (B) follows from $S_1$. $b$ is true in $W_V$, yet *if gadget, then dies before 50* fails in $W_{NV}$, so the nomic conditional is false.

## Concepts
- [[primacy-of-repetitions]] (new) — the doctrine, in a logical and a temporal variant, that repetitions justify or generate universal beliefs; Popper rejects both (p. 440)
- [[similarity-is-relative]] (new) — similarity, hence repetition, always holds in some respect and presupposes a point of view or interest (p. 441)
- [[instrumentalism]] (new) — Berkeley-style view that abstract theories are only prediction tools; Popper rejects it because it does not solve the problem of structural properties, which pervade ordinary speech (p. 443)
- [[dispositional-universals]] (new) — all universal terms express law-like behaviour, in varying degrees, so none reduces to experience (pp. 444–445)
- [[falsifiability]] — Popper's testability criterion; here the only general mark separating laws from metaphysics (p. 446)
- [[accidental-universal-statement]] (new) — a true strictly universal statement that holds only through contingent conditions (moa example) (p. 448)
- [[natural-necessity]] (new) — deducibility from a statement function satisfied in all worlds differing from ours, if at all, only in initial conditions; definition (N°) (pp. 453–454)
- [[nomic-conditional]] (new) — $a \xrightarrow[N]{} b$, true iff $(a\to b)\in N$; Popper's version of subjunctive conditionals (p. 455)
- [[essentialism]] (new) — a view Popper criticizes elsewhere; he calls the doctrine that laws are in no sense contingent a particularly severe form of it, entailing the existence of ultimate explanations (p. 452)
- [[operationalism-circularity]] (new) — Popper holds that operational definitions are circular; for "soluble", identifying the recovered substance already requires solubility, and in general experiments are never conclusive (p. 462)

## Canonical instances
- [[moa-example]] — a true universal statement (*all moas die before fifty*) that is not a law (p. 448)
- [[swan-transcends-experience]] — a statement like *this swan here is white* already goes beyond observation, because of the word swan (p. 444)
- [[soluble-and-dissolved]] — dispositional in different degrees; the sugar and bone-test examples (p. 445)
- [[flower-pot-phenomenalism]] — a flower-pot at the centre of the sun shows the modal translation to be absurd (p. 462)
- [[goldbach-conceivability]] — Kneale's objection and Popper's reply on "conceivable" (pp. 449–450)
- [[minuet-analogy]] — natural necessity like musical necessity (p. 451)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] No sharp line between empirical and theoretical language (p. 443) ↔ Quine's web of belief: both deny a privileged observational core.
- [structural-analogy] Natural necessity as truth across worlds that vary only initial conditions ↔ possible-worlds semantics for necessity: same quantifier-over-worlds shape, with an accessibility restriction.
- [metaphor] Laws as prohibitions on events ↔ Kuhn-style rules of a paradigm: both constrain what may happen or count.
- [structural-analogy] Rejection of operational definitions of dispositions ↔ underdetermination: tests never conclude and need further tests.

## Questions this raises
- If natural necessity can never be established positively, what does it add beyond being a universal statement that has survived severe tests?
- Is (N°) really non-circular when variation of initial conditions alone already assumes a distinction between laws and conditions?
- How can a statement ("laws exist") be metaphysical, unfalsifiable, and still be critically discussed?
- Does the "everything is dispositional" argument threaten the idea of an observational basis that Popper keeps for testing?
