# On the Use and Misuse of Imaginary Experiments, Especially in Quantum Theory

## L0 — One line
Imaginary experiments are legitimate as critical or heuristic tools, but in argument the defender may make only idealizations that favour or are acceptable to the opponent; Popper argues that several replies in the quantum-theory debates broke this rule.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Popper asks when an imaginary (thought) experiment may be used in argument. Galileo's refutation of Aristotle is his model of the critical use. Heuristic (idea-suggesting) uses are also valuable. The dangerous use is the apologetic one: defending a theory with idealizations or special assumptions that suit the defender. Popper's rule is that idealizations (simplifying assumptions) must be concessions to the opponent, or acceptable to him. He argues that replies by Bohr and Bohm, Heisenberg's microscope argument and Jordan's account of complementarity are inadmissible. He says he is attacking methods of argument, not quantum theory. This matters because these methods went unchallenged for years and, he thinks, one of them spread a metaphysical picture almost unnoticed.

**Prerequisites:** reductio ad absurdum; high-school notions of momentum, position, photons, waves and particles; the idea of an ad hoc assumption.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Three uses of imaginary experiments: critical (Galileo's stones), heuristic (idea-suggesting: atomism, Carnot, Einstein's lift) and apologetic (defensive), which Popper warns against (pp. 465–466).
- Rule for argumentative use: the opponent's view is stated clearly, and every idealization is a concession to him or acceptable to him. Carnot's cycle satisfies it (pp. 466–467).
- Popper argues that Bohr's reply to Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen (EPR) changes the explanation of "smearing" without saying so, seems ad hoc, and uses a method that Popper finds impermissible. Popper says Bohr's E = mc² reply (point 6) makes extravagant assumptions (pp. 468–470).
- Popper finds Bohm's reply to EPR unsatisfactory and says his reply to Einstein's billiard-ball case rests on assumptions that are ad hoc and paralysing (pp. 470–473).
- Popper argues that Heisenberg's microscope does not show that position and momentum measurements disturb the electron symmetrically. Popper's own section-77 experiment was largely based on that asymmetry and, he says, is invalid for that reason (pp. 473–475).
- Popper is convinced the microscope's influence came from a metaphysical picture (unknowable thing in itself, particle and wave "traps") spread under anti-metaphysical disclaimers (pp. 475–477).
- Popper argues that Jordan's formulation of complementarity is refuted by the very two-slit experiment used to illustrate it. Popper feels Bohr owes a better explanation (pp. 477–480).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Aim and scope (p. 464).** The criticisms are logical. Popper does not refute particular arguments, some of which may have been discarded by their authors. He says certain methods of argument are inadmissible, mainly the apologetic use of imaginary experiments in the discussion of quantum theory. He does not wish to criticize quantum theory or any particular interpretation of it (fn. 1), nor doubt that imaginary experiments are fruitful.

**(1) Three uses (pp. 464–466).**
- *Critical use: Galileo against Aristotle.* The target is the Aristotelian supposition that a heavier body has a greater natural velocity. Join a faster and a slower body: the faster is partly retarded by the slower, and the slower partly sped up by the faster, so the composite moves slower than the faster one alone. Galileo's example: stones with speeds 8 and 4. Yet the composite is bigger than the first stone, so by the supposition it should move faster than 8 (added step). This is a contradiction, so the supposition is refuted (p. 465). Popper calls this a "perfect model" of the best use, the critical one (p. 465).
- *Heuristic use.* Examples: cutting gold into ever smaller parts as the basis of atomism; Carnot's cycle; Einstein's accelerated lift, which illustrates the local equivalence of acceleration and gravity and suggests that light rays in a gravitational field may curve. Popper calls this use "important and legitimate" (pp. 465–466).
- *Apologetic use.* Popper thinks it goes back to discussions of measuring rods and clocks in special relativity. At first those experiments were illustrative, which is legitimate. Later, in quantum theory, they were also used, at times, as arguments, critical or defensive. Heisenberg's imaginary microscope played an important part (p. 466).
- *Critical experiments and replies.* A critical imaginary experiment tries to show that a theory's author overlooked possibilities. This is legitimate. It must also be legitimate to reply, for example by showing that the experiment is impossible in principle. In the reconstruction made in defence, no idealization or special assumption may be introduced unless it favours the opponent or the opponent would have to accept it (p. 466). Fn. 4: Einstein showed Popper's own section-77 experiment to be impossible in principle, from the quantum-theoretical point of view.

**(2) The rule (pp. 466–467).** Argumentative use is legitimate only if the opponent's views are stated clearly and the idealizations are concessions to him, or at least acceptable to him. Example: in Carnot's cycle, every idealization increases the machine's efficiency. So an opponent who says a heat machine can do mechanical work without passing heat from a hotter to a colder place must agree these are concessions (pp. 466–467). Idealizations that break the rule are impermissible in critical argument (p. 467).

*Intuition note.* A fair argument makes only concessions that the opponent must accept. If the defender adds convenient assumptions of his own, the argument breaks the rule and, on Popper's view, is not admissible.

**(3) EPR and Bohr's reply (p. 467).** EPR attempt to use idealizations acceptable to Bohr, and Bohr does not challenge their legitimacy. Two particles A and B interact. Measuring the position (or momentum) of B lets the theory calculate the position (or momentum) of A, which has moved far away and can no longer be disturbed by the measurement. So A's momentum (or position) cannot be "smeared", as Heisenberg would have it (p. 467). Fn. 5: Heisenberg thought only of smearing the measured particle. Popper asks how, if the smearing extends to partners from earlier interactions, the whole world is to avoid being smeared by one observation. The answer, presumably, is that owing to the reduction of the wave packet the observation destroys the old picture and creates a new one. The interference is then with our way of representing the system, not with the world.
**Bohr's reply (as Popper reports it):** position is measured by an instrument rigidly fixed to the frame of reference, momentum by a movable diaphragm, and choosing one system of reference cuts us off from the other. Popper's reading, "if I understand him properly", is that A is not interfered with, but its coordinates may be smeared through smearing of the frame of reference (pp. 467–468).

**(4) Three objections to Bohr (pp. 468–469).** Popper says Bohr's reply seems to him unacceptable for at least three reasons.
1. *Silent change of argument.* Before EPR, smearing was explained by interference with the system measured. It seems to Popper that Bohr, surreptitiously, dropped this and replaced it, more or less clearly, by interference with the frame of reference. Popper thinks the change is too big to pass unnoticed: it would have to be explicitly acknowledged that the older position was refuted, and shown why this does not destroy the principle on which it was built (p. 468). EPR meant only to refute certain interpretations of the indeterminacy formulae, not the formulae. In a sense, though not explicitly, Bohr's reply acknowledged that EPR succeeded in their purpose, since he gave up the view that measurement interferes with A. Extension: measure A's position at the same instant as B's momentum. Then, for that instant, both positions and momenta of A and B are obtained (A's momentum and B's position being destroyed or smeared). That is enough to show it is incorrect to read the formulae as saying a system cannot have both a sharp position and a sharp momentum at once, although both cannot be predicted at once (p. 468).
2. *Seems ad hoc.* Momentum can be measured spectroscopically, directly or by the Doppler effect, with a spectroscope rigidly fixed to the same frame. That the spectroscope absorbs B is irrelevant to A. So a movable frame is not essential to the experiment (p. 469).
3. *Method seems impermissible.* Bohr does not say how the diaphragm measures B's momentum. A later paper describes measuring twice the position of a slit diaphragm suspended by weak springs. The momentum then depends on position measurements. The first momentum measurement interferes with the diaphragm's momentum, so it is only retrospective. It cannot give the momentum "before as well as after the passing" of B (p. 469).
Conclusion: it does not seem to Popper that Bohr kept to the rule of making only idealizations or assumptions that favour his opponents, quite apart from its being far from clear what he wanted to contest (p. 469).

*Intuition note.* EPR attacked an explanation (smearing by disturbing the system), not the formulae. Popper says Bohr's reply quietly changed the explanation and used assumptions that seem ad hoc to him; in a sense, though, it conceded EPR's purpose.

**(5) Warning (p. 469).** With such experiments there is a grave danger of carrying the analysis only as far as it serves one's purpose. It can be avoided only if the principles above are strictly adhered to.

**(6) Bohr and E = mc² (p. 470).** Against a critical experiment of Einstein's based on E = mc², Bohr used arguments from Einstein's gravitational theory (general relativity). But E = mc² can be derived from special relativity, even non-relativistically, so assuming it does not assume Einstein's gravitation theory. If, as Bohr suggests, characteristic formulae of that theory are needed to keep quantum theory consistent with E = mc², Popper says this amounts to two strange assertions. The first is that quantum theory contradicts Newton's gravitation theory. The second is that Einstein's formulae follow from quantum theory. Fn. 10 adds that m_i = m_g is part of Newton's theory. Popper's verdict: extravagant assumptions with an apologetic purpose (p. 470).

**(7) Bohm and EPR (pp. 470–471).** Bohm tries to show that A, though far away, is still interfered with unpredictably, so that his theory agrees with Heisenberg's reading of the indeterminacy relations. Popper says this fails. By the extension in (4), B's position can be measured while someone far away measures A's momentum at the same instant, or before any smearing effect could reach A (pp. 470–471). Popper reads Bohm's reply as implicit in his assertion that the smearing effect travels faster than light, or perhaps even instantaneously, supported by the further assumption that it cannot be used to transmit signals. **Popper asks:** if both measurements are simultaneous, does the observed particle "begin to dance" in the microscope? If it does, is that not a signal? This smearing is part of the interpretation, not the formalism (p. 471). Fn. 11: Popper understands that Bohm no longer holds some of these views.

**(8) Einstein's billiard ball and Bohm (pp. 471–473).**
- *Setup.* A macroscopic "particle" moves at constant velocity between two parallel walls with elastic reflection. In Schrödinger's theory this is a standing wave. In de Broglie's pilot-wave theory, or Bohm's causal interpretation, the velocity comes out zero, whatever velocity was assumed (Pauli's criticism, revived by Einstein) (p. 471).
- *Bohm's reply.* **Bohm:** the particle is at rest, and observation "triggers" motion (p. 472). **Popper:** the interpretation paralyses the free particle. Bohm notes this but does not discuss it. He says observation would show it moving, but that was not the point, and he builds a new imaginary experiment with a triggering photon. He does not explain how the photon reveals the full speed rather than an acceleration towards it. Popper calls these ad hoc assumptions (p. 472).
- *Two-ball version.* Two balls, one between the left wall and the centre, one between the right wall and the centre, collide elastically at the centre. The result is again standing waves and zero velocity. Triggering the left ball would (according to Bohm) overthrow the balance of forces, so it starts moving, presumably left to right. Then, on this account, the right ball would have to start at once in the opposite direction (pp. 472–473).
- *Imagined answer.* **Popper's Einstein (Popper writes that Einstein might have answered as follows, "I think"):** for a big ball no reason is given why classical measurement should fail. Is it seriously asserted that an oscillating ball cannot exist unobserved, or that assuming it moves leads to the conclusion that it does not? If the interference stops, does it halt as suddenly as it started? Does its energy become field energy? Is the process irreversible? (p. 473). Even assuming these questions can be answered somehow, Popper thinks they illustrate the significance of Pauli's and Einstein's criticism and of the critical use of imaginary experiments, and also the danger of the apologetic use (p. 473).

**(9) Heisenberg's microscope (pp. 473–475).**
- Heisenberg tried to establish various points, of which Popper mentions three: (a) the indeterminacy formulae state insuperable barriers to measurement precision; (b) measurement disturbs the object, whether position or momentum is measured; (c) the spatio-temporal path cannot be tested. Popper holds the arguments "clearly invalid", whatever the merit of the points, because they fail to show that position and momentum measurements are symmetrical with respect to disturbance (p. 474).
- *Position.* High-frequency, high-energy photons give the electron an unknown momentum, a "severe knock".
- *Momentum.* Low-frequency light is used, so low that the electron's momentum is assumed undisturbed. The observation reveals momentum but fails to reveal position, which stays indeterminate. This asserts only failure to disclose, not that position was disturbed. So the cases are far from symmetrical (p. 474).
- *Why the asymmetry was missed.* It is veiled by talk of "results of measurement", whose uncertainty is symmetric. The formalism is symmetric, but the experiment does not account for that. It is wrongly assumed that measuring momentum smears position (pp. 474–475).
- *Consequence for Popper's section-77 experiment.* It rested largely on the asymmetry, yet it is invalid, because the asymmetry invalidates Heisenberg's whole discussion of measurement. Only measurements by physical selection can illustrate the formulae, and physical selection must satisfy the scatter relations (it disturbs the system). Were Heisenberg's measurements possible, momentum could be checked between two position measurements without disturbance, and part of the path could be tested, contrary to (c) (p. 475).
- *Why unnoticed.* The formulae follow from the formalism, so physicists, Popper supposes, treated the experiment as an illustration of a derivable formula. It is a bad illustration, and inadequate as a basis for interpretation (p. 475).

*Intuition note.* In Heisenberg's own story, measuring position knocks the electron, but measuring momentum with gentle light does not. So the story cannot explain why the mathematics treats position and momentum alike.

**(10) The metaphysical picture (pp. 475–477).** Popper is convinced the microscope was influential because it conveyed a metaphysical picture while disclaiming metaphysics. He adds a remark about our "post-rationalist age", which is preoccupied with killing the Father (that is, Metaphysics) while keeping Him inviolate in some other form (pp. 475–476). The picture, which Popper says is somehow conveyed through Heisenberg's discussion although never really implied in it: the thing in itself is unknowable. We know only appearances, which result from the thing and our perceiving apparatus (as Kant pointed out). We set a classical "particle trap" or "wave trap", and the thing, triggered, assumes one appearance. There is a symmetry between the two. We also "bait" the trap with energy, which preserves the conservation laws (p. 476). This is the picture conveyed by Heisenberg, "perhaps also" by Bohr (p. 476).
Popper does not object to metaphysics of this kind, though he is "not much attracted" by this blend of positivism and transcendentalism. He does not object to metaphors either. He objects to its almost unconscious spread, with antimetaphysical disclaimers; it should be criticized (p. 476). Much of Bohm's work seems inspired by the same metaphysics. Popper calls it an admirable attempt to make it explicit, but wonders whether it is worth the trouble, since Heisenberg's experiment cannot support it (pp. 476–477). He sees a fairly obvious link between Bohr's complementarity and this view of an unknowable reality, but does not discuss it (p. 477).

**(11) Jordan and complementarity (pp. 477–479).** Popper says Bohr has analysed many subtle imaginary experiments in a similarly apologetic vein. Because Bohr's formulations are vague and difficult to discuss, Popper uses Jordan's.
- **Jordan's principle:** any single experiment bringing forth at the same time both wave and particle properties of light would be logically and mathematically absurd (p. 477).
- **Jordan's illustration:** the two-slit experiment. Monochromatic light falls on a screen with two close slits. Assume (i) the slits are small enough to give interference fringes on a plate; and (ii) an arrangement can tell which slit a single photon passed. Jordan says the two assumptions contradict each other (pp. 477–478).
- **Popper's reply:** he does not contest the contradiction. It would not be a logical or mathematical absurdity, only a clash with the quantum formalism (p. 478). He contests a different point: the very experiment Jordan uses to illustrate the principle can be shown to refute it. Keep only (i): fringes appear, which brings forth wave properties. At low intensity the fringes can be analysed as the density of single-photon hits, which shows particle properties. So one experiment brings forth at least some properties of both kinds. Being able to find the slit as well would show particle properties more strongly, but that is irrelevant. Jordan's principle claimed that no experiment whatever combines the two, and that claim is "simply false", refuted by almost all typical quantum experiments (pp. 478–479).
- *Other readings.* Popper asks whether Jordan meant that no experiment brings out all wave and all particle properties; clearly this cannot have been his intention, since even all wave properties alone cannot be combined in one experiment (p. 479).
- **Arbitrariness:** some wave properties and some particle properties no experiment can combine. Jordan generalized this into a principle, illustrated it with an impossible experiment, and the half that everybody admits to be possible refutes it, at least in Jordan's formulation (p. 479).
- *The other half.* Close one slit: many signs of wave character remain, such as a wave-like density distribution. Opponents admit the particle properties then show very fully, since the path can be traced (p. 479).

*Intuition note.* The principle says "never both". But the ordinary low-intensity two-slit experiment already shows fringes (wave) built from separate hits (particle). Popper says the principle, in Jordan's formulation, is therefore refuted.

**(12) Verdict (pp. 479–480).** From a rational point of view all these arguments are inadmissible. Popper does not doubt an interesting intuitive idea lies behind complementarity, but neither Bohr nor his school has explained it, even to critics like Einstein who tried hard for years to understand it. His impression, with an admitted chance of being wrong, is that it may be the metaphysical idea of (10). Bohr "owes us a better explanation" (p. 480). Fn. 20 (added 1967) points to a later paper by Popper.

## Toy example
*Ours, not from the text.* A rival theory T says fall speed = 2 × mass (kg → m/s). Stones of 4 kg and 2 kg should fall at 8 and 4 m/s. Tie them together. Assume, as in Galileo's argument, that the slower stone retards the faster one, so the pair falls at less than 8 m/s, say 6 m/s. Yet the pair weighs 6 kg, so T predicts 2 × 6 = 12 m/s. Since the pair should fall at less than 8 m/s (here 6) but T says 12, the two claims clash, so T is refuted (Galileo-style).
Now a defender says the string weighs 1 kg. The pair is then 7 kg and T predicts 14 m/s, still above 8. Because the extra assumption does not help T, the critic can accept it. But a defender who said "assume T applies only to untied stones" would be adding a special assumption the critic need not grant, which is Popper's test.

## Concepts
- [[imaginary-experiment]] (new) — a thought experiment used to illustrate, to argue, or to defend a theory (pp. 464–466).
- [[critical-use]] (new) — using an imaginary experiment to show a theory overlooked possibilities; Galileo's stones is the model (pp. 465–466).
- [[heuristic-use]] (new) — using one to suggest or explain ideas (atomism, Einstein's lift) (p. 465).
- [[apologetic-use]] (new) — using one in a defensive mood to protect a theory; Popper's worry is idealizations or special assumptions that favour the defender (pp. 466, 469).
- [[concession-rule]] (new) — idealizations in argument must favour, or be acceptable to, the opponent (p. 466).
- [[ad-hoc-assumption]] (new) — an extra assumption introduced only to avoid a criticism, as Popper charges against Bohr and Bohm (pp. 469, 472–473).
- [[indeterminacy-formulae]] (new) — Heisenberg's formulae. Popper separates the formulae from certain interpretations of them, which EPR's experiment was meant to refute (p. 468).
- [[physical-selection]] (new) — Popper's term; only measurements of this kind illustrate the formulae, and they disturb the system (p. 475).
- [[complementarity-principle]] (new) — Bohr's principle, here in Jordan's formulation: no single experiment brings forth both wave and particle properties of light (p. 477).
- [[validity-soundness]] — Popper calls Heisenberg's arguments "invalid"; he means they do not establish the claimed symmetry (p. 474).

## Canonical instances
- [[galileo-falling-stones]] — critical reductio of Aristotle's claim about natural velocities (p. 465).
- [[carnot-cycle]] — idealizations that favour the opponent's side, so the argument is fair (pp. 466–467).
- [[epr-experiment]] — two particles A and B; meant to refute certain interpretations of the indeterminacy formulae, such as smearing of A (pp. 467–468).
- [[heisenberg-microscope]] — Popper argues position and momentum measurements are not symmetric in disturbance (p. 474).
- [[einstein-billiard-ball]] — standing wave gives zero velocity in the pilot-wave theory; Bohm's reply is ad hoc (pp. 471–473).
- [[two-slit-experiment]] — Popper argues it refutes Jordan's formulation of complementarity (pp. 477–479).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Concession rule ↔ computational social science: stress-testing a model under assumptions that favour the rival, so that a surviving result is convincing.
- [structural-analogy] Ad hoc rescue assumptions ↔ research programme (canon): adjustments that only protect a theory after criticism look like a degenerating programme.
- [metaphor] Popper's "trap" picture of appearances ↔ measurement effects in social science: the way you measure a system may shape what you see.

## Questions this raises
- How can one decide whether an idealization is acceptable to an opponent when the opponent has not stated his view clearly?
- Does Popper's symmetry objection to Heisenberg undermine the indeterminacy formulae themselves, or only one way of motivating them?
- If metaphysical pictures spread unconsciously through illustrations, how can one detect and criticize them in other fields?
- Is Popper's reading of Bohr's complementarity (the metaphysics of point 10) fair, given that he admits "I may be wrong"?
