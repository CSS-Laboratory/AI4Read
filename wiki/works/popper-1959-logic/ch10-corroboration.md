# Corroboration, or how a theory stands up to tests

## L0 — One line
Theories cannot be made "probable" by evidence; they can only be corroborated, meaning they have survived severe tests, and the more testable (logically improbable) a theory is, the more corroborable it is.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can we say a scientific theory is "probable", or only that it has stood up to tests? Popper argues that the probability of hypotheses cannot be reduced to the probability of events. He also argues that an appraisal of a theory as "probable" faces infinite regress (or apriorism). He proposes "corroboration" instead: a timeless logical report on how severe the tests were that a theory survived, relative to the basic statements accepted so far. The more testable a theory is, the more corroborable it is. Probability logic implies the opposite. The unit ends with a picture of science as bold conjecture under rigorous test, never certain, and a 1972 addendum on induction.

**Prerequisites:** relative frequency and conditional probability; universal vs singular statements; modus tollens; Popper's earlier ideas of falsifiability, basic statements and degree of testability (as used here).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Theories are not verifiable. The "uniformity of nature" is better read as a methodological rule: new theories must explain the old corroborated regularities. A "principle of induction" would be metaphysical, so it leads to infinite regress or apriorism (pp. 249–252).
- The probability of a hypothesis cannot be defined as a truth-frequency of statements. Each attempt Popper examines gives unacceptable results: probabilities such as 1/2, 0 or 1, or subjective values (pp. 253–258).
- Appraising a theory as "probable" is itself a non-verifiable synthetic claim and needs its own appraisal. Replacing "true" with "probable" gains nothing (pp. 261–263).
- Corroboration is a logical relation between a theory and the accepted basic statements. It depends more on the severity of tests than on the number of instances (pp. 264–267).
- Corroborability stands, in a rough sense (Popper's hedge), in inverse ratio to logical probability. Probability logic, e.g. Keynes's, implies the opposite, so corroboration cannot be probability (pp. 268–271, notes *2–*3).
- Corroboration is timeless but carries a time index, so it is not a truth value. Truth and falsity remain timeless (pp. 273–276).
- Science advances "quasi-inductively" towards more universal theories. Metaphysical ideas become scientific only when falsifiable (pp. 276–278).
- Science is bold conjecture under severe tests, never certainty. The 1972 addendum gives a negative solution plus a positive rule of preference for better-corroborated theories (pp. 278–282).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 248, with note *1, pp. 248–249)
- Probability logic is a logic that ascribes degrees of probability, not only "true" or "false", to statements. Inductivists want induction to fix a statement's probability. The principle of induction itself might then be only "probably valid" (p. 248).
- **Popper's view:** the whole problem of the probability of hypotheses is misconceived. We should assess what tests a hypothesis has withstood, i.e. how far it is "corroborated" (p. 248).
- **Note *1:** "corroboration" (*Bewährung*) was meant as a neutral term. It does not prejudge whether standing up to tests makes a hypothesis "more probable" in the sense of the probability calculus. Carnap translated it as "degree of confirmation". Popper disliked the associations ("make firm", "prove") but adopted the usage for a time. Carnap then used "degree of confirmation" as a synonym of probability, so Popper went back to "corroboration" (p. 249).

### §79 Concerning the so-called verification of hypotheses (pp. 249–252)
- **Objection (defenders of "verification"):** people say a theory is verified when some predictions are. They add that the logical uncertainty may be neglected. They say no theory has yet had to be regarded as falsified because a well-confirmed law suddenly broke down. What happens is only that new experiments decide against an old theory, which often survives as a limiting case. Regularities directly testable by experiment do not change, and scientific method presupposes the "uniformity of nature" (pp. 249–250).
- **Popper's reply:** the argument expresses a metaphysical faith in regularities. He shares it, but abstains from arguing for or against it. His point is that non-verifiability is methodologically important (p. 250).
- He takes up only the uniformity principle. It expresses superficially an important methodological rule: any new system of hypotheses should yield the old, corroborated regularities (p. 250, note *2).
- Example: suppose the sun does not rise tomorrow. Science would have to explain this by deriving it from laws. The revised theories would also have to derive our older experiences (p. 250).
- Hence uniformity is replaced by a postulate of invariance of natural laws in space and time. It is wrong to assert that regularities do not change, because that can be neither argued for nor against. Instead, it is part of our definition of natural laws that they are invariant and have no exceptions. The uniformity principle, like the "law of causality", is a metaphysical interpretation of a methodological rule (p. 251).
- **Why a "principle of induction" fails:**
  - It is itself metaphysical. If it were empirical, there would be an infinite regress (section 1) (p. 251).
  - If it were falsifiable, then any falsified theory derived with its help would falsify it by modus tollens. (Modus tollens: if premises imply a false conclusion, a premise is false.) Observation statements are assumed unshaken, so they cannot take the blame (p. 251, note *3).
  - It would therefore have to be non-falsifiable. That makes it a synthetic statement valid a priori, i.e. an irrefutable statement about reality (pp. 251–252).
- **Result:** turning the metaphysical faith into an inductive theory of knowledge leaves only a choice between infinite regress and apriorism (p. 252).

*Intuition note.* "Nature is uniform" cannot be proved or disproved, so Popper treats it as a rule we impose: laws must hold everywhere and always, and new theories must keep what old ones got right. An induction principle strong enough to justify theories would have to be immune to refutation, and that is a claim about the world we could never test.

### §80 The probability of a hypothesis and the probability of events: criticism of probability logic (pp. 252–261)
- **Question:** might the probability of a hypothesis be reduced to the probability of events, so it can be handled mathematically (p. 252)? Popper says the theory of the probability of hypotheses seems to have arisen from confusing psychological with logical questions. Our confidence may depend on past corroboration, but that is psychology, not methodology (p. 252).
- **Reichenbach's view:** ascribing probability to statements or to events is a matter of terminology. A die's face has probability 1/6, or equally the statement that this face will turn up has probability 1/6 (p. 253).
- Section 23 defined an *event* as a class of singular statements. A sequence of statements $p_j, p_k, \bar p_l, p_m, \bar p_n,\dots$ (a bar marks "false") gives probability as relative **truth-frequency** (the share of true statements in a sequence) (p. 253).
- With one statement, the truth-frequency is 1 or 0. So truth and falsity are limiting cases, and classical logic's operations are limiting cases of operations on truth-frequencies. That calculus is "probability logic" (Reichenbach, following Post and von Mises) (pp. 253–254).
- **Popper's claim:** identifying the probability of hypotheses with this probability of statements is a confusion. The issues are "not even touched" by probability logic. Saying a hypothesis is "probable" cannot be translated into a statement about the probability of events (p. 254, with note *2).
- **Which reference sequence?** Reichenbach identifies a scientific hypothesis with a sequence of statements. Popper tries several readings (pp. 254–257):
  1. *Elements = singular statements that agree or disagree with the hypothesis.* A hypothesis refuted on average by every second singular statement would get probability 1/2. Note *3 says clear-cut falsification is assumed to give probability zero, so only unfalsified cases are discussed (p. 255).
  2. *Ratio of tests passed to tests not yet attempted.* Popper says this can be computed precisely and the result is always zero (p. 255).
  3. *Ratio of favourable to indifferent results.* This is not a truth-frequency, since an indifferent statement is not objectively false. It would make probability depend on the experimenter's training and skill, so it is hopelessly subjective (pp. 255–256).
  4. *Hypothesis as a sequence of basic statements.* This would be possible if universal statements had the form of a claim that something occurs at every place $k$, but they do not (sections 15, 28). Basic statements (singular statements that agree or clash with a universal statement) are never derivable from universal statements alone (p. 256, note *4).
  5. *Use derivable negated basic statements.* These form an infinite class. Only finitely many falsifying basic statements can be accepted. The falsity-frequency complement is then 1 for every self-consistent hypothesis (p. 256). Note *4 says almost any theory is "verified" by almost all instances (p. 256).
  6. *Hypothesis as an element of a sequence of hypotheses.* The reference sequence can be chosen in many ways (section 71). We can never know whether a hypothesis is true, so we cannot speak of a truth-frequency within a sequence of hypotheses. Using non-falsified over falsified hypotheses gives probability 1 in every infinite sequence. In a finite sequence of $n$ hypotheses, learning that a hypothesis is false would lower its probability by $1/n$ (so a falsified hypothesis would get e.g. 3/4 instead of 0). This contradicts the programme of expressing reliability through evidence (p. 257).
- **Note *5** summarises two kinds of definition. The first counts the testable statements of the theory that turn out true. The second treats the theory as an element of a class of theories. Popper says both give results unacceptable to adherents of probability theory of induction. He says Reichenbach's reply summarised pages of Popper's own book without saying so. He draws the rule that one should strengthen an opponent's position before criticising it (p. 258).
- **Conclusion:** identifying the probability of hypotheses with the probability of events is "a complete failure". This holds whether or not all physical hypotheses are really probability statements. It also holds if one separates deterministic "precision laws" from "probability laws". Both kinds can never become "probable", only corroborated, i.e. "prove their mettle" under tests (p. 259).
- **Diagnosis of the opposite view.** Jeans first agrees that nothing is known for certain but then says a theory is "almost certain" to be true (p. 259). The commonest error is thinking that probability hypotheses can themselves only be probable (p. 259).
  - A persuasive argument for it: such hypotheses are logically neither verifiable (they are universal) nor strictly falsifiable (no basic statement contradicts them). Reichenbach calls them "undecidable" (pp. 259–260).
  - The symmetry between verifiability and falsifiability accepted by classical inductivist logic suggests the belief that a continuous scale of degrees of validity, with truth and falsity as unattainable limits, can be correlated with these statements (Reichenbach is quoted) (p. 260).
  - **Popper's answer:** being undecidable, probability statements are metaphysical unless we adopt a methodological rule making them falsifiable. As a matter of logical form, their non-falsifiability means they cannot be empirically corroborated at all, not that they can be better or worse corroborated. Otherwise, since they rule out nothing, every relevant instance would count as corroborating them (p. 260).
- Popper believes physics uses probability assumptions as falsifiable statements, but declines to argue about how physicists actually proceed. What he says can be shown is that his own view is internally consistent and free of the difficulties of other views. He admits it cannot be proved correct (p. 260, with note *6).

*Intuition note.* A hypothesis is not a bag of statements with a percentage of "true ones". It is a universal claim with infinitely many consequences, and each way of counting that Popper examines gives an unacceptable or subjective number. So a record of how often it has passed cannot be turned into a degree of probability.

### §81 Inductive logic and probability logic (pp. 261–263)
- Popper doubts any concept of "probability of hypotheses" can express a degree of validity and still be tied to relative frequency. For the sake of argument he supposes one exists, and asks what it does to the problem of induction (p. 261).
- Take Schrödinger's theory called "probable". This statement is its **appraisal**. It must be synthetic (non-tautological), like the statements that the theory is true or false, and, like the theory itself, non-verifiable (it appears it cannot be deduced from basic statements with finality). So we must ask how it is justified or tested, and the problem of induction returns (p. 262).
- Note *1: the statement $p(S,e)=r$ (relative logical probability of $S$ given evidence $e$) may be tautological. The appraisal $p_k(S)=r$ ("today's probability") needs a "rule of absolution". If this rule is analytic, $p_k$ has no practical significance as acceptability. In a sufficiently large universe $p_k(t,e)\approx 0$ for every universal theory $t$ when $e$ consists only of singular statements, yet we do accept some theories. If $p_k$ is read as degree of adequacy, the rule is simply false (p. 262).
- **The regress:** an appraisal is either asserted "true" (a priori synthetic) or called "probable" (needing an appraisal of the appraisal, and so on). A "principle of induction" assigned a probability continues the regress. Assigned truth, it faces regress or apriorism (pp. 262–263). **Heymans** is quoted as saying probability theory cannot explain inductive arguments, because the conclusion goes beyond the premises (p. 263).
- **Result:** nothing is gained by replacing "true" with "probable". Only taking the asymmetry between verification and falsification into account avoids the pitfalls (p. 263).
- Believers in probability logic may reply that the criticism springs from a mentality tied to the framework of classical logic; Popper freely admits he is incapable of following their methods of reasoning (p. 263). In note 1 (added in proof), he allows that some formal system for degrees of corroboration might share limited analogies with the calculus of probability (e.g. Bayes's theorem). He is satisfied that it is quite impossible to tackle the problem of induction by such methods with any hope of success (p. 261).

*Intuition note.* Saying "probably true" is still a claim about the world that needs support. The support then needs support, and so on. Falsification avoids this because a single accepted basic statement can contradict a theory logically.

### §82 The positive theory of corroboration (pp. 264–267)
- **Objection to Popper:** he too uses appraisals, such as "corroborated" and "provisional conjecture" (p. 264).
- **Reply 1:** "provisional conjecture" has the status of a tautology. It paraphrases that strictly universal statements cannot be derived from singular ones (p. 264).
- **Reply 2:** the corroborative appraisal is not a hypothesis. It can be derived from the theory plus the accepted basic statements. It says these do not contradict the theory, taking into account the theory's testability and the severity of tests, up to a stated time (p. 264).
- Incompatibility is falsification. Compatibility alone does not justify a positive degree of corroboration, because any number of systems (including metaphysical ones) are compatible with the accepted basic statements (pp. 264–265).
- **Tentative rule:** a positive degree if the theory is compatible with the accepted basic statements and a non-empty subclass of them is derivable from it together with other accepted basic statements. Popper has no serious objection but finds it insufficient. Note *1 says this rule becomes his demarcation criterion if the restriction to accepted basic statements is dropped. It becomes an adequate definition of "positively corroborated" if one restricts to results of sincere attempts to refute (p. 265).
- **Counting instances is not enough.** The hypothesis that all crows are black has presumably many more corroborating statements than Millikan's value of the electronic charge. Millikan's hypothesis is still better corroborated (p. 266).
- Degree of corroboration depends on the **severity of tests**, which depends on testability and so on simplicity. The simpler or more falsifiable hypothesis is more corroborable. Actual corroboration may still be slight, or the theory may even be falsified. It also drops if the theory is superseded by a better testable theory from which it can be deduced (p. 266).
- Degrees of corroboration may not be comparable in all cases. Popper then says we can speak only roughly in terms of a positive degree, a negative degree, and so forth (p. 266). Note *2 now holds that one can define degree of corroboration so that comparison (e.g. Newton vs Einstein) and numerical values for statistical hypotheses are possible (pp. 266–267).
- **Rules and asymmetry:**
  - We do not continue to give positive corroboration to a theory falsified by an inter-subjectively testable experiment. We may still give it to a kindred theory (Einstein's photon theory, kin to Newton's corpuscular theory) (p. 267).
  - In general an inter-subjectively testable falsification is regarded as final, provided it is well tested. A later appraisal can replace positive by negative corroboration but not the reverse (p. 267).
- Popper believes theory, not experiment, opens the way to new knowledge. Experiment saves us from a track that leads nowhere (p. 267).
- The corroboration appraisal is a logical relation between the theory and the accepted basic statements, taking test severity into account (p. 267).

*Intuition note.* A theory earns credit by taking risks and surviving, not by piling up easy confirmations. Surviving a precise test of a bold claim counts for more than many easy instances.

### §83 Corroborability, testability, and logical probability (pp. 268–273)
- A theory can be better corroborated the better testable it is. Testability is the converse of **logical probability** (how little a statement says, so how likely it is to be true a priori). So corroboration takes logical probability into account. Through section 72 it is linked loosely to the probability of events (p. 268).
- **How we reason about corroboration:**
  - It rises with the number of corroborating instances. Popper says we usually give the first instances far more weight than later ones, which add only little once a theory is well corroborated, unless they come from a new field of application (p. 268).
  - A theory of higher universality, or higher precision, can be better corroborated (p. 268).
  - One reason Popper gives why palmists' prophecies get no positive corroboration is that they are cautious and imprecise, so the logical probability of their being correct is extremely high (pp. 268–269).
- **Popper's thesis:** corroborability, and the degree of corroboration of a theory that has passed severe tests, stand "as it were" in inverse ratio to logical probability. They increase with testability and simplicity (p. 269).
- **Probability logic implies the opposite.** Its upholders let the probability of a hypothesis increase with logical probability, though they mean much what Popper means by corroboration (p. 269).
- **Note *2:** Popper wavered between $C(g)=1-P(g)$ and $C(g)=1/P(g)$, where $C(g)$ is corroborability and $P(g)$ the absolute logical probability. He favours the first, or a logarithmic scale on the second (p. 269).
- **Note *3 (the crucial point):** if $h_1$ and $h_2$ are comparable in content and $h_1$ has the smaller (absolute) logical probability, then for any evidence $e$ the relative probability of $h_1$ given $e$ never exceeds that of $h_2$ given $e$. So the more testable hypothesis can never get higher probability on the evidence. So degree of corroboration cannot be probability (p. 269). The consequence is that if you value high probability, you must say very little, and tautologies keep the highest probability (p. 270).
- **Keynes's view:** for a generalization $g$ with condition $\varphi$ and conclusion $f$, "the more comprehensive the condition and the less comprehensive the conclusion", the greater its a priori probability (p. 270).
  - Popper calls this "perfectly accurate". Keynes's probability of a hypothesis therefore rises with a priori logical probability, the opposite of corroboration (p. 270).
  - Popper argues that Keynes nevertheless means by his probability what Popper means by corroboration. Keynes's probability rises with the number and the diversity of corroborating instances (pp. 270–271).
  - Popper objects that theories with instances in widely different fields usually have high universality. Keynes's two requirements, least universality and greatest diversity, are as a rule incompatible (p. 271).
  - Note *5 grants that Keynes distinguishes a priori probability from probability on evidence. He assumes, for $\varphi=\varphi_1\varphi_2$ and $f=f_1f_2$, that a priori $g(\varphi,f_1)\ge g(\varphi,f)\ge g(\varphi_1,f)$. He proves the a posteriori probabilities change the same way (p. 270).
- **Popper's diagnosis (Keynes's view):** inductive logic wants hypotheses as certain as possible, so theory content should go as little as possible beyond what is established. Note *7 says this amounts to the unacceptable rule of always choosing the most ad hoc hypothesis (p. 271). Keynes denies the value of prediction. Keynes says the peculiar virtue claimed for prediction is "altogether imaginary" (p. 271).
- This is consistent, says Popper, but it makes one ask why we generalise at all. If predictions add nothing, why not rest content with basic statements (pp. 271–272)? Note *8 says Carnap takes laws to be "not indispensable" and calls stating them "expedient". Popper replies that the real question is scientific curiosity, the aim of finding well-testable explanatory theories (p. 272).
- **Kaila's view:** simple theories with few auxiliary hypotheses have high probability because few hypotheses give fewer chances to clash with reality. Popper gives the opposite reason: such theories are corroborable because they are severely testable (p. 272). Popper asks why one would then build adventurous theories (p. 272).
- **Popper's parsimony rule** is not Kaila's. Popper wants simplicity as high testability, not fewer statements. This yields both the rule to use auxiliary hypotheses sparingly and the demand for few axioms of high universality, from which many axioms can be deduced (p. 273).

*Intuition note.* A claim that forbids a lot (precise, universal) is more likely to be wrong, so it has a low probability. For the same reason, passing tests that could easily have refuted it is more impressive. Note *3 makes this precise for two hypotheses comparable in content and any evidence (p. 269).

### §84 Remarks concerning the use of the concepts "true" and "corroborated" (pp. 273–276)
- **"True" and "false" can be avoided** in the logic of science (p. 273). Instead of saying that the prediction $p$ is true provided theory $t$ and basic statement $b$ are true, we say $p$ follows from the consistent conjunction of $t$ and $b$. A falsified theory is described as contradicted by a set of accepted basic statements. Acceptance of basic statements is a conventional decision (p. 274). Popper does not forbid using "true"; the fact that we can avoid it shows it gives rise to no new fundamental problem (p. 274).
- **Note *1 (Tarski):** Popper says Tarski's theory takes truth as correspondence with the facts, the idea he credits to Aristotle. It reduces correspondence to "satisfaction" via a semantic metalanguage, for formalised languages. After meeting Tarski he no longer hesitates to speak of truth. His views on science were clarified but not fundamentally changed (p. 273).
- **Logical concepts are timeless.** "Tautology", "contradiction", "true" and "false" describe a statement irrespective of changes in the world. If we call a statement true yesterday and false today, we implicitly say we were mistaken yesterday (pp. 274–275).
- **Corroboration is also a logical appraisal and timeless.** It asserts a logical relation between a theory and a *system of accepted basic statements*. We can never call a statement simply "corroborated". It needs a subscript for the accepted system, e.g. a date (p. 275).
- So corroboration is not a truth value. One statement can have many corroboration values, all correct at once, relative to different sets of basic statements (p. 275).
- **Pragmatists** (who define truth by success): Popper says he can agree if they merely mean that a logical appraisal of the success of a theory can be no more than an appraisal of its corroboration. Identifying corroboration with truth would not be useful. Ordinary usage says "hardly corroborated" but not "hardly true" (pp. 275–276, with note *3).

*Intuition note.* "True" is a fixed property. "Corroborated" is a record up to a given date $k$, so it can change as new tests arrive, and each dated report remains correct.

### §85 The path of science (pp. 276–278)
- One may discern something like a general direction in physics, from lower to higher universality, usually called the "inductive" direction. This does not require inductive inferences. Popper explains it by testability: a well-corroborated theory can be superseded only by a more universal, better testable one that contains it or a good approximation. He suggests it may be better to call the trend "**quasi-inductive**" (p. 276).
- In the quasi-inductive process, theories of one level are proposed and tested deductively. Higher-level ones are then proposed and tested with the help of lower ones. Testing is deductive from higher to lower levels (note *1: these deductions are explanations). The levels themselves are reached in time from lower to higher (p. 276).
- **Why not invent the highest-level theory at once?** Popper replies that conjectures of all levels are proposed. Those too far above current testable science may give rise to a metaphysical system with no new testable statement, so no crucial experiment can be designed for it (p. 277). Note *2 defines a *crucial experiment* as one designed to refute a theory, or to decide between two theories by refuting at least one, without proving the other (p. 277).
- If a crucial experiment can be designed, the system contains a well-corroborated theory as a first approximation plus something new and testable. It is then not metaphysical. Such links come as a rule from theories proposed to meet the current problem situation (p. 277).
- **Model:** ideas are particles suspended in a fluid. Testable science is the deposit that settles in layers of universality. Ideas floating in metaphysical regions may be reached by the growth of science and settle. Examples: atomism, a single physical principle, terrestrial motion, the corpuscular theory of light, the fluid theory of electricity (revived as the electron-gas hypothesis) (pp. 277–278).
- Such ideas may have brought order or led to successful predictions, but they gain scientific status only in falsifiable form, when it is possible to decide empirically between them and a rival (p. 278).

*Intuition note.* Science climbs towards general theories because each new theory must both keep what the old one explained and add new testable content. Speculative ideas become science only when they can be put at risk.

### Concluding picture of science (pp. 278–281, unnumbered)
- Science is not a system of certain or well-established statements, nor does it steadily approach finality. It is not *epistēmē* (knowledge) and can never claim to have attained truth or even probability (p. 278).
- Science is more than a biological tool. The search for truth is still the strongest motive of discovery (p. 278). "We do not know: we can only guess." Guesses are guided by a metaphysical faith in laws. Popper says we might, like Bacon, describe our science as made of rash and premature anticipations, but these bold conjectures are carefully controlled by systematic tests, and we try to overthrow them (pp. 278–279).
- Frank's view that progress comes from gathering experience and organising it better is, Popper says, not actually wrong but seems to miss the point. It resembles Bacon's induction (p. 279).
- The advance of science is not due to accumulating perceptual experiences. Science cannot be distilled from uninterpreted sense-experience. Bold ideas are our only instrument, and those unwilling to risk refutation do not take part in the scientific game (p. 280).
- Experiment is planned action guided by theory. We formulate the questions and, after attempts to elicit a clear "no", we give the answer. Weyl: nature meets theories with a decisive "No" or an inaudible "Yes" (p. 280).
- Epistēmē is an idol. Every scientific statement stays tentative. A corroboration is relative to other statements which are themselves tentative. Only subjective conviction can be "absolutely certain" (note 5: a psychological remark) (p. 280).
- The idol of certainty, including probability, falls, and with it a defence of obscurantism. Worship of the idol hampers bold questions and rigorous tests (p. 281).
- The aim is not resignation. Science aims at an infinite yet attainable goal: finding ever new, deeper and more general problems and subjecting tentative answers to ever more rigorous tests (p. 281).

### Addendum, 1972 (pp. 281–282)
- Degree of corroboration is a brief report on how the theory has stood up to tests and how severe they were (p. 281).
- **(1)** The problem of induction is not insoluble. *Negative solution:* we can never rationally justify a theory or our belief that it is true or probably true. *Positive solution:* we can sometimes rationally justify a *preference* for a theory in light of its corroboration, i.e. the present state of critical discussion (pp. 281–282). Degree of corroboration is not a measure of verisimilitude (nearness to truth), which would have to be timeless. It only reports what has been ascertained up to a moment about competing claims (p. 282).
- **(2)** Are there genuine regularities in nature? Popper says yes. One argument is that if none were apparent, neither observations nor language could exist (p. 282). **(3)** The force of this depends on some commonsense realism (p. 282).
- **(4)** The pragmatic problem of induction "solves itself": preferring the theory nearer the truth is risky but rational. **(5)** The psychological problem is, Popper suggests, trivial: belief or trust is always irrational but may be important for action. **(6)** Not all possible problems of induction are solved (p. 282).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show that a bolder claim has lower probability on any evidence, yet is more corroborable.

A fair die: six equally likely outcomes. Take two hypotheses about one roll.
- $h_1$: the roll shows 2 (allows 1 outcome of 6).
- $h_2$: the roll shows an even number (allows $\{2,4,6\}$, 3 outcomes).

$h_1$ entails $h_2$, so $h_1$ is the bolder claim (it forbids 5 outcomes, $h_2$ forbids 3).

1. Absolute probabilities: $P(h_1)=1/6$, $P(h_2)=3/6=1/2$.
2. Evidence $e$ = the roll is not 5 or 6 (outcomes $\{1,2,3,4\}$, 4 outcomes). $P(h_1\mid e)=1/4$. $h_2$ with $e$ leaves $\{2,4\}$, so $P(h_2\mid e)=2/4=1/2$. Thus $1/4\le 1/2$.
3. Evidence $e'$ = the roll is 2 or 4. $P(h_1\mid e')=1/2$, $P(h_2\mid e')=2/2=1$. Again $h_1\le h_2$. (added step) In general $P(h_1\wedge e)\le P(h_2\wedge e)$ because every outcome allowed by $h_1$ is allowed by $h_2$, so the inequality holds for any $e$ with $P(e)>0$. This is the point of note *3 (p. 269).
4. Popper's two candidate corroborability measures (note *2, p. 269): $C=1-P$ gives $h_1$: $5/6$, $h_2$: $1/2$. $C=1/P$ gives $h_1$: $6$, $h_2$: $2$. In both, the bolder $h_1$ is more corroborable.
5. A "palmist" claim, "some face shows", has $P=1$, so $C=1-1=0$ (or $1/1=1$, the minimum). It forbids nothing and gets no credit (cf. p. 268).
6. Testing: if the roll is 3, both hypotheses are refuted. If it is 4, $h_1$ is refuted and $h_2$ survives. If it is 2, both survive, but $h_1$ survived a test where 5 of 6 outcomes would have refuted it, so it is more severely tested. A single refutation settles the matter for $h_1$ (asymmetry, p. 267).

Limitation: this treats logical probability as a share of equally likely outcomes. Popper doubts numerical absolute logical probabilities for real theories (p. 269, note *2).

## Concepts
- [[corroboration]] — a timeless logical report of how a theory has stood up to severe tests relative to accepted basic statements; Popper's neutral replacement for "confirmation" (pp. 248–249, 264, 275)
- [[falsifiability]] — theories can be contradicted by accepted basic statements; the asymmetry with verification underlies corroboration (pp. 263, 267)
- [[demarcation problem]] — here only touched: the tentative corroboration rule becomes Popper's demarcation criterion if the "accepted" restriction is dropped (p. 265, note *1)
- [[probability-of-hypotheses]] (new) — the idea of a degree of validity of a hypothesis, which Popper rejects as reducible to probability of events (pp. 254–258)
- [[truth-frequency]] (new) — relative frequency of true statements in a sequence, used by Reichenbach to interpret probability of statements (p. 253)
- [[logical-probability]] (new) — probability of a statement fixed by how little it says; inversely related to testability (pp. 268–269, note *1)
- [[severity-of-tests]] (new) — how hard a test is to pass; it, not instance-count, drives degree of corroboration (p. 266)
- [[induction-regress-or-apriorism]] (new) — any principle of induction is either empirical (infinite regress) or non-falsifiable synthetic a priori (pp. 251–252, 262–263)
- [[uniformity-of-nature-as-methodological-rule]] (new) — Popper reads the principle as the rule that new theories explain old corroborated regularities and that laws hold without exception (pp. 250–251)
- [[corroboration-time-index]] (new) — every corroboration claim carries a subscript for the accepted basic statements (p. 275)
- [[quasi-inductive-evolution]] (new) — advance towards more universal, better testable theories by conjecture and deductive testing, not induction (p. 276)
- [[verisimilitude]] (new) — nearness to truth; corroboration is not its measure (pp. 281–282)
- [[analytic / synthetic distinction]] — used here: an appraisal of a theory must be synthetic, while "provisional conjecture" is a tautology (pp. 262, 264)

## Canonical instances
- [[sun-not-rising-case]] — thought experiment: a failed prediction would require a revised theory that still derives old experience (p. 250)
- [[reichenbach-die-example]] — die face probability 1/6 read as a truth-frequency of statements (p. 253)
- [[crows-vs-millikan]] — the hypothesis that all crows are black has more instances, but Millikan's charge hypothesis is better corroborated (p. 266)
- [[palmists-prophecies]] — cautious, imprecise predictions with extremely high logical probability, so not corroborable (pp. 268–269)
- [[keynes-generalization]] — Keynes's probability increases with a priori logical probability (p. 270)
- [[einstein-photon-theory]] — a kindred theory may be corroborated though a related one was falsified (p. 267)
- [[particles-in-fluid-model]] — layers of universality settle as testable science (p. 277)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Monotonicity of probability: if $h_1\Rightarrow h_2$ then $P(h_1\mid e)\le P(h_2\mid e)$ (note *3, p. 269) ↔ probability calculus: stronger statements are never more probable.
- [structural-analogy] Corroboration versus probability of hypotheses ↔ Bayesian confirmation ($P(H\mid E)>P(H)$): Popper rejects probability as a measure of evidential support, and notes only limited formal analogies with Bayes's theorem (p. 261, note 1).
- [structural-analogy] Quasi-inductive evolution with crucial experiments ↔ research programme (progressive vs degenerating): theories that carry old results plus new testable content count as advances (pp. 276–277).
- [structural-analogy] Corroboration indexed to the accepted basic statements ↔ confirmation holism / web of belief: evidence is assessed relative to other statements that are themselves tentative (pp. 275, 280).
- [metaphor] Layers of sediment ↔ growth of science through levels of universality (p. 277).

## Questions this raises
- If degree of corroboration is only a dated report, why is it rational to prefer better-corroborated theories for future action? How far does the 1972 addendum answer this (pp. 281–282)?
- How is "severity of a test" to be measured in practice, given that the unit offers only rough comparisons and later notes say a definition is possible (pp. 266–267)?
- Is Popper's argument against truth-frequency a refutation of all probabilistic accounts of hypotheses, or only of Reichenbach's frequency version (cf. note *2, p. 254; note 1, p. 261)?
- If accepted basic statements rest on conventional decisions (p. 274), how secure is a "final" falsification (p. 267)?
