# Two Notes on Induction and Demarcation, 1933–1934

## L0 — One line
Popper argues that falsifiability, not meaningfulness, separates empirical science from metaphysics, that a logic of induction is superfluous, and that hypotheses should not be given probabilities.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Two early texts: a 1933 letter and a 1934 conference remark. The question is how to draw the line between empirical science and metaphysics, and how to deal with Hume's problem of induction. Popper rejects the Vienna Circle's verifiability criterion of *meaning*, because it would also exclude natural laws. He proposes falsifiability (a claim can be refuted by a test) as a criterion of *demarcation*. Laws can be refuted by tests but never verified, so, he holds, induction has no role. Science is a system of hypotheses we keep while they survive tests. Assigning them probabilities, as Reichenbach tries, is useless and misleading. The texts show Popper's position as it stood before his book appeared.

**Prerequisites:** truth and falsity of general statements; modus tollens (if A implies B and B is false, then A is false); relative frequency as probability.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Popper separates the problem of demarcation (science vs. metaphysics) from the pseudo-problem of a criterion of meaning. He says critics misread him on this (pp. 312–313).
- Hume's problem arises from a clash between empiricism and the invalidity of inductive arguments. Schlick's escape, that laws are not genuine statements, seems to Popper to be verbal anyway (pp. 313–314).
- Popper calls the assumption shared by the older solutions unfounded: that genuine statements must be both verifiable and falsifiable. If it is dropped, we can consistently read laws as genuine statements that are falsifiable only (p. 314).
- Wittgenstein's meaning criterion would eliminate natural science along with metaphysics, because laws are not reducible to observation statements (p. 315).
- The falsifiability criterion separates science from metaphysics without calling metaphysics meaningless. Its role is formally analogous to non-contradictoriness for science in general (pp. 315–316).
- Popper believes that any theory of induction, classical or probabilistic, must lead to infinite regress or rely on an untestable a priori principle. He also holds that a theory of induction is superfluous in a logic of science (pp. 316–317).
- Hypotheses can be appraised by their deductive consequences alone, never justified or shown probable (p. 317).
- Popper thinks Reichenbach's frequency-based "probability of hypotheses" gives unacceptable results. He also thinks we shall have to get used to seeing science as a system of guesses rather than a body of knowledge (p. 318).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introductory remarks (Popper's later editorial comments) (pp. 312–313)
- **Popper's later voice:** the letter was prompted by Vienna Circle discussion of his views, though none of his manuscripts was published (p. 312).
- The Circle wrongly took him to replace the verifiability criterion of *meaning* by a falsifiability criterion of *meaning*. He says he was concerned with demarcation, not meaning (p. 313).
- He says he formulated the problem and the criterion in autumn 1919, before Wittgenstein's views were discussed in Vienna (p. 313).
- His criterion is meant to separate scientific systems from perfectly meaningful metaphysical ones. He does not claim it applies to meaningless nonsense (p. 313).

### Note 1: Letter of 1933, "A criterion of the empirical character of theoretical systems" (pp. 313–316)

**(1) Preliminary question.**
- Hume's problem of induction is the question of the validity of natural laws. It arises from an apparent contradiction between two things (p. 313):
  - the principle of empiricism, that only experience can decide the truth or falsity of a factual statement;
  - Hume's realization that inductive (generalizing) arguments are invalid.
- **Schlick's view (influenced by Wittgenstein):** laws are "not genuine statements" but rules for the transformation of statements, a kind of pseudo-statement (pp. 313–314). **Popper:** this solution seems to him verbal anyway (p. 314).
- Schlick's solution shares an unfounded assumption with apriorism, conventionalism and the older attempts. The assumption is that every genuine statement must be, in principle, completely decidable, so that both ultimate empirical verification and ultimate empirical falsification are logically possible (p. 314).
- If the assumption is dropped, the contradiction can be resolved. Popper says we can, quite consistently, interpret laws and theories as genuine statements that are only partially decidable. For logical reasons they cannot be verified but can be falsified, and they are tested by systematic attempts to falsify them (p. 314).

*Intuition note:* "All swans are white" can never be confirmed finally by observation, but one black swan refutes it. Popper builds his criterion on this one-sidedness. (The swan example is ours.)

**(2) Main problem: demarcation.**
- Demarcation is the problem of finding a criterion that distinguishes empirical-science assertions from "metaphysical" ones. Popper calls it Kant's problem of the limits of scientific knowledge (p. 314).
- **Wittgenstein's proposal:** a meaningful proposition must be a truth function of atomic propositions, that is, logically reducible to singular observation statements. Anything not reducible is meaningless, nonsensical or metaphysical (pp. 314–315).
- **Popper's objection:** this removes natural science too, since laws are no more reducible to observation statements than metaphysical utterances are. He points to the problem of induction. Applied consistently, the criterion would seem to make laws meaningless pseudo-propositions, and so the line of demarcation collapses (p. 315).
- **Popper's criterion:** falsifiability, that is, at least one-sided (asymmetrical) decidability. Statements or systems convey information about the empirical world only if they can clash with experience. More precisely, they must be systematically testable, by tests chosen under a "methodological decision" that might refute them (p. 315).
- This solves the induction problem. Popper notes that the only argument proceeding "in an inductive direction" is deductive modus tollens (p. 315).
- It also solves demarcation. Falsifiability separates empirical theories from metaphysical, conventionalist and tautological systems with sufficient precision. It does not call metaphysics meaningless, and Popper adds that metaphysics can, from a historical point of view, be seen as the source from which the theories of empirical science spring (pp. 315–316).
- He restates the criterion in a variation of an Einstein remark: so far as a scientific statement speaks about reality, it must be falsifiable, and so far as it is not falsifiable, it does not speak about reality (p. 316). Footnote 4 (added 1957) gives Einstein's original, which concerned mathematics: so far as its statements speak about reality they are not certain, and so far as they are certain they do not speak about reality (p. 316).
- **Formal analogy:** falsifiability plays for empirical science a role like non-contradictoriness for science in general. A contradictory system fails to single out a proper subset of all possible statements. Likewise a non-falsifiable system fails to single out a proper subset of all possible empirical (singular synthetic) statements (p. 316).

*Intuition note:* A system that forbids nothing cannot clash with experience, so on Popper's criterion it conveys no information about the world. Being able to clash with experience is the minimum he requires.

### Note 2: Remarks of 1934 on the "logic of induction" and the "probability of hypotheses" (pp. 316–318)
- Popper's thesis is that no satisfactory theory of "induction" is possible. Any such theory, whether in classical logic or a probability logic, must for purely logical reasons either lead to infinite regress or use an a priori principle of induction. Such a principle is synthetic and cannot be tested empirically (pp. 316–317).
- Reichenbach distinguishes a procedure of *finding* a hypothesis from a procedure of *justifying* it. Popper says finding cannot be rationally reconstructed. In his opinion, analysing justification does not lead to anything belonging to inductive logic, for a theory of induction is superfluous and has no function in a logic of science (p. 317).
- Theories can never be justified or verified. Still, under certain circumstances hypothesis A can achieve more than B, for example (p. 317):
  - B is contradicted by observations and A is not;
  - more predictions can be derived from A.
- The best we can say is that A has so far proved its worth, and has done better than rivals. We cannot say it is justified, verified or even probable. This appraisal relies only on deductive consequences, so induction need not be mentioned (p. 317).
- **Historical diagnosis:** science was seen as knowledge as certain as possible, with induction guaranteeing its truth. When certainty proved unattainable, people tried "probability" as watered-down certainty. Speaking of probability instead of truth does not escape regress or apriorism, so using probability for hypotheses is useless and misleading (p. 317).
- **Probability:** in physics and games of chance it can be defined by relative frequency (following von Mises) (pp. 317–318). **Popper on Reichenbach:** his extension to "inductive probability" or the probability of hypotheses is, in Popper's opinion, doomed to failure. Popper has no objection to "truth-frequency" in a sequence of statements, a concept he credits to Whitehead (p. 318).
- First problem: hypotheses cannot be satisfactorily read as sequences of statements. Even if they could, the result is unsatisfactory. One is led, for example, to a definition giving probability 1/2 instead of 0 to a hypothesis that has been falsified a thousand times, since this would have to be done if it is falsified by every second test result (p. 318).
- Second attempt: treat the hypothesis as an element of a sequence of hypotheses, with probability from a falsity-frequency (Popper notes in a footnote that the sequence-of-hypotheses interpretation corresponds to the view upheld by Grelling in the discussion). This too is unsatisfactory. Simple considerations show it cannot meet even the modest demand that a falsifying observation sharply lower the hypothesis's probability (p. 318).
- **Conclusion:** Popper thinks we shall have to get accustomed to seeing science not as a "body of knowledge" but as a system of hypotheses, guesses or anticipations that in principle cannot be justified. We work with them while they stand up to tests, and we are never justified in saying that we know they are true, more or less certain, or probable (p. 318).

*Intuition note:* On the frequency definition, a hypothesis falsified by every second test gets probability 1/2 rather than 0. Popper's modest demand is that a falsifying observation should produce a marked decrease in a hypothesis's probability.

## Toy example (ours)
Hypothesis H: "every coin in this jar is silver." Test coins one by one.
- Ten silver coins found: H survives ten tests. It is not verified, since coin 11 could be copper.
- Coin 11 is copper: by modus tollens (H implies coin 11 is silver; coin 11 is not silver; so H is false), H is refuted. One test suffices.

Now apply the frequency idea of p. 318. Suppose H is falsified by every second test. After 2000 tests, 1000 are falsifying and 1000 are not. The "truth-frequency" is 1000/2000 = 1/2. A frequency-based probability would give H a value of 1/2, whereas Popper says it should be 0.

## Concepts
- [[demarcation problem]] — finding a criterion to distinguish empirical-science statements from metaphysical ones; Popper calls it Kant's problem (p. 314)
- [[falsifiability]] — a statement or system conveys information about the empirical world only if it can be refuted by tests; one-sided decidability (p. 315)
- [[verifiability-criterion-of-meaning]] (new) — Circle/Wittgenstein idea: a meaningful proposition is reducible to observation statements; Popper rejects it as a basis for demarcation (pp. 314–315)
- [[problem-of-induction]] (new) — Hume's problem: the clash between empiricism and the invalidity of inductive arguments (p. 313)
- [[asymmetry-of-verification-and-falsification]] (new) — laws cannot be verified but can be falsified; modus tollens is the only "inductive-direction" argument (pp. 314–315)
- [[probability-of-hypotheses]] (new) — assigning probability to hypotheses, e.g. via truth- or falsity-frequency; Popper calls it useless and misleading (pp. 317–318)

## Canonical instances
- [[schlick-laws-as-rules]] (new) — laws treated as rules for transforming statements, a pseudo-statement view Popper rejects (pp. 313–314)
- [[wittgenstein-tractatus-criterion]] (new) — meaning as reducibility to atomic observation propositions; it would also exclude laws (pp. 314–315)
- [[hypothesis-falsified-1000-times]] (new) — a falsified hypothesis receiving probability 1/2 under a frequency definition (p. 318)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] truth-/falsity-frequency of hypotheses ↔ frequentist probability: relative frequency in a reference sequence, here argued not to fit hypotheses.
- [structural-analogy] falsifiability as a restriction to a proper subset of statements ↔ logic: non-contradictoriness. Popper himself states the analogy (p. 316).
- [metaphor] Popper's rejection of probabilities for hypotheses ↔ Bayesian confirmation: a contrasting stance, since the unit does not discuss Bayesian methods.

## Questions this raises
- If appraisal uses only deductive consequences, what exactly counts as hypothesis A having "achieved more" than B (p. 317)?
- What is a "methodological decision" about testing, and how does it fix which tests count (p. 315)?
- Does Popper's reply to Reichenbach cover other ways of assigning probabilities to hypotheses, beyond the two frequency interpretations he discusses?
- In what sense can systems of metaphysical statements be "perfectly meaningful" though not falsifiable, as Popper says (p. 313)?
