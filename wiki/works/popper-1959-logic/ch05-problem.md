# The Problem of the Empirical Basis

## L0 — One line
Basic statements, the singular statements on which tests of theories stop, are accepted by rule-governed decision, not justified by perceptual experience, so science has no absolute bedrock.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Popper asks what kind of singular statements form the "empirical basis" on which the falsification of theories rests, and how they can themselves be falsified. He rejects psychologism, the view that experiences justify them, including its version with protocol sentences (sentences reporting immediate experience). Basic statements are singular existential (there-is) statements about observable events in a space-time region. We accept them by a decision made under rules, and they can always be tested further. This, he argues, defuses Fries's trilemma of dogmatism, infinite regress and psychologism. Theory guides experiment, and theories are chosen by how well they survive severe tests. Science is like piles driven into a swamp, not a building on bedrock.

**Prerequisites:** the distinction between universal and singular statements; falsifiability as a test by deducing consequences from a theory (earlier chapters); basic propositional logic (conjunction, negation, conditional).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Fries's trilemma (dogmatism, infinite regress, psychologism): if only statements can justify statements, the demand for justification leads to regress. To avoid both dogma and regress, Fries and almost all epistemologists who wished to account for empirical knowledge opted for psychologism (p. 75).
- Protocol-sentence theories (Carnap, Neurath) are, in Popper's charge, psychologism translated into the formal mode of speech (talk about sentences instead of experiences). Neurath's revisable protocol sentences, with no rules for revising them, would let any system be defended (pp. 77–79).
- Popper separates objective, inter-subjectively testable (testable by others) science from "our knowledge" and its feelings of conviction. Those interest only the psychologist (pp. 79–80).
- Basic statements must meet two logical conditions: nothing observable follows from a universal statement without initial conditions, and a basic statement can contradict a universal one. Popper lays down the rule that they have the form of singular existential statements, which meets both. They must also describe an observable event (pp. 82–84).
- Any test stops at some basic statement that we decide to accept. Logic does not force the stopping point, and testing can resume (pp. 86–87).
- Basic statements are conventions to that extent: decided by agreement during the testing of theories, not collected as stray experiences. Theory dominates experiment (pp. 88–90).
- Theories are chosen by how well they hold their own in competition, with severity of tests taken first (the conventionalist puts simplicity first), not by experiential justification. The decision enters at singular, not universal, statements. The jury analogy illustrates decision versus justification (pp. 91–93).
- The swamp-and-piles image: no "given" base exists (p. 94).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening.** Falsifiability of theories has been reduced to falsifiability of basic statements. Popper asks what kind of singular statements these are and how they can be falsified. The practical research worker may care little, but obscurities and misunderstandings surround the problem (p. 74).

### 25. Perceptual experiences as empirical basis: psychologism
- Many accept as obvious that the empirical sciences reduce to sense-perceptions. Popper says this doctrine stands or falls with inductive logic and rejects it along with induction. He does not deny a grain of truth in the view that mathematics and logic are based on thinking and the factual sciences on sense-perceptions, but what is true in it has little bearing on the epistemological problem (p. 74).
- **Fries's view (as Popper reports it):** if scientific statements are not to be accepted dogmatically, they must be justified. If the justification is logical, statements can be justified only by statements, so the demand for proof (Fries's "predilection for proofs") is bound to lead to infinite regress (p. 75).
- To avoid both dogmatism and regress, it seems as if one can only turn to psychologism: statements can be justified by perceptual experience too. Fries, and with him almost all epistemologists who wished to account for empirical knowledge, opted for it. Fries taught that sense-experience gives "immediate knowledge", which justifies "mediate knowledge" expressed in language, including science (p. 75).
- **Sensationalists and positivists (as reported):** scientific statements "speak of our experiences". Perception is the sole source of knowledge, and a feeling of conviction distinguishes true statements from false ones. Science is merely an attempt to classify and describe this perceptual knowledge (pp. 75–76).
- **Popper's objection (in his opinion):** this founders on the problems of induction and of universals. Every description uses universal names, so every statement has the character of a theory or hypothesis. *Here is a glass of water* cannot be verified by any observational experience. "Glass" and "water" denote bodies with law-like behaviour, while an immediate experience is given only once and is unique. Universals cannot be reduced to classes of experiences (p. 76).

*Intuition note:* Even the plainest report (a glass of water) says more than any single experience shows, because its universal words cannot be matched to one specific experience. So it works like a hypothesis and cannot be certain.

### 26. Protocol sentences
- **Reininger:** correspondence between statement and fact is nothing but a logical correspondence between statements of different levels of universality, ultimately with statements recording experiences (p. 77).
- **Carnap:** the logic of science speaks of sentences, not objects (the formal mode of speech, against the material mode). Popper says he can agree with this. In the logic of science, Carnap says, sentences may be said to be tested only by comparison with other sentences (p. 77).
- **Popper's charge:** Carnap nevertheless retains psychologism, since protocol sentences are said to need no confirmation, to serve as basis for all other sentences, and to describe the "contents of immediate experience". The protocol theory is "psychologism translated into the formal mode of speech" (pp. 77–78).
- **Neurath:** protocol sentences should contain words like "see" together with the full name of the author. Like Reininger, he holds that protocol sentences can sometimes be rejected, against Carnap's earlier view (since revised) that they are ultimate. Reininger describes a method for testing his elementary statements in cases of doubt (deducing and testing conclusions). Neurath gives none. He only remarks that we can either delete a protocol sentence that contradicts a system or accept it and modify the system (p. 78).
- **Popper's verdict:** revisability is a notable advance. Apart from the mere translation into the formal mode, it is Neurath's only advance upon Fries's immediacy theory. But without rules limiting the arbitrariness of deleting or accepting, empirical statements are no longer distinguished from other statements. Every system becomes defensible, and given a good supply of protocol sentences it could even be confirmed by witnesses. Neurath avoids one form of dogmatism, but he unwittingly throws empiricism overboard (pp. 78–79).
- If protocol sentences can be overthrown by theories, their function is unclear. Since Neurath does not try to solve the problem of demarcation, it seems to Popper that his idea of protocol sentences is merely a relic of the view that empirical science starts from perception (p. 79).

### 27. The objectivity of the empirical basis
- Popper wishes to distinguish sharply between objective science and "our knowledge". He readily admits that only observation gives knowledge concerning facts, but this awareness does not justify or establish the truth of any statement. He does not believe epistemology should ask how someone who has had experience S can justify his description of it and defend it against doubt, even if "experience" is changed to "protocol sentence". It should ask how we test scientific statements by their deductive consequences, and which consequences can be selected if they are in turn to be inter-subjectively testable (pp. 79–80). A later starred note rephrases this as: how can we best criticize our theories, rather than defend them against doubt (p. 80, note *1).
- **Analogy with logic:** psychologism about logic (an inference justified by the felt compulsion to think so) is now perhaps a thing of the past. Nobody would justify an inference by writing in the margin a protocol of having felt acute conviction while checking it. Popper says that for logic and for empirical science alike the answer is the same: our knowledge may be linked with feelings of conviction (in the empirical case, with "perceptual assurance"), but all this interests only the psychologist, not the logical connections that alone interest the epistemologist (p. 80).
- A widespread belief says *I see that this table here is white* has a profound advantage over *This table here is white*. Popper replies that, for evaluating possible objective tests, the first statement, which speaks about me, does not appear more secure than the second (p. 81).
- A logical chain is made secure by breaking it into small steps anyone trained can check. Likewise an empirical statement can be presented, by describing experimental arrangements, so that anyone with the relevant technique can test it. A critic who rejects it must formulate a contradicting assertion and give test instructions; telling us his feelings of doubt or conviction will not satisfy us (p. 81).
- An assertion that is untestable because of its logical form can at best be a stimulus that suggests a problem (Fermat's problem, sea-serpent reports). Science then suspends judgment (p. 81).
- Viewed as a tool or means of production, science is no more closely tied to "our experience" than other instruments. It is not incorrect to call science an instrument to predict later experiences from given ones, but Popper thinks this talk does not contribute to clarity. It has hardly more point than the not incorrect remark that an oil derrick's purpose is to give us the sight and smell of oil (p. 82).

### 28. Basic statements
- Role: we need them to decide whether a theory is falsifiable (section 21) and for the corroboration of falsifying hypotheses (section 22) (p. 82).
- **Conditions.** (a) From a universal statement without initial conditions, no basic statement can be deduced. (b) A universal statement and a basic statement can contradict each other (pp. 82–83).
- Condition (b) can be satisfied only if the negation of a basic statement can be derived from the theory it contradicts. Together with (a), this means a basic statement must have a logical form such that its negation cannot be a basic statement (p. 83).
- Singular statements can differ in logical form from their negations, as universal and existential statements do. *There is a raven in the space-time region $k$* is a *singular existential* (singular there-is) statement. *There is no raven in region $k$* is a *singular non-existence* statement (pp. 83–84).
- **Rule:** basic statements have the form of singular existential statements (p. 84).
  - Condition (a) is met, since a singular existential statement can never be deduced from a strictly universal statement (a strict non-existence statement).
  - Condition (b) is met, since omitting the reference to region $k$ yields a purely existential statement, and that may contradict a theory (p. 84).
- **Note *1 (later addition):** nothing observable follows from a pure all-statement. *All swans are white* and *All swans are black* do not contradict each other, and together merely imply that there are no swans (p. 82, note *1).
  - Singular statements deducible from purely universal ones cannot be basic statements. Examples are instantial statements such as *If there is a swan at $k$, then there is a white swan at $k$*. They cannot play the role of test statements (potential falsifiers). If they could, both theories would get an overwhelming (indeed infinite) number of "verifications", given that most of the world is empty of swans (p. 83, note *1).
  - Their negations are potential falsifiers and may be basic statements if the conditions stated in the text are satisfied. Basic statements have greater informative content than their instantial negations (p. 83, note *1).
- **Conjunction:** the conjunction of two basic statements that do not contradict each other is a basic statement. Joining a basic statement to a non-basic one may sometimes also give a basic statement (p. 84).
  - Example: $r$ = *There is a pointer at the place $k$*, $\bar p$ = *There is no pointer in motion at the place $k$*. Then $r\cdot\bar p$ is equivalent to *There is a pointer at rest at the place $k$* (p. 84).
  - If theory $t$ with initial conditions $r$ yields prediction $p$, then $r\cdot\bar p$ is a falsifier of the theory, and so a basic statement. The conditional $r\to p$ is no more basic than $\bar p$, since it is equivalent to the negation of $r\cdot\bar p$ (p. 84).
- **Material requirement:** the event must be observable, i.e. basic statements must be testable inter-subjectively by observation, which can only refer to observers suitably placed in space and time (p. 84).
- Popper denies that this readmits psychologism. He says "observable" might just as well be replaced by "an event involving position and movement of macroscopic physical bodies". A theory that is inter-subjectively testable will also be inter-sensually testable. So his theory is neutral between psychologism and mechanism (p. 85).
- Popper does not intend to define "observable". He proposes to introduce it as an undefined primitive term that becomes precise in use, as the epistemologist learns "symbol" or the physicist "mass-point". Observations may be psychological, but observability is not (p. 85).
- **Definition (material mode):** basic statements assert that an observable event is occurring in a certain individual region of space and time (p. 85).

*Intuition note:* A falsifier is a there-is statement, which can clash with a theory saying that nothing of this kind exists. Instantial statements (if swan, then white) cannot serve as test statements.

### 29. Relativity of basic statements; resolution of Fries's trilemma
- Every test must stop at some basic statement we decide to accept, or the test leads nowhere. Logically, nothing ever compels us to stop at this basic statement rather than that. Any basic statement can be tested again with basic statements deducible from it with the help of some theory, and this procedure "has no natural end" (p. 86).
- In this way we stop at statements especially easy to test, about which investigators are likely to agree. If they do not, they continue the tests or start over. If agreement became impossible, language as a means of universal communication would fail (a new "Babel of Tongues") and scientific discovery would be reduced to absurdity (p. 86).
- Statements about personal experiences (protocol sentences) are clearly not easily testable, so they are not very suitable as stopping points. Records such as test certificates can be re-examined if needed. In general, and especially in critical cases, we stop at easily testable statements, not at perception or protocol sentences as Carnap recommends. Popper denies that we stop at perception statements because their testing is complicated (p. 87; p. 86 note 1, where he accepts Carnap's report of his theory except for a few details).
- A later starred note says Carnap's paper erroneously attributed this view to Popper (p. 87, note 2).
- **Resolution of the trilemma** (p. 87):
  - *Dogmatism:* the basic statements at which we stop have the character of dogmas only in so far as we may desist from justifying them by further tests. This is innocuous, since they can easily be tested further if needed.
  - *Infinite regress:* the chain of deduction is in principle infinite, but this is innocuous because nothing is to be proved by it.
  - *Psychologism:* the decision to accept a basic statement is causally connected with our experiences, which can motivate a decision. But a basic statement cannot be justified by them, no more than by thumping the table (pp. 87–88).

### 30. Theory and experiment
- Basic statements are accepted as the result of a decision or agreement, and to that extent they are conventions. The decisions follow a procedure governed by rules. A rule of special importance says we should not accept stray, logically disconnected basic statements, but should accept them in the course of testing theories (p. 88).
- **Naive empiricist's picture (rejected):** collect experiences first and climb the ladder of science, or collect protocol sentences. Popper points out that the order "Record what you are now experiencing" is ambiguous, and that no collection of statements gathered this way could add up to a science. A science needs points of view and theoretical problems (p. 88).
- Agreement on basic statements usually arises on the occasion of applying a theory, as a purposeful action guided by theoretical considerations (pp. 88–89).
- **Whitehead's problem** (why the tactile and visual breakfast, and the tactile and visible *Times*, come together): the inductive logician must find these coincidences "accidental", because he is committed to the view that theories are nothing but statements of regular coincidences. On Popper's view the connections between experiences are explicable and deducible in terms of theories under test. The question why we are lucky in our theories, why there are natural laws, is metaphysical (p. 89).
- **Experiment:** the theoretician puts definite questions to the experimenter, who tries to elicit a decisive answer to these and no others, screening off other sources of error. The theoretician must long before have formulated the question as sharply as possible, so it is he who shows the experimenter the way. The experimenter's work too is largely theoretical, and theory dominates the experimental work from planning to the finishing touches (pp. 89–90).
- Examples: de Broglie's prediction of the wave-character of matter, confirmed by Davisson and Germer. The Michelson–Morley experiment led to relativity. The falsification of the Rayleigh–Jeans and Wien radiation formulae by Lummer and Pringsheim led to quantum theory. Accidental discoveries are comparatively rare (p. 90).
- **Note *3 (later addition):** observations and observation statements are always interpretations in the light of theories. This is one of the main reasons why verifications are deceptively easy to find and why we need a critical attitude of trying to refute theories (p. 90, note *3).
- **Theory choice:** not due to experiential justification or logical reduction to experience. We choose the theory that best holds its own against rivals, "the fittest to survive". It is the one that has stood up to the severest tests and is testable in the most rigorous way. A theory is a tool judged by the results of its applications (p. 91).
- **Versus the conventionalist:** Popper's answer resembles the conventionalist's in that decisions settle the fate of theories and the choice is in part determined by considerations of utility. But for Popper the decision enters at the *singular* (basic) statements, not directly at universal ones. The conventionalist selects the simplest system, and for him aesthetic motives are decisive. Popper proposes that the severity of tests be taken into account first, and holds that the result of a test, i.e. agreement about basic statements, ultimately decides the fate of a theory (pp. 91–92).
- **Versus the positivist:** basic statements are not justifiable by immediate experiences but are, from the logical point of view, accepted by a free decision (p. 92).
- **Jury analogy** (pp. 92–93):
  - The verdict answers a question of fact (*quid facti?*), put sharply and shaped by the legal system (the analogue of a system of theories).
  - By its decision the jury accepts a statement about a factual occurrence, a basic statement as it were. Together with the universal statements of the law, consequences can be deduced from it.
  - The statement need not be true merely because the jury has accepted it, so verdicts can be quashed or revised.
  - The rules are designed chiefly, if not solely, to discover objective truth, yet the verdict never justifies the truth of what it asserts. The jurors' convictions are motives, not justifications. Rules differ (e.g. simple or qualified majority), so the link between conviction and verdict varies.
  - The judge's judgment is reasoned and open to logical challenge. The jury's decision can be challenged only by asking whether it followed the accepted rules of procedure.
  - Applying the legal "theory" needs a prior verdict, just as applying a theoretical system needs accepted basic statements.
- **Conclusion:** the empirical basis has nothing "absolute" about it. Science does not rest upon solid bedrock: its theories rise above a swamp, on piles driven down but not to any natural or "given" base. We stop driving them when we are satisfied that they are firm enough, at least for the time being (p. 94).

### Addendum, 1972
Popper's four points (p. 94):
1. "Basis" has ironical overtones, being a basis that is not firm.
2. He assumes a realist and objectivist standpoint and tries to replace perception as basis with critical testing.
3. Observational experiences are never beyond being tested and are impregnated with theories.
4. Basic statements are test statements and, like all language, impregnated with theories. Even a "phenomenal" language ("now here red") would carry theories about time, space and colour.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take 1,000 space-time regions. Suppose 3 contain swans and all three swans are white. Compare $T_1$: *All swans are white* and $T_2$: *All swans are black*.

- Instantial statement for region $k$ under $T_1$: *If there is a swan at $k$, then there is a white swan at $k$.* In the 997 swan-free regions the antecedent is false, so each such statement is true. That gives $T_1$ 997 trivial "verifications".
- Under $T_2$ the instantial statements are *If there is a swan at $k$, then there is a black swan at $k$*. These too are true in the same 997 swan-free regions, so $T_2$ gets the same 997 "verifications". Counting such statements as tests would therefore favour neither theory, which is Popper's reason for excluding them as test statements (p. 83, note *1).
- Take a swan region $k$. Initial condition $r$: *There is a swan at $k$.* Prediction of $T_2$: $p_2$ = *the swan at $k$ is black*. Then $r\cdot\bar p_2$ = *There is a non-black swan at $k$* is a singular existential statement. If observers agree it is true (the swan is white), $T_2$ counts as falsified.
- For $T_1$, the falsifier $r\cdot\bar p_1$ = *There is a non-white swan at $k$* is judged false in all 3 swan regions. So $T_1$ survives these three tests. Corroborating it requires a prior decision to accept those basic statements, which could in principle be retested (e.g. by other observers).

## Concepts
- [[basic-statement]] (new) — singular existential statement that an observable event occurs in a given space-time region; the form a test statement must have (pp. 83–85)
- [[falsifiability]] — theories are testable by deriving basic statements that could contradict them; here reduced to the falsifiability of basic statements (p. 74)
- [[corroboration]] — outcome of a test that a theory survives; it too depends on accepted basic statements (pp. 82, 86)
- [[demarcation-problem]] — what separates empirical science from other systems; Popper says Neurath does not try to solve it (p. 79)
- [[psychologism]] (new) — the view that statements can be justified by perceptual experience as well as by statements (p. 75)
- [[fries-trilemma]] (new) — the choice between dogmatism, infinite regress and psychologism (p. 75)
- [[protocol-sentences]] (new) — Neurath's and Carnap's sentences representing experiences; in Popper's view psychologism in the formal mode (pp. 76–78)
- [[formal-vs-material-mode-of-speech]] (new) — speaking of words and sentences versus speaking of objects and facts (Carnap's terms) (p. 77)
- [[singular-existential-statement]] (new) — statement of the form *there is an X in region $k$*; differs in logical form from its negation (pp. 83–84)
- [[instantial-statement]] (new) — statement deducible from a universal one (*if swan at $k$, then white swan at $k$*); not a basic statement (p. 83, note *1)
- [[inter-subjective-testability]] (new) — property that anyone trained in the technique can test a statement, with investigators likely to agree; the requirement for observability (pp. 81, 84, 86)
- [[observable-event]] (new) — undefined primitive term, explained by psychologistic or mechanistic examples; not itself psychological (p. 85)
- [[conventionalism]] (new) — Popper's account of the view that universal statements are chosen by simplicity; contrasted with his decisions on singular statements (pp. 91–92)
- [[theory-ladenness]] (new) — observation and test statements are interpretations in light of theories (p. 90, note *3; p. 94)
- [[underdetermination]] — nearest canon term, but not stated in this unit; the unit says only that testing basic statements has no natural end (p. 86)

## Bridges (candidates)

## Questions this raises
- Neurath's problem was too few rules for deleting protocol sentences. Do Popper's "rules" for accepting basic statements actually limit arbitrariness enough, and what are they (p. 79, p. 88)?
- If "observable" is an undefined primitive, can the epistemologist settle disputes about which events count as observable (p. 85)?
- How do observations "impregnated with theories" differ from circular justification, given the call to test with other theories (p. 90, note *3; p. 94)?
- Popper calls the question why natural laws exist "metaphysical". What status does such a question have (p. 89)?
