# A Survey of Some Fundamental Problems

## L0 — One line
Popper argues that science cannot justify its theories by induction, so he proposes to analyse it as deductive testing, and to mark it off from metaphysics by falsifiability.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Popper asks what the method of the empirical sciences is. The usual answer is induction, but he argues that no principle of induction can be justified without infinite regress (an endless chain of justifications) or apriorism (accepting it as valid before experience). He replaces it with deductive testing: theories are conjectured, consequences are deduced, and any false consequence refutes the theory. Because verification and falsification are asymmetric, he proposes falsifiability (open to refutation by experience), not verifiability, as the criterion separating empirical science from metaphysics. He offers this as a convention to be judged by its fruitfulness. Scientific objectivity means inter-subjective testability (testable by anyone); feelings of conviction justify nothing.

**Prerequisites:** universal vs singular statements; deduction (valid inference); tautology; modus tollens (if A then B; not B; so not A).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The problem of induction: it is far from obvious that any number of singular observations justifies a universal statement; Popper argues that a principle of induction would have to be synthetic, and that justifying it leads to infinite regress or apriorism (pp. 3–6).
- Probability does not rescue induction, because a "probable" principle of induction itself needs justification, and so on (p. 6).
- Popper assumes that the logic of knowledge analyses only the testing of an already-stated idea; how ideas arise is a matter for psychology (pp. 7–8).
- Deductive testing: conclusions are drawn from a tentative hypothesis and compared with experience. Passing a test gives only temporary support ("corroboration"), while one falsification refutes (pp. 9–10).
- Popper thinks the demarcation problem is more fundamental than induction. The inductivist/positivist solutions fail, since they would exclude natural laws as meaningless (pp. 11–14).
- Popper's demarcation criterion is falsifiability. It rests on the asymmetry between verifying and falsifying universal statements, and it is a convention, not a discovery (pp. 15, 18–19).
- Falsifiability dissolves Hume's problem, because falsification needs only deductive logic. The empirical method is defined to exclude evasions of falsification (pp. 19–20).
- Objectivity means inter-subjective testability. Subjective conviction cannot justify a statement; since scientific statements must be objective, basic statements must also be inter-subjectively testable, so there are no "ultimate" statements (pp. 22–26).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 3).** Scientists put forward statements or systems of statements and test them. Popper proposes that the logic of scientific discovery (the logic of knowledge) should give a logical analysis of this procedure, that is, of the method of the empirical sciences (p. 3).

### 1 The problem of induction (pp. 3–7)
- It is usual to call an inference "inductive" if it goes from singular statements (reports of observations or experiments) to universal statements (hypotheses or theories). On the widely accepted view that Popper opposes, empirical science is characterised by inductive methods, so the logic of discovery would be inductive logic (pp. 3–4).
- Such inferences are not obviously justified, since the conclusion may always be false. Many white swans do not justify "all swans are white" (p. 4).
- The problem of induction asks whether and under what conditions inductive inferences are justified. It can also be put as the question of the truth of experience-based universal statements. An experience report can only be singular. So "known from experience" usually means the universal statement is somehow reducible to known singular ones, and asking whether natural laws are known to be true appears to be only another way of asking whether induction is justified (p. 4).
- Justifying induction requires a **principle of induction**, a statement that puts inductive inferences into acceptable form. **Reichenbach's view:** this principle determines the truth of scientific theories, and without it science could not distinguish its theories from the poet's fancies (p. 4).
- The principle cannot be a tautology or analytic statement. If it were, inductive inferences would be purely logical transformations and there would be no problem. So it must be synthetic, a statement whose negation is logically possible, and we must ask why accept it (p. 5).
- **Reichenbach:** science accepts it and no one seriously doubts it in everyday life. **Popper:** even if so, the whole of science might err. He holds that the principle is superfluous and leads to logical inconsistencies (p. 5).
- Regress argument, with Hume's work as background: the principle must itself be a universal statement. If its truth is "known from experience", justifying it needs inductive inferences, hence a higher-order principle, and so on, giving an infinite regress (p. 5).
- Kant took the principle (as "universal causation") to be a priori valid. Popper judges this attempt unsuccessful (pp. 5–6).
- **Probabilistic version.** **Reichenbach:** induction decides upon probability, since science can reach only degrees of probability between unattainable truth and falsity (p. 6). **Popper:** this fails too. A probability assigned by induction needs a new, modified principle, which needs justification in turn. Calling the principle "probable" gains nothing. Probability logic leads to infinite regress or apriorism (p. 6).
- Popper's alternative is the theory of the **deductive method of testing**: a hypothesis can only be empirically tested, and only after it has been advanced. This view might be called "deductivism", in contrast to "inductivism". Belief in inductive logic is largely due to confusing psychological and epistemological problems (p. 7).

*Intuition note.* More observations never turn "some" into "all". Any rule that claimed to license the step would itself be a general claim, so it would need the same kind of backing, and the backing never ends.

### 2 Elimination of psychologism (pp. 7–9)
- To Popper, conceiving a new idea seems neither to call for logical analysis nor to be open to it. How an idea occurs is a question for empirical psychology (Kant's *quid facti*, question of fact). The logic of knowledge asks questions of justification or validity (*quid juris*): can a statement be justified, is it testable, does it depend on or contradict others? (p. 7).
- A statement can be examined logically only after someone has formulated it. So Popper separates conceiving an idea from examining it logically. He assumes that the logic of knowledge consists solely in investigating the methods of the systematic tests every new idea must undergo (pp. 7–8).
- **Objection:** epistemology should give a "rational reconstruction" of the steps leading to a discovery. **Reply:** reconstructing the release of an inspiration is, he says, a matter for empirical psychology and hardly for logic. Reconstructing the later tests is acceptable. This gives only a logical skeleton of testing, not the real thought-processes (p. 8).
- Popper's arguments are independent of this issue. His own view is that there is no logical method of having new ideas. Every discovery contains "an irrational element" or "creative intuition" (Bergson's sense). Einstein is cited: no logical path leads to the highly universal laws, only intuition (pp. 8–9).

### 3 Deductive testing of theories (pp. 9–10)
- Conclusions are deduced from a tentatively advanced hypothesis and compared with each other and with other statements to find their logical relations (equivalence, derivability, compatibility, incompatibility) (p. 9).
- **Four lines of testing** (p. 9):
  1. Internal consistency, by comparing conclusions among themselves.
  2. Logical form, to see whether the theory is empirical or, for example, tautological.
  3. Comparison with other theories, to see whether it would be a scientific advance if it survives.
  4. Empirical application of its derivable conclusions.
- The fourth line is also deductive. Using other, previously accepted statements, singular "predictions" are deduced. Those are selected that are not derivable from the current theory, especially those it contradicts. They are then compared with experiments and applications (p. 10).
- If the decision is positive (verified), the theory has passed for the time being. If negative, the falsification also falsifies the theory from which the conclusions were deduced (p. 10).
- A positive decision supports a theory only temporarily, since later negative ones may overthrow it. A theory that withstands detailed, severe tests and is not superseded is "corroborated" (p. 10).
- No inductive logic appears in this procedure. Popper never assumes that singular truths establish theories as true, or even as probable (p. 10).

*Intuition note.* Theories are put to risky tests. A falsified conclusion falsifies the theory it was deduced from, while a passed test only says "not refuted yet".

### 4 The problem of demarcation (pp. 10–16)
- **Anticipated objection:** by rejecting induction, Popper removes what separates science from metaphysics. **Reply:** he rejects inductive logic precisely because it gives no suitable criterion of demarcation (pp. 10–11).
- The **problem of demarcation** is that of finding a criterion to distinguish the empirical sciences from mathematics, logic and metaphysical systems. Hume's problem is induction. Kant's problem is demarcation, which Popper thinks is the more fundamental (p. 11).
- The main reason why empiricist epistemologists tend to trust induction seems to be their belief that it alone gives a demarcation criterion (p. 11). Older positivists admitted only concepts "derived from experience". Modern positivists admit only statements reducible to elementary statements of experience (atomic propositions, protocol-sentences). Popper says this implied criterion is identical with the demand for inductive logic (pp. 11–12).
- Positivists treat demarcation as a discovery about the nature of things and try to show metaphysics is nonsense. If "meaningless" just meant "not belonging to empirical science", the charge would be trivial. Their real aim is the annihilation of metaphysics (pp. 12–13).
- Attempts to define "meaningful" reiterate the inductivist criterion. **Wittgenstein (Tractatus):** every meaningful proposition is logically reducible to elementary propositions, "pictures of reality". **Popper's criticism:** this coincides with the inductivists' criterion of demarcation (if their word "scientific" is replaced by "meaningful"), and scientific laws cannot be so reduced. Consistently applied, it rejects as meaningless the natural laws Einstein calls the physicist's supreme task (p. 13).
- **Schlick:** there is no logical justification of universal statements about reality, "simply because they are not genuine statements" (p. 14). **Popper:** this gives scientific and metaphysical systems equal status, since both come out as pseudo-statements. So positivism lets metaphysics invade science (p. 14).
- **Popper's aim:** not to overthrow metaphysics but to define "empirical science" and "metaphysics" so that we can say whether a system's study is the concern of empirical science (pp. 14–15).
- His criterion is a **proposal or convention**. Discussion of conventions is reasonable only between parties with a common purpose, and the choice of purpose is ultimately a decision beyond rational argument (p. 15). Those who want absolutely certain, irrevocably true statements will reject it (p. 15).
- The only rational support he can see is to analyse its logical consequences and show its fertility for the theory of knowledge. He admits being guided by value judgments (p. 15).
- This is not an attack on metaphysics. Some metaphysical ideas (speculative atomism) have aided science. Psychologically, Popper is "inclined to think" discovery is impossible without faith in speculative ideas (p. 16). The first task of the logic of knowledge is still to give a concept of empirical science and draw a clear line (p. 16).

### 5 Experience as a method (pp. 16–17)
- Many theoretical systems resemble the accepted system of empirical science. This is described as a great, presumably infinite, number of "logically possible worlds". Empirical science is meant to represent one world, the real world of experience (p. 16).
- The empirical theoretical system must satisfy three requirements (pp. 16–17):
  1. It must be synthetic, representing a non-contradictory, possible world.
  2. It must satisfy the criterion of demarcation (not metaphysical).
  3. It must be distinguished from other such systems.
- The third requirement is met by the fact that the system has been submitted to tests and has stood up to them, through the deductive method. On this view "experience" appears as a distinctive method, and the theory of knowledge is a theory of the empirical method (p. 17).

### 6 Falsifiability as a criterion of demarcation (pp. 17–20)
- The inductivist criterion (the positivist dogma of meaning) demands that scientific statements be "conclusively decidable": both verifying and falsifying them must be logically possible. **Schlick:** a genuine statement must be conclusively verifiable. **Waismann:** the meaning of a statement is the method of its verification (p. 17).
- **Popper:** there is no induction, so theories are never empirically verifiable. To avoid excluding natural science, we need a criterion admitting unverifiable statements. He proposes falsifiability. A scientific system need not be singled out once and for all in a positive sense, but its form must allow refutation by experience (p. 18).
- Footnote *3: falsifiability is offered as a criterion of demarcation, not of meaning. It draws a line inside meaningful language (p. 18, n. *3).
- Example: "It will rain or not rain here tomorrow" is not empirical, because it cannot be refuted. "It will rain here tomorrow" is empirical (p. 19).
- **Objection 1:** refutability is a negative requirement, odd for a discipline that gives positive information. **Reply** (promised in sections 31–46): a statement conveys more positive information the more likely it is to clash with possible singular statements, so "the more they prohibit the more they say" (p. 19).
- **Objection 2:** falsifiability might suffer the same problems as verifiability. **Reply:** there is an asymmetry. Universal statements are never derivable from singular ones but can be contradicted by them. By modus tollens one can argue deductively from the truth of singular statements to the falsity of universal ones. This is the only strictly deductive inference that goes in the "inductive direction", from singular to universal (p. 19).
- **Objection 3:** conclusive falsification is impossible. One can always add an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis, change a definition, or refuse to acknowledge any falsifying experience. **Reply:** Popper admits this is logically possible. His answer is to characterise the empirical method as excluding exactly those evasions (sections 20 f.): it exposes the system to falsification in every conceivable way, to select the fittest through a struggle for survival (pp. 19–20).
- **Hume's problem.** The root of the problem is an apparent contradiction between the fundamental thesis of empiricism (experience alone decides the truth or falsity of scientific statements) and Hume's finding that induction is inadmissible. It arises only if all empirical statements must be conclusively decidable. If we admit unilaterally decidable, especially falsifiable, statements, it disappears. Falsification uses only deductive logic (p. 20).

*Intuition note.* No pile of white swans proves "all swans are white", but one black swan refutes it. Popper builds his line between empirical science and metaphysics on this one-sided logic.

### 7 The problem of the "empirical basis" (pp. 21–22)
- Falsification needs singular statements as premisses, so the criterion appears only to shift the problem to the empirical character of singular statements. Something is gained: in research, doubt about the empirical character of singular statements rarely arises (p. 21).
- A **basic statement** is a statement that can serve as a premise in an empirical falsification, in brief a statement of a singular fact (p. 21).
- Perceptual experiences were thought to justify basic statements ("based upon", "manifest by inspection"). But statements can be logically justified only by statements, so the link stayed obscure and was described in metaphors (p. 21).
- Popper believes a solution can be found by separating subjective experiences or feelings of conviction, which can never justify a statement, from objective logical relations among statements (p. 22).

### 8 Scientific objectivity and subjective conviction (pp. 22–26)
- Following Kant, a justification is **objective** if in principle anybody can test and understand it. Popper's version: scientific statements are objective because they can be **inter-subjectively tested** (p. 22). Feelings of conviction are "subjective" and the business of psychology (p. 23).
- Kant was perhaps the first to see that objectivity is closely tied to the use of hypotheses and universal statements. Only when events recur by rule, as in repeatable experiments, can observations be tested, in principle, by anyone. Isolated "coincidences" are not accepted (p. 23).
- A significant physical effect is one regularly reproducible by anyone following the prescribed procedure. An "occult effect", with no instructions for reproducing it, is rejected. Disputes over whether unrepeatable unique events occur are metaphysical, not scientific (pp. 23–24).
- Footnote example: Miller's divergent positive result in Michelson's experiment (1921–26): since later tests were again negative, it is now customary to regard those as decisive and to put Miller's result down to unknown sources of error (p. 24, n. 6).
- No intensity of conviction, including Popper's own, justifies a statement. Even the fact that one has such a feeling can appear in science only as a psychological hypothesis, which itself needs inter-subjective testing (p. 24).
- The difficulty: objectivity removes any logical way to reduce scientific truth to experience. It also bars a favoured status for experience statements (protocol sentences), which can occur in science only as psychological statements, that is, hypotheses whose standards of inter-subjective testing (given the state of psychology then) are not very high (p. 25).
- If basic statements are inter-subjectively testable, other testable statements can be deduced from them. So "there can be no ultimate statements in science" and no statement that cannot in principle be refuted. Theories are tested by statements of lower universality, which are tested in turn, "ad infinitum" (p. 25).
- **Regress?** No. Deductive testing neither establishes nor justifies the tested statements, so the regress of section 1 does not recur (pp. 25–26). He admits that testability ad infinitum without ultimate statements does create a problem, which he does not discuss in detail here: tests cannot actually go on forever. But he demands only that every scientific statement be capable of being tested, not that it have been tested. He refuses to accept statements "resignedly" as true merely because they seem logically untestable (p. 26).

*Intuition note.* Science stands on statements that anyone can re-check. Tests cannot go on forever; Popper admits this creates a problem, but demands only that statements be testable in principle.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Hypothesis H: "Every coin in this jar is Danish."
- Check 1,000 coins, all Danish. H is not thereby shown true, because coin 1,001 may still be Swedish. This is the swan situation: no number of singular statements entails H.
- Check coin 1,001 and find it is Swedish. The singular statement "coin 1,001 is in the jar and is not Danish" contradicts H.
- Modus tollens: if H, then coin 1,001 (which is in the jar) is Danish; coin 1,001 is not Danish; so H is false. One step settles it.
- The statement "this jar holds Danish coins or does not" cannot be refuted by any coin, so by Popper's criterion it is not empirical. H can be refuted by a coin, so it is.
- The asymmetry in numbers: 1,000 confirming checks give no entailment of H, and 1 contradicting check refutes it.

## Concepts
- [[problem-of-induction]] (new) — the question of whether and how inferences from singular to universal statements are justified (p. 4)
- [[principle-of-induction]] (new) — a supposed synthetic statement that would make inductive inferences logically acceptable; Popper holds it leads to regress or apriorism (p. 4)
- [[analytic / synthetic distinction]] — Popper uses "synthetic" for a statement whose negation is logically possible (p. 5)
- [[deductive-testing]] (new) — testing a hypothesis by deducing predictions and comparing them with experience; refutation uses only deduction (pp. 7, 9–10)
- [[psychology-vs-logic-of-knowledge]] (new) — how an idea arises (psychology) versus how it is justified and tested (logic) (p. 7)
- [[demarcation problem]] — finding a criterion separating empirical science from mathematics, logic and metaphysics (p. 11)
- [[falsifiability]] — a system is empirical only if it can be refuted by experience (p. 18)
- [[corroboration]] — the status of a theory that has withstood severe tests; only temporary support (p. 10)
- [[verification-asymmetry]] (new) — universal statements are not derivable from singular ones but can be contradicted by them (p. 19)
- [[basic-statement]] (new) — a singular-fact statement that can serve as a premise in falsification (p. 21)
- [[inter-subjective-testing]] (new) — objectivity as testability in principle by anyone (p. 22)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Infinite regress of justifying induction ↔ epistemology: Agrippan-style regress of justification — each justifier needs its own justifier.
- [structural-analogy] Asymmetry of verification and falsification ↔ statistics: a null hypothesis can be rejected by data but never confirmed by it.
- [metaphor] Struggle for survival among theories (p. 20) ↔ evolutionary biology: selection of the fittest.

## Questions this raises
- If the empirical method excludes ad hoc evasions only by a methodological rule, is that rule itself a convention, and what justifies it?
- How do single falsifying statements get accepted when basic statements are themselves testable and revisable?
- Does a theory refuted by a test ever tell us which of its premises failed, given the auxiliary statements used in deduction (p. 10)?
- Can "corroboration" give any reason to rely on a theory in practice, if it is not probability?
