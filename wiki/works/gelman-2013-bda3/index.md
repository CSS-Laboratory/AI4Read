# Gelman, A., Carlin, J. B., Stern, H. S., Dunson, D. B., Vehtari, A., & Rubin, D. B. (2013). *Bayesian Data Analysis* (3rd ed.). Chapman & Hall/CRC. DOI: 10.1201/b16018

## In one paragraph
This book teaches applied Bayesian statistics as a three-step workflow. First, build a full probability model for the unknowns and the data. Second, condition on the observed data to get a posterior distribution. Third, check the fit and revise the model. It makes hierarchical models central, because they let many related parameters share information (partial pooling) without overfitting. It also makes posterior predictive checking central, so that you learn where a model fails for its purpose. The book shows how data collection and missing data enter the model, and how to turn inferences into decisions. It then supplies the computing tools: simulation, Markov chain Monte Carlo, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and approximations. The later chapters apply these to regression, generalized linear models, robust models, missing data, nonlinear models, basis functions, Gaussian processes and mixtures. The goal throughout is working inference you can check.

## The path through the work
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch01-probability]] — Bayesian data analysis means building a full probability model for unknowns and data, conditioning on the observed data to get a posterior distribution, and then checking and revising the model.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch02-single]] — With one unknown parameter, conjugate priors (beta–binomial, normal–normal, gamma–Poisson, and others) give closed-form posteriors that act as a compromise between prior and data, and vague priors need careful handling.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch03-introduction]] — With several unknowns, get the joint posterior of all of them, then integrate out (or ignore, in simulation) the nuisance parameters. In practice: draw the nuisance parameter from its marginal posterior, then the parameter of interest from its conditional posterior.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch04-asymptotics]] — With plenty of data from a well-behaved fixed model, the posterior is approximately normal and the prior matters little, which explains why Bayesian and classical answers often agree, but the chapter also lists where this breaks down and where classical ideas mislead.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch05-hierarchical]] — When many parameters are related, treat them as exchangeable draws from a population distribution with unknown hyperparameters, and do a full Bayesian analysis of everything jointly; this gives partial pooling that fits the data without overfitting.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch06-model]] — A fitted Bayesian model should be checked by simulating replicated data from its posterior predictive distribution and comparing them with the observed data, to see where and how much the model fails for its purpose.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch07-evaluating]] — Bayesian models can be compared by estimated out-of-sample predictive accuracy (the log predictive density, corrected for overfitting), the authors prefer to improve them by continuous expansion rather than discrete choice, and a model that fits well can still give bad answers when the data cannot speak to what matters.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch08-modeling]] — Bayesian inference must model how the data were collected (through an inclusion indicator $I$); designs that are "ignorable" let you ignore that model (while still conditioning on the design variables), and all others require it.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch09-decision]] — Bayesian posterior and predictive distributions become decisions when you compute the expected utility of each option, including the value of information from later data, and pick the best option.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch10-introduction]] — When the posterior cannot be handled in closed form, we approximate its integrals with random draws (rejection sampling, importance sampling) or deterministic rules. Then we check the results with fake data and simplified models.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch11-basics]] — Markov chain simulation (Gibbs sampler, Metropolis-Hastings) draws from a posterior by building a chain whose stationary distribution (one that a chain step leaves unchanged) is that posterior, and the draws are only usable after convergence is checked with several dispersed, split sequences (R̂, a between-/within-sequence variance ratio, and effective sample size, the equivalent number of independent draws).
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch12-computationally]] — Gibbs and Metropolis samplers can be made much more efficient by reparameterizing, tuning, adding auxiliary variables, and by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, which uses gradients and momentum to suppress random-walk behaviour.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch13-modal]] — When posterior simulation is too slow or impossible, one can approximate the posterior by a simpler distribution fitted at its modes, or fitted by iterative optimisation (EM, variational Bayes, expectation propagation).
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch14-introduction]] — Classical normal linear regression can be thought of as Bayesian posterior inference under a noninformative prior; simulation from that posterior gives predictions and model checks, and the same machinery extends to unequal variances and to prior information.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch15-hierarchical]] — When predictors or groupings sit at several levels, give each batch of regression coefficients its own normal population distribution with its own estimated variance; this partial pooling sits between "no pooling" and "complete pooling" and can be run as one big augmented regression.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch16-generalized]] — A generalized linear model (linear predictor + link + non-normal random component) can be handled in Bayesian fashion by repeated normal "pseudodata" regressions, stabilised by weakly informative priors, and applied, including in hierarchical models, to Poisson, logistic, multinomial and loglinear data.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch17-models]] — Replacing normal, binomial and Poisson models by longer-tailed or overdispersed mixture versions (such as the t) lets outliers have less influence, and the effect of such a choice can be checked by sensitivity analysis computed with Gibbs sampling, importance weighting or EM.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch18-models]] — Missing values are treated as extra unknowns with a joint posterior alongside the parameters, so inference alternates between imputing the missing data and drawing the parameters, and under an ignorable mechanism the missingness process need not be modelled for inference (it is still needed when simulating replicated data for model checking).
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch19-parametric]] — When parameters and predictors do not combine linearly, the usual Bayesian recipe (build the model, compute the posterior, check the fit) still applies, but each problem generally has to be tackled afresh: it needs its own model, its computation can be challenging, and sometimes new graphical checks are needed. Two worked examples show how.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch20-basis]] — A smooth unknown regression curve can be written as a weighted sum of fixed basis functions, which makes the model linear in the weights, so selection or shrinkage priors on the weights give flexible Bayesian regression, with computation by Gibbs sampling or related MCMC.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch21-gaussian]] — A Gaussian process puts a prior directly on an unknown function, and, with a chosen covariance function (which sets smoothness and structure), the same idea supports smooth nonlinear regression and time-series decomposition through multivariate-normal formulas, and density estimation via a transformation.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch22-finite]] — A mixture model adds unobserved component indicators z to the model, so that standard hierarchical-model tools (EM/ECM, Gibbs sampling, posterior predictive checks) can fit populations made of subpopulations, with extra care needed for label switching and for the number of components.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/ch23-dirichlet]] — A Dirichlet process is a prior on an unknown distribution; used as the prior of a mixing distribution, it gives a mixture model whose number of clusters is not fixed in advance but grows with the data.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/appa-standard]] — Realistic distributions for complicated multivariate models can typically be built from a set of simple standard distributions, whose densities, moments, closure properties and simulation recipes are collected here.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/appb-outline]] — With iid data (independent, identically distributed) and suitable regularity conditions, the posterior concentrates at the parameter value $\theta_0$ that minimizes Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence (a measure of how far the model is from the truth), and it approaches a normal distribution with mean $\theta_0$ and variance $(nJ(\theta_0))^{-1}$.
- [[gelman-2013-bda3/appc-computation]] — One small hierarchical model (eight schools) is fitted with Stan, with grid and conditional simulation, with Gibbs and Gibbs–Metropolis, and with hand-written Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), to show practical Bayesian computation, tuning and debugging.

## Big ideas across units
- **The build–condition–check workflow.** Ch. 1 states the three steps. Later units fill in each step: posterior computation (ch10–ch13), model checking and comparison (ch06, ch07), and decisions made from the fitted model (ch09). Ch. 14 and ch22 repeat the cycle of fitting, checking and expanding. Units: ch01, ch06, ch07, ch09, ch10, ch14, ch22.
- **Hierarchical models and partial pooling.** Shrinkage appears first in the kidney-cancer example (ch02). Ch. 5 develops it fully, ch. 15 extends it to batches of regression coefficients, and ch. 16, ch. 17 and ch. 22 reuse it. Units: ch02, ch04, ch05, ch08, ch15, ch16, ch17, ch22.
- **Eight schools as the running example.** The same small hierarchical model is used for the posterior and the choice of τ prior, for checking, for model comparison, for HMC, for robustness and for hand-coded computation. Units: ch05, ch06, ch07, ch12, ch17, appc.
- **Checking a model, comparing models, and expanding it continuously.** Posterior predictive checks look for failure in a specific respect. Predictive accuracy compares models. Continuous expansion is preferred to discrete choice, and robust and mixture expansions are examples of it. Units: ch06, ch07, ch14, ch17, ch19, ch22.
- **Choosing priors.** The book moves from conjugate priors to noninformative, improper and weakly informative priors, to priors on group-level standard deviations, and to Cauchy and shrinkage priors on regression coefficients. Units: ch02, ch03, ch05, ch15, ch16, ch20.
- **Simulation as the working tool.** Draws from the posterior summarise inference. The book builds from simple draws (ch01, ch03) through rejection and importance sampling (ch10), Gibbs and Metropolis (ch11), and HMC (ch12), to deterministic approximations (ch13). Appendix C codes these by hand and in Stan. Units: ch01, ch03, ch10, ch11, ch12, ch13, appc.
- **Modeling data collection and missing data.** Surveys, experiments, censoring and nonresponse are all treated as missing-data problems with an inclusion indicator. Ignorability says when this indicator can be left out of the model. The same ideas return in regression and in imputation. Units: ch08, ch14, ch18.
- **Flexible, mixture-based models.** Scale mixtures give robustness. Basis functions, Gaussian processes, finite mixtures and Dirichlet processes relax parametric forms, and each is fitted with the same hierarchical and MCMC machinery. Units: ch17, ch20, ch21, ch22, ch23.

## How to read it
- **Essential first.** The catalog's focus chapters are ch01, ch02, ch05, ch06, ch07, ch08 and ch11. Together they cover the workflow, one-parameter models, hierarchical models, checking and comparison, data collection, and basic MCMC.
- **Dependencies, from the units' prerequisites:**
  - ch03 uses the conjugate models of ch02.
  - ch05 uses ch02 and ch03.
  - ch06 uses the hierarchical model of ch05.
  - ch07 uses ch05 and ch06.
  - ch08 uses ch03, ch05 and ch06.
  - ch11 uses the hierarchical normal model of ch05.
  - ch12 uses ch11 and ch05.
  - ch14 uses ch03, ch06 and ch08.
  - ch15 builds on ch14.
  - ch16 uses hierarchical models, Gibbs and Metropolis sampling, and EM.
  - ch17 uses eight schools, Gibbs sampling and EM.
  - ch18 uses ch08 and also ch11 and ch13.
  - ch21 follows on from ch20, and ch23 assumes ch22.
  - appc uses sections 5.4–5.5 of ch05, ch11 and ch12.
- **Can wait on a first pass.**
  - Ch. 4 says its large-sample results are not needed to do Bayesian analysis. They are useful approximations and aids to understanding. Appendix B only outlines their proofs.
  - Appendix A is a reference to consult when a distribution is needed.
  - Ch. 9 is an application of fitted models to decisions.
  - Ch. 13 covers approximations for when simulation is too slow, and ch. 12 covers efficiency improvements.
  - Ch. 16–23 are model families that can be read when needed. Ch. 19 is two case studies, not a general method.
- **Computing path.** Ch. 10 opens the computing sequence, and ch. 11 comes before ch. 12. Appendix C shows the computing in code.
