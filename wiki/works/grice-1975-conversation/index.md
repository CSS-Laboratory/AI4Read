# Grice, H. P. (1975). Logic and Conversation. In P. Cole & J. L. Morgan (eds.), *Syntax and Semantics, Vol. 3: Speech Acts* (pp. 41–58). New York: Academic Press. Reprinted in Grice, *Studies in the Way of Words* (Harvard UP, 1989), pp. 22–40.

## One digest
This work has a single digest, which is also its work-level summary: [[grice-1975-conversation/main]].

## In one line
Grice maintains that the belief in differences of meaning between logical words and their natural-language counterparts (*and*, *or*, *if*, *some*, *the*) is, broadly speaking, a mistake, arising from inattention to the conditions governing conversation, such as the maxims of cooperative talk, which generate "conversational implicatures" beyond what is said.

## The gist
Formalists and informalists in philosophical logic both assume that words like *and*, *or*, *if* diverge in meaning from the logical symbols ∧, ∨, ⊃. (Formalists favour the formal devices; informalists defend a logic of natural expressions.) Grice maintains that this assumption is, broadly speaking, a mistake, arising from inadequate attention to the conditions governing conversation. He proposes a Cooperative Principle with four categories of maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner). A speaker presumed to be cooperative can convey more than he says (an *implicature*), for example by blatantly failing to follow a maxim. The hearer must be able to work this out by reasoning. Grice gives examples and derives features such as cancellability (for generalized implicatures) and possible indeterminacy.

**Prerequisites:** truth-functional connectives (∧, ∨, ⊃); the idea that a sentence has a literal meaning; basic notions of rational, goal-directed behaviour.
