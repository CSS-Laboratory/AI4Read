# Logic and Conversation (H. P. Grice)

## L0 — One line
Grice maintains that the belief in differences of meaning between logical words and their natural-language counterparts (*and*, *or*, *if*, *some*, *the*) is, broadly speaking, a mistake, arising from inattention to the conditions governing conversation, such as the maxims of cooperative talk, which generate "conversational implicatures" beyond what is said.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Formalists and informalists in philosophical logic both assume that words like *and*, *or*, *if* diverge in meaning from the logical symbols ∧, ∨, ⊃. (Formalists favour the formal devices; informalists defend a logic of natural expressions.) Grice maintains that this assumption is, broadly speaking, a mistake, arising from inadequate attention to the conditions governing conversation. He proposes a Cooperative Principle with four categories of maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner). A speaker presumed to be cooperative can convey more than he says (an *implicature*), for example by blatantly failing to follow a maxim. The hearer must be able to work this out by reasoning. Grice gives examples and derives features such as cancellability (for generalized implicatures) and possible indeterminacy.

**Prerequisites:** truth-functional connectives (∧, ∨, ⊃); the idea that a sentence has a literal meaning; basic notions of rational, goal-directed behaviour.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- In Grice's sketch, formalists regard meaning that natural counterparts have beyond the formal devices as an imperfection and favour constructing an ideal language. Informalists (in the reply Grice says they might make) hold there must be a place for a more or less unsystematic logic of natural expressions. Grice maintains that their shared assumption that the divergences exist is, broadly speaking, a mistake (pp. 41–43).
- What a speaker *says* is closely related to the conventional meaning of the words. What he *implicates* can differ from what he says (pp. 43–45).
- Some implicatures are conventional. Grice's interest is a subclass of nonconventional ones, the conversational implicatures, which rest on general features of discourse (p. 45).
- The Cooperative Principle (CP) says: make your contribution as the exchange's accepted purpose requires. One may perhaps distinguish four categories of maxims under it: Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner (pp. 45–46).
- Grice wants to show that following the CP is reasonable, not just a habit. He is uncertain whether this can be done (pp. 48–49).
- Among the ways of failing to fulfil a maxim are violating it, opting out, a clash, and flouting it. A flout characteristically gives rise to an implicature; when it does, the maxim is said to be exploited (p. 49).
- A conversational implicature must be derivable by the hearer from five kinds of data, by an argument, not just intuition (p. 50).
- Figures of speech (irony, metaphor, meiosis (deliberate understatement), hyperbole) are examples of exploitation (pp. 52–53).
- Grice argues that conversational implicature has certain features: a generalized one can be cancelled; insofar as the calculation uses only what is said and manner plays no role, it is nondetachable; to speak approximately, and initially at least, implicata are not part of meaning; their truth is not required by the truth of what is said; and they may be indeterminate (pp. 57–58).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction: formalists vs informalists (pp. 41–43)
**Setting.** It is a commonplace of philosophical logic that there are, or appear to be, divergences in meaning between at least some of the *formal devices* (~, ∧, ∨, ⊃, (x), ∃(x), and a symbol that looks like ʃ x in the image, read here as the description operator ιx; see Reading notes; under a standard two-valued interpretation) and natural counterparts such as *not, and, or, if, all, some, the* (p. 41). Some logicians may at some time have wanted to claim there are no divergences; Grice says such claims, if made at all, were somewhat rashly made (p. 41). Those who concede that the divergences exist adhere, in the main, to one of two groups (p. 41).

**Formalist outline (Grice's sketch).** For very general patterns of valid inference the formal devices have a decisive advantage. A few simple formulas must be acceptable if the devices have their assigned meaning. Many less obvious formulas can then be shown acceptable from those (pp. 41–42). Meaning that natural counterparts have beyond the formal devices is an imperfection. It blocks precise definition, can leave statements without a definite truth value, and leaves a way open to metaphysics (p. 42). The proper course is to construct an ideal language, clear and determinate in truth value, as a secure foundation for science (p. 42). Grice does not say all formalists would accept the whole outline, but thinks all would accept some part (p. 42).

**Informalist reply (one Grice says an informalist might make).** It rejects three assumptions: that the main test of a language is its service to science, that an expression cannot be guaranteed as fully intelligible unless an analysis has been provided, and that every analysis must be a precise logical equivalence (p. 42). We can know what an expression means without knowing its analysis. An analysis usually specifies the conditions for or against applying the expression (pp. 42–43). Many valid natural-language inferences do not use the formal devices, so a less systematic logic of the natural counterparts is needed. It can be guided but not replaced by the formal logic, and the two sometimes conflict (p. 43).

**Grice's stance.** He will say nothing on the general place of reforming natural language; he confines himself to the dispute about the alleged divergences and does not enter it on behalf of either contestant (p. 43). He holds that the contestants' common assumption that the divergences exist is "broadly speaking" a mistake. It comes from inadequate attention to the conditions governing conversation (p. 43). He therefore turns to the general conditions on conversation (p. 43).

*Intuition note.* Grice's claim here is that the assumed divergences between logical and natural words are, broadly speaking, a mistake: what seems to differ may come from the conditions governing conversation rather than from meaning. These pages do not work out the cases of *and*, *or*, *if*.

### Implicature (pp. 43–45)
**Example.** A asks B how their friend C is doing in his bank job. B replies: *Oh quite well, I think; he likes his colleagues, and he hasn't been to prison yet.* What B implied (for example that C may yield to temptation) differs from what B *said*, namely only that C had not been to prison yet (p. 43). Grice introduces *implicate*, *implicature* and *implicatum* as terms of art, so he need not choose among *imply, suggest, mean* (pp. 43–44).

**"Say."** What is said is closely related to the conventional meaning of the sentence uttered (p. 44). For *He is in the grip of a vice* one also needs to know who *x* is, the time of utterance, and which sense of the phrase is meant (p. 44). The text leaves open whether *Harold Wilson is a great man* and *The British Prime Minister is a great man* say the same thing. Either way, the apparatus can account for implicatures linked to one singular term rather than the other (p. 44).

**Conventional implicature.** In *He is an Englishman; he is, therefore, brave*, the speaker is committed by the words' meaning to bravery following from Englishness. Grice does not want to say he has *said* that it follows, nor that the utterance would be, strictly speaking, false if it failed to follow (pp. 44–45). So some implicatures are conventional, unlike the prison one (p. 45). He will study a subclass of nonconventional ones, the **conversational implicatures**, tied to general features of discourse (p. 45).

### The Cooperative Principle and the maxims (pp. 45–49)
**Cooperative Principle (CP).** Talk exchanges are characteristically, to some degree at least, cooperative efforts, and each participant recognizes in them, to some extent, a common purpose or at least a mutually accepted direction (fixed at the start or evolving, definite or loose) (p. 45). Rough principle, expected ceteris paribus (other things being equal): make your contribution such as the accepted purpose or direction of the exchange requires, at the stage where it occurs (p. 45).

**Maxims** (echoing Kant's categories) (p. 45):
- **Quantity:** (1) Be as informative as required for the current purposes. (2) Do not be more informative than required (p. 45). Grice calls the second maxim disputable. It might be said that overinformativeness is merely a waste of time; it might be answered that it may confuse or mislead hearers about the speaker's point. There is perhaps a different reason for doubting this maxim: its effect will be secured by a later maxim, Relation (p. 46).
- **Quality:** supermaxim (a general maxim over the specific ones) "Try to make your contribution one that is true". (1) Do not say what you believe false. (2) Do not say what you lack adequate evidence for (p. 46).
- **Relation:** "Be relevant." The maxim hides hard problems about kinds of relevance and how topics shift. Grice finds them very difficult (p. 46).
- **Manner** (concerns *how* things are said): supermaxim "Be perspicuous" (be clear and easy to follow). (1) Avoid obscurity. (2) Avoid ambiguity. (3) Be brief. (4) Be orderly. Grice adds that one might need others (p. 46).

**Status.** Some maxims are less urgent than others. The first Quality maxim may be more basic, since others work only if it is satisfied, but it is treated as one of the list (pp. 46–47). Other maxims (aesthetic, social, moral, e.g. "Be polite") can also generate nonconventional implicatures (p. 47). Grice stated the maxims as if the purpose were maximally effective exchange of information. He says this is too narrow, and the scheme must allow for purposes like influencing others' actions (p. 47).

**Analogues outside talk** (p. 47): Quantity, mending a car (four screws, not two or six); Quality, baking (sugar, not salt; a real spoon, not a rubber trick spoon); Relation, mixing a cake (not a good book; an oven cloth only later); Manner, clear, prompt performance by a partner.

**Why follow the CP?** The "dull" answer is that people do behave this way, learned in childhood, and departing takes effort (p. 48). As a self-described rationalist, Grice wants to show it is *reasonable* to follow them (p. 48). He was once attracted to a quasi-contractual account. Participants share a common immediate aim, contributions are dovetailed, and there is an understanding that the exchange continues unless both agree to end (p. 48). He says this may apply to some cases, but there are too many types of exchange, like quarrelling and letter writing, for it to fit comfortably (pp. 48–49). His alternative aim is to show that anyone who cares about the central goals of communication (giving and receiving information, influencing and being influenced) must be expected to have an interest, given suitable circumstances, in talk exchanges that will be profitable only on the assumption that they are conducted in general accordance with the CP and maxims (p. 49). He is uncertain whether any such conclusion can be reached, and is fairly sure he cannot reach it until he is clearer about relevance (p. 49).

*Intuition note.* The maxims are stated as rules, but Grice presents them as what participants will be expected (ceteris paribus) to observe. He hopes to show that following them is reasonable, not just habit.

### From maxims to implicature (pp. 49–50)
**Ways of failing to fulfil a maxim: Grice names four, saying the ways include these** (p. 49):
1. **Violate** it quietly and unostentatiously. In some cases the speaker will be liable to mislead.
2. **Opt out** openly (e.g. *I cannot say more; my lips are sealed*).
3. **Clash**: for example, being as informative as required would break the Quality maxim of adequate evidence.
4. **Flout** it: blatantly fail to fulfil it. If the speaker is able to fulfil it without violating another maxim, is not opting out, and (given the blatancy) is not trying to mislead, the hearer faces a minor problem: how can his saying what he did say be reconciled with the supposition that he is observing the CP? This situation characteristically gives rise to a conversational implicature; when one is generated this way, the maxim is **exploited** (p. 49).

**Definition (pp. 49–50).** By saying (or making as if to say) that $p$, a speaker has *conversationally implicated* that $q$ provided that:
1. he is presumed to be observing the maxims, or at least the CP;
2. he is supposed to be aware that, or to think that, $q$ is required to make his saying $p$ (or doing so in THOSE terms) consistent with that presumption;
3. he thinks (and would expect the hearer to think that he thinks) that the hearer can work out, or intuitively grasp, that the supposition in (2) is required (pp. 49–50).

**Prison example reworked (p. 50).** A reasons: (1) B apparently violated "Be relevant", yet I have no reason to think he opts out; (2) given the circumstances, I can regard his irrelevance as only apparent if, and only if, I suppose him to think C is potentially dishonest; (3) B knows I can work out (2). So B implicates that C is potentially dishonest (p. 50).

**Must be calculable.** An intuitively grasped implicature counts as conversational only if the intuition can be replaced by an argument. Otherwise it is conventional (p. 50). The hearer relies on: (1) conventional meaning and references; (2) the CP and maxims; (3) context; (4) background knowledge; (5) the fact that all this is available to both parties, who know or assume that it is (p. 50). General pattern (p. 50): he has said $p$; there is no reason to suppose he is not observing the maxims, or at least the CP; he could not be doing this unless he thought $q$; he knows (and knows that I know that he knows) that I can see that the supposition that he thinks $q$ is required; he has done nothing to stop me thinking $q$; he intends me to think, or is at least willing to allow me to think, that $q$; and so he has implicated $q$.

*Intuition note.* An implicature is a conclusion the hearer reaches by asking: "Why would a cooperative speaker say this, in this way?" The speaker intends, or is at least willing to allow, the hearer to think $q$.

### Examples (pp. 50–56)
**Group A: no maxim clearly violated** (p. 51).
- (1) A: *I am out of petrol.* B: *There is a garage round the corner.* B would break "Be relevant" unless he thinks the garage may be open and have petrol, so he implicates that (p. 51).
- (2) A: *Smith doesn't seem to have a girlfriend these days.* B: *He has been paying a lot of visits to New York lately.* B implicates that Smith has, or may have, a girlfriend in New York (p. 51).
- In both, the speaker implicates what he must be assumed to believe to keep the presumption that he observes Relation (p. 51).

**Group B: violation explained by a clash** (pp. 51–52).
- (3) A is planning a holiday in France and wants to see C. A: *Where does C live?* B: *Somewhere in the South of France.* B is less informative than needed. This is explicable only if giving more would break the Quality maxim, so B implicates that he does not know which town (pp. 51–52).

**Group C: exploitation, i.e. flouting a maxim to convey an implicature, in the manner of a figure of speech** (p. 52). A maxim is violated at the level of what is said, but the hearer may assume the maxim, or at least the CP, is observed at the level of what is implicated (p. 52). Grice's own labels are kept below.
- **(1a) Flouting the first Quantity maxim.** A testimonial for a philosophy job candidate says only that his English is excellent and his tutorial attendance regular. The writer is not opting out and cannot lack knowledge, so he must wish to convey information he is reluctant to write. That is tenable only if he thinks Mr. X is no good at philosophy (p. 52). Patent tautologies (statements true by their form alone: *Women are women*, *War is war*) are totally noninformative at the level of what is said. They are informative at the level of what is implicated, and the hearer must explain why *that* tautology was chosen (p. 52).
- **(3b) Second Quantity maxim** (on the assumption that such a maxim should be admitted). Volunteering excess information about certainty and evidence for $p$ may make A doubt B's certainty if the excess seems undesigned. If designed, it may obliquely convey that $p$ is controversial. Grice says it is arguable that this could be explained by Relation without invoking an alleged second Quantity maxim (pp. 52–53).
- **(2a) First Quality maxim flouted** (p. 53).
  - *Irony:* A says *X is a fine friend* after X betrayed a secret. Since it is obvious that A does not believe it, the most obviously related proposition is its contradictory (p. 53).
  - *Metaphor:* *You are the cream in my coffee* is categorially false (false because it puts a thing in the wrong category). Its contradictory would be a truism, so the speaker presumably attributes features in which the audience resembles the substance (p. 53). Metaphor and irony can combine in two stages: the hearer is meant to reach first a metaphor reading (*You are my pride and joy*) and then an irony reading (*You are my bane*) (p. 53).
  - *Meiosis:* *He was a little intoxicated*, of a man who broke all the furniture. *Hyperbole:* *Every nice girl loves a sailor* (p. 53).
- **(2b) Second Quality maxim.** *She is probably deceiving him this evening*, with a tone or gesture showing no adequate reason. The hearer assumes a related proposition with a reasonable basis, e.g. that she is the sort to deceive her husband (pp. 53–54).
- **(3) Real violation of Relation.** At a tea party A says *Mrs. X is an old bag.* After silence B says *The weather has been quite delightful this summer, hasn't it?* B blatantly refuses to make his remark relevant. He implicates that A's remark should not be discussed and perhaps that A committed a gaffe (p. 54).
- **(4) Manner maxims flouted** (pp. 54–56).
  - *Ambiguity.* Only deliberate ambiguity, intended to be recognised, counts (p. 54). (a) No striking difference in straightforwardness between the readings: Blake's line, in the related form *I sought to tell my love, love that never told can be*. Grice says there seems to be no alternative to supposing the ambiguities are deliberate and that the poet conveys both readings, though not explicitly saying either (p. 54). (b) One reading less straightforward: the message *Peccavi* ("I have Sind"/"I have sinned"), where the ambiguity lies in the translation (pp. 54–55). Whether or not the straightforward reading is conveyed, it seems the nonstraightforward one must be, or the effort would be pointless. Whether the straightforward one is also conveyed seems to depend on whether that would fit other conversational requirements, such as relevance: if they are not satisfied it is not conveyed, and if they are, it is (p. 55).
  - *Obscurity.* A may be deliberately obscure, but not too obscure, in front of a third party such as a child, hoping B understands. If A expects B to see the deliberate obscurity, Grice thinks it reasonable to suppose A is implicating that the contents should not be imparted to the third party (p. 55).
  - *Failure to be brief.* (a) *Miss X sang "Home sweet home."* (b) *Miss X produced a series of sounds that corresponded closely with the score of "Home sweet home".* A reviewer who chooses (b) implicates a striking difference from ordinary singing, most obviously some hideous defect (pp. 55–56).

### Particularized and generalized implicature (pp. 56–57)
So far only **particularized** implicatures have appeared: carried on a particular occasion by special features of context. **Generalized** ones are those where using a certain form of words would normally, without special circumstances, carry such an implicature (p. 56). Noncontroversial examples are perhaps hard to find, since it is all too easy to treat a generalized implicature as if it were conventional (p. 56).

**Example: "an X."** *X is meeting a woman this evening* normally implicates that she is not X's wife, mother, sister or perhaps even close platonic friend. *X went into a house yesterday and found a tortoise inside the front door* would normally surprise the hearer if the house were X's own (p. 56). Sometimes there is no such implicature (*I have been sitting in a car all morning*) or a reverse one (*I broke a finger yesterday*) (p. 56). Grice is inclined to think one would not lend a sympathetic ear to a philosopher who suggested three senses of *an X*, and he prefers an account that he says may be incorrect in detail (pp. 56–57).

**Grice's account.** When using *an X* implicates that the X is not closely connected to an identifiable person, the implicature is present because the speaker has failed to be specific as he might have been expected to be, so it is likely to be assumed that he cannot be specific. Grice classes this as a failure to fulfil the first Quantity maxim (p. 57). The hard question is why specifying closeness or remoteness should presumptively matter. He says the answer must lie in this region: transactions with closely connected persons or things differ in concomitants and results from those with remotely connected ones. Finding a hole in *my* roof differs from finding one in someone else's. Information, like money, is often given without the giver knowing just what use the recipient will put it to. So if the specification would let the hearer answer many follow-up questions himself, there is a presumption that the speaker should include it (p. 57).

### Features of conversational implicature (pp. 57–58)
1. **Cancellable.** Since it is possible to opt out of the CP, a generalized implicature can be cancelled in a particular case. This can be explicit, by a clause saying or implying that the speaker opts out, or contextual (p. 57).
2. **Nondetachable.** Insofar as the calculation needs only what is said (or the conventional commitment of the utterance), and manner plays no role, it will not be possible to find another way of saying the same thing that simply lacks the implicature. The exception is where the substituted version's special feature is relevant through a Manner maxim. Grice therefore expects a generalized implicature carried by a familiar, nonspecial locution to have a high degree of nondetachability (pp. 57–58).
3. **Not part of the expression's conventional meaning.** To speak approximately, since the calculation presupposes knowledge of conventional force, the implicatum is not included in that original specification. It may not be impossible for an implicature to become conventionalized, but supposing so in a given case needs special justification. So, initially at least, implicata are not part of the meaning of the expressions (p. 58).
4. **Truth of the implicatum is not required by the truth of what is said.** What is said may be true and what is implicated false. The implicature is carried by the saying, or by putting it that way (Grice's phrase), not by what is said (p. 58).
5. **Possible indeterminacy.** Since there may be various specific explanations, possibly an open list, the implicatum is a disjunction of them. If the list is open, the implicatum has the indeterminacy that many actual implicata seem to have (p. 58).

*Intuition note.* Grice derives these features from what a conversational implicature is (p. 57). Cancellability follows from the possibility of opting out of the CP; nondetachability from the calculation resting on what is said rather than the wording; non-meaning from the calculation presupposing conventional force; falsity-compatibility from the implicature coming from the saying; indeterminacy from a possibly open list of explanations.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Ana: *Did you like the film?* Ben: *The popcorn was good.*
- Said: the popcorn was good (conventional meaning of the words).
- Ana's calculation, following the pattern on p. 50: (1) Ben said $p$ = *the popcorn was good*. (2) This does not answer her question, so it seems to break Relation. (3) She has no sign that Ben opts out. (4) Ben could be cooperative only if he thinks $q$ = *I did not like the film much*. (5) Ben knows she can see this, and he lets her think $q$. So Ben implicates $q$.
- Cancellation test (our extension; Grice states cancellability for generalized implicatures, p. 57): if Ben adds *Sorry, I can't say more about the film; I promised not to discuss it*, he has opted out, so Ana no longer infers $q$.
- Truth test: the popcorn may be good while Ben actually loved the film, so $q$ may be false although $p$ is true (feature 4, p. 58).

## Concepts
- [[conversational implicature]] — what a speaker conveys beyond what he says, derived by the hearer from the CP and maxims by a calculation (pp. 49–50)
- [[cooperative principle & maxims]] — make your contribution as the exchange's purpose requires; maxims of Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner (pp. 45–46)
- [[conventional implicature]] (new) — an implicature fixed by the conventional meaning of the words, e.g. *therefore* (pp. 44–45)
- [[what is said]] (new) — content closely tied to the conventional meaning of the uttered sentence, plus references and the like (p. 44)
- [[maxim flouting / exploitation]] (new) — blatantly failing to fulfil a maxim; when this gives rise to an implicature, the maxim is said to be exploited (p. 49)
- [[particularized vs generalized implicature]] (new) — by special context vs normally carried by a form of words (p. 56)
- [[cancellability]] (new) — a generalized conversational implicature can be cancelled, explicitly or contextually, by opting out of the CP (p. 57)
- [[nondetachability]] (new) — insofar as the calculation needs only what is said and manner plays no role, no other way of saying the same thing lacks the implicature (pp. 57–58)
- [[formalist vs informalist]] (new) — rival camps on the divergence of logical and natural meaning (pp. 41–43)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Clause (3) of the definition (nested "he thinks that I think…") ↔ common knowledge: both rely on mutually known facts about what each party knows (pp. 49–50).
- [structural-analogy] Hearer's calculation of an implicature ↔ inference to the best explanation: finding what must be supposed to keep the speaker's act rational (p. 58).
- [structural-analogy] Maxims as expectations about a partner ↔ conventions that solve coordination problems: Grice offers analogies from cooperative non-talk tasks (p. 47).

## Questions this raises
- If implicatures are calculated from the CP, when does a generalized implicature become conventionalized (p. 58)?
- Can Grice's argument that following the maxims is reasonable be completed, given the unresolved problems of relevance (p. 49)?
- Can the account of implicature really show that the supposed divergences for *and*, *or*, *if* disappear? These pages state this aim (p. 43) but do not work out those cases.
- How should maxims be generalized when talk aims at influencing action, not information exchange (p. 47)?
