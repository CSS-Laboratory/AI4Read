# Osborne, M. J. (2004). *An Introduction to Game Theory*. Oxford University Press.

## In one paragraph
The book teaches game theory as a set of models of interacting decision-makers. It starts from rational choice by one person. It then builds the Nash equilibrium: a steady state in which no player gains by changing her own action alone. The idea is extended step by step. Players may randomize. They may move in sequence, where subgame perfect equilibrium removes empty threats. They may lack information, as in Bayesian games. They may be genes under selection, or players of a repeated game. Each idea is tested on worked examples, such as the Prisoner's Dilemma, Cournot and Bertrand competition, auctions, Hawk–Dove and voting. Other chapters treat coalitions (the core), cautious play (maxminimization) and rationalizability. The point is to see which outcomes are stable, which are efficient, and when cooperation can last.

## The path through the work
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch01-introduction]] — Game theory builds precise models of interacting decision-makers, and many of them rest on rational choice: each person picks an available action that is at least as good, by her own consistent preferences, as every other available action.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch02-nash-p1]] — A Nash equilibrium is an action profile in which no single player can gain by changing only her own action, and it models a steady state among experienced players who hold correct beliefs about each other.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch02-nash-p2]] — A Nash equilibrium is exactly an action profile in which every player's action is a best response to the others' actions, and this lets us find equilibria with best-response functions and discard strictly dominated actions.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch03-nash-p1]] — Finding each player's best response function and intersecting them is the main way the author solves, in specific examples, three classic models: quantity competition (Cournot), price competition (Bertrand) and two-candidate electoral competition (Hotelling).
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch03-nash-p2]] — In games where each action is a number and payoffs jump when one action passes another, Nash equilibria can be many, so the author uses weak dominance to single out some of them, and equilibrium analysis is used to compare auction forms and to find a legal rule whose unique equilibrium is socially best.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch04-mixed-p1]] — When players may randomize, a stable pattern of play (a mixed strategy Nash equilibrium) exists in every finite game, and in it each player is indifferent among all actions she uses with positive probability.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch04-mixed-p2]] — In a mixed strategy equilibrium each player is indifferent among the actions she uses, and the author uses this characterization to derive the "larger group, less help" result, to search for all equilibria, and to restate the equilibrium test for continuum games; mixed equilibria also model stochastic steady states.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch05-extensive]] — In a sequential game where everyone sees all earlier moves, the equilibrium notion that models a robust steady state is subgame perfect equilibrium, which demands optimal play after every history and, in finite-horizon games, is found by backward induction.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch06-extensive]] — Solving sequential games from the end backwards (subgame perfect equilibrium) gives sharp predictions in the ultimatum, holdup, Stackelberg, vote-buying and race games, and the results illustrate how moving first, moving second and committing matter.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch07-extensive]] — Subgame perfect equilibrium and backward induction extend to simultaneous moves and chance moves, and the chapter applies them to firms, voters and committees, then questions whether backward-induction logic is convincing in the chain-store and centipede games.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch08-coalitional]] — A coalitional game gives actions to groups, and the core is the set of grand-coalition actions that no group can break away from and improve upon; Osborne applies it to markets, house exchange, voting and matching; it gives a very precise prediction in the horse and house markets but can be empty, as in majority voting.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch09-bayesian]] — A Bayesian game models imperfect information with states, signals and beliefs; each signal defines a "type", and an equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium of the ordinary game whose players are all the types.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch11-strictly]] — In a two-player game where the players' interests are exactly opposed, playing the cautious maxminimizing strategy is the same thing as playing a Nash equilibrium strategy (when an equilibrium exists).
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch12-rationalizability]] — An action is rationalizable if it survives unlimited rounds of "my opponent is rational, and knows that I am", which for finite games is the same as surviving iterated elimination of strictly dominated actions.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch13-evolutionary]] — An action or mixed strategy is evolutionarily stable (an ESS) when it is a Nash equilibrium and also beats every rival best response in encounters between mutants, so that rare mutants die out.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch14-repeated]] — If players are patient enough, the threat of future punishment can make cooperation an equilibrium of the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, and for patient players every feasible payoff pair that gives both more than 1 can be approximately sustained, even with subgame perfection.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch15-repeated]] — In any infinitely repeated game, every feasible payoff profile that gives each player more than her minmax payoff can be approximated by a Nash equilibrium when players are patient, and no Nash equilibrium gives a player less than her minmax payoff.
- [[osborne-2004-introduction/ch17-mathematics]] — The appendix collects the basic mathematical tools of the book (sums, sets, functions, profiles, geometric sums, probability with expected value and Bayes' rule, and the vocabulary of proofs) in informal form.

## Big ideas across units
- **Nash equilibrium as a steady state with correct beliefs.** Rational choice (ch01) is combined with correct beliefs. The same idea is reinterpreted as a stochastic steady state, a polymorphic population and a perturbed steady state. Units: ch01-introduction, ch02-nash-p1, ch02-nash-p2, ch04-mixed-p1, ch04-mixed-p2, ch05-extensive, ch13-evolutionary.
- **Best responses as the working tool.** Equilibria are found by intersecting best-response functions in tables, diagrams and calculus. Units: ch02-nash-p2, ch03-nash-p1, ch03-nash-p2, ch04-mixed-p1, ch12-rationalizability.
- **Dominance narrows the predictions.** Strictly dominated actions are never used. Weak dominance selects among many equilibria (second-price bids, votes). Iterated elimination equals rationalizability. Units: ch02-nash-p2, ch03-nash-p2, ch04-mixed-p1, ch04-mixed-p2, ch07-extensive, ch12-rationalizability.
- **Indifference and randomization.** In a mixed equilibrium each player is indifferent among the actions she uses. This yields the crime-reporting result, mixed ESSs and maxminimization in strictly competitive games. Units: ch04-mixed-p1, ch04-mixed-p2, ch11-strictly, ch13-evolutionary.
- **Credibility and backward induction.** Subgame perfection removes non-credible threats. It gives sharp predictions in sequential games, is questioned in the chain-store and centipede games, and underlies the repeated-game punishment analysis. Units: ch05-extensive, ch06-extensive, ch07-extensive, ch14-repeated, ch15-repeated.
- **Equilibria that are bad for the players, and the cooperation problem.** Examples are the Prisoner's Dilemma, Cournot output, common-property overuse, public goods, holdup, and the cutoff in the public-good game. Repeated play can restore cooperation. Units: ch02-nash-p1, ch02-nash-p2, ch03-nash-p1, ch06-extensive, ch09-bayesian, ch14-repeated, ch15-repeated.
- **Auctions across information settings.** Second-price truthful bidding, first-price equilibria and revenue equivalence are studied under perfect information, with a continuum of actions, and with private or common values (winner's curse). Units: ch03-nash-p2, ch04-mixed-p2, ch09-bayesian.
- **Beliefs, information and testing theory against behaviour.** What players know and believe drives the models: types, rationalizability, the limits of backward induction. Experiments often disagree with the theory (ultimatum, centipede, O'Neill). Units: ch02-nash-p1, ch04-mixed-p1, ch06-extensive, ch07-extensive, ch09-bayesian, ch11-strictly, ch12-rationalizability, ch15-repeated.

## How to read it
- **Essential (the catalog's focus chapters):** ch01, ch02 (both parts), ch03 (both parts), ch04 (both parts), ch05, ch09, ch13, ch14. They run in book order: ch02 builds on ch01, and ch03 and ch04 build on ch02. The first part of ch04 comes before the second.
- **Dependencies:**
  - ch05 needs Nash equilibrium and best responses (ch02).
  - ch06 and ch07 need ch05.
  - ch09 needs ch02 and ch04 (mixed strategies appear only in some exercises).
  - ch13 needs ch02 and the mixed strategies of ch04.
  - ch14 needs Nash equilibrium and subgame perfection (ch05).
  - ch15 needs ch14.
  - ch11 and ch12 both need Nash equilibrium and mixed strategies (ch04).
- **Can be skipped on a first pass:** ch08 uses only ch01 ideas (Nash equilibrium for contrast), so it stands apart. ch11 and ch12 are side topics. ch06 and ch07 extend ch05 with applications and discussion. ch15 generalizes ch14. The second half of ch04-mixed-p2 (support enumeration, continuum games, Bernoulli appendix) and the auction extensions in ch03-nash-p2 are optional on first reading.
- **Reference:** ch17 is a mathematics appendix. Look things up there as needed, especially probability and Bayes' rule for ch04 and ch09, and geometric sums for ch14.
