# probability-as-extended-logic

## Canon spine entry

- **Probability as extended logic** (bayesian): probability as the unique consistent calculus of plausibility, generalizing true/false to degrees (Cox, Jaynes) (UG)

## Definitions in the sources

### information-theory

- [[jaynes-2003-probability/appa-other]] (p. 652): Jaynes's foundation: plausibilities obey rules forced by consistency, with AND and NOT as primitives
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch01-plausible]] (pp. 12, 19): plausible reasoning as an extension of deductive logic by weak syllogisms; Jaynes states that the rules follow from the desiderata
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch02-quantitative]] (pp. 24, 35): plausibilities as real numbers obeying the product and sum rules, deduced from desiderata; deductive logic is the limit of certainty
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch03-elementary]] (p. 52): probability describes the robot's state of knowledge given the stated information, not physical properties of the urn
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch04-elementary]] (pp. 86, 98, 117): conclusions come from $P(A|\text{all evidence})$; Cox's theorems forbid inconsistency
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch05-queer]] (p. 123): Jaynes's view: the rules give consistent plausible reasoning from the information used, no more
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch07-central-p1]] (pp. 207, 217): probabilities represent information; unknown circumstances must not enter inferences
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch09-repetitive-p2]] (p. 293): the view Jaynes defends against Fisher, Neyman and others
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch10-physics]] (p. 325): the Laplace–Jeffreys–Cox view; validity is a matter of logic, not physical assumptions
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch15-paradoxes]] (p. 486): rules derived by Cox's functional equations, for finite sets [as `Probability as extended logic`]
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch16-orthodox]] (p. 493): Laplace–Jeffreys view that orthodox critics attacked
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch17-principles-p2]] (pp. 533, 536, 552): Jaynes's approach of applying probability theory directly as logic
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/ch22-introduction]] (pp. 635, 650): probabilities describe a state of knowledge; they need not be known physical frequencies, and are the best estimates of frequencies available from the information held
- [[jaynes-2003-probability/front-preface]] (pdf 21, 23): probability rules as the unique consistent calculus of plausible reasoning, not only of frequencies
